Microprocessors—entire CPUs on a single integrated circuit chip—emerged in the early 1970s and made programmable computing affordable for individuals and small organizations.

The Intel 4004 (1971):
- First commercially available microprocessor
- 2,300 transistors (compared to millions in modern processors)
- 10 micrometers transistor size (compared to 5nm today)
- Could execute ~100,000 operations per second
- Cost ~$200 (equivalent to ~$1,500 today)

Evolution of microprocessors:
- 1971: Intel 4004 (4-bit, 2.3K transistors)
- 1974: Intel 8080 (8-bit, 6K transistors)
- 1978: Intel 8086 (16-bit, 29K transistors) - foundation for x86 architecture
- 1985: Intel 386 (32-bit, 275K transistors) - enabled PC revolution
- 1993: Intel Pentium (32-bit, 3.1M transistors)
- 2003: Intel Pentium 4 (32-bit, 42M transistors) - end of simple CPU evolution
- 2024: Intel Core i9 (64-bit, 50+ billion transistors) - multiple cores, caches

Microprocessor economics:
- Manufacturing cost dropped exponentially due to Moore's Law
- Enabled personal computers: Altair 8800 (1975), Apple II (1977), IBM PC (1981)
- Made computing accessible beyond academic institutions and large corporations

Key enabler for the personal computer revolution:
- 1975: Altair 8800 used Intel 8080, could be assembled for $395 by hobbyists
- 1977: Apple II and Commodore PET brought microcomputers to homes
- 1981: IBM PC validated the personal computer market for business use

Microprocessor specialization (1990s onward):
- CPUs optimized for different tasks (mobile, server, gaming, cryptocurrency mining)
- GPU evolution: graphics processors became programmable and useful for parallel computation
- Specialized processors: TPUs (tensor processing units) for AI, FPGAs for custom tasks

The phrase "microprocessor" is somewhat outdated terminology—modern processors are termed simply CPUs or processors. "Microprocessor" historically meant CPU-on-a-chip as a distinction from room-sized computers, but that distinction is no longer meaningful.
