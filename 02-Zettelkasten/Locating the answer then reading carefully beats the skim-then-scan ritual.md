The classroom ritual — skim the whole passage, then scan for keywords, then read — is not supported by the strongest teacher sources or by what the research actually measures.

The objection is that neither operation produces an answer. <mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">Skimming gives general meaning and scanning gives a location; the mark comes from understanding the located sentence.</mark> One ex-examiner stopped using the words in lessons entirely, narrowing legitimate scanning to targets with no synonyms — a city name, a number, a surname — and otherwise reading at normal speed.

The research does not say fast reading is useless, though. Bax's eye-tracking study of candidates on onscreen IELTS reading found successful and unsuccessful candidates differed significantly in their ability to read *expeditiously*. <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">So the evidenced skill is locate fast, then read carefully — not read fast throughout.</mark>

On timing, the test owner suggests no more than twenty minutes per passage and banking time on passage one for passage three. The alternative view keeps the question-first approach but sets no fixed split, abandoning any question after about two minutes and returning later. <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">"Read the questions first" is supported; "read all the questions first" is not.</mark>

Reading has no transfer time in either format — answers go straight onto the sheet inside the hour. And there is no penalty for a wrong answer, so every blank gets a guess before time runs out.
