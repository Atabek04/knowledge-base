### What it is

Ausubel's axiom: *"The most important single factor influencing learning is what the learner already knows."*

<mark style="background: yellow">Advance organizers</mark> are introductory materials presented *before* new content — at a higher level of abstraction — that create cognitive "hooks" for incoming information.

Without them, new material arrives with nothing to attach to. It is either forgotten or stored as isolated rote facts.

---

### The subsumption mechanism

New information is learned by being subsumed into existing cognitive structures.

Advance organizers create or activate a relevant structure *before* it is needed. Three subsumption modes:

- **Derivative subsumption** — new detail extends an existing concept without changing it
- **Correlative subsumption** — new info modifies and enriches an existing concept
- **Obliterative subsumption** — details fade over time; only the abstracted principle is retained long-term

---

### The two types

<mark style="background: yellow">**Expository organizer**</mark> — used when material is genuinely new.
Provides a conceptual framework from scratch.
*Example:* Before teaching TCP/IP, give a 2-minute overview of "how two computers agree to talk." Learners get a skeleton to hang specifics on.

<mark style="background: yellow">**Comparative organizer**</mark> — used when material resembles something already known, and confusion is likely.
Explicitly maps similarities *and* differences to prior knowledge.
*Example:* Before teaching stacks: "This is like an array, except you can only access one end."

Preventing false transfer is as important as activating helpful transfer.

---

### Progressive differentiation

Ausubel's sequencing rule: present the most <mark style="background: cyan">general, abstract concepts first</mark>, then progressively differentiate into specifics.

This is the opposite of induction (specific → general).

The conceptual umbrella comes first. Details attach underneath.

---

### Application to instruction design

- Open every new topic with a 1–2 paragraph organizer *before* teaching details
- Use expository when starting a genuinely new domain
- Use comparative when introducing a variation on a known concept
- Never assume the learner will construct the framework themselves mid-lesson
- Directly prevents the filter spike described in [[High affective filter blocks language acquisition even when input is comprehensible|Krashen's affective filter]]
