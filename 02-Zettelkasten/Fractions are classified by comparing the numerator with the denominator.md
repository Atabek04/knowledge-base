English names four kinds of fraction, and the names sound like judgements — "proper", "improper" — as though one form were correct and the other a mistake. They are not. Every one of the four is a legitimate way to write a number.

The classification turns on a single comparison, which is why the whole vocabulary collapses into one question: <b>which of the two numbers is bigger?</b>

This note assumes the two numbers already have names — see [[The numerator counts the parts taken and the denominator names the size of each part|numerator and denominator]].

---

### The four types

| Type | Rule | Example | Value |
|---|---|---|---|
| <b>Proper</b> | numerator < denominator | `3/4` | less than 1 |
| <b>Improper</b> | numerator ≥ denominator | `7/4` | 1 or more |
| <b>Mixed number</b> | a whole number written <i>before</i> a proper fraction | `1¾` | 1 or more |
| <b>Unit fraction</b> | numerator is exactly 1 | `1/5` | one single part |

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Proper and improper are the real axis — they partition every fraction between them.</mark> Mixed and unit are notation and special case respectively, not a third and fourth category on the same level.

#### Mixed number — the whole number in front

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">A mixed number is a whole number written before a proper fraction</mark> — `1¾`, a whole number "mixed" with a fraction. It is read aloud as "one and three quarters", with an audible <i>and</i> where the numbers meet.

It denotes the same value as the improper `7/4`. The two forms are interchangeable, and each is preferred in a different setting:

- <b>Mixed</b> reads better to a human — "one and three quarters" is immediately a size.
- <b>Improper</b> calculates better — you cannot multiply or divide `1¾` without first converting it back to `7/4`.

<mark style="background: #FF9E9EA6;">"Improper" is a warning about readability, not about correctness.</mark> Algebra runs on improper fractions almost exclusively.

#### Unit fraction

A fraction whose numerator is 1, such as `1/5`. The name is literal: it is one single unit of the denomination the denominator names.

Unit fractions are the building blocks — `3/5` is just three copies of `1/5`.
