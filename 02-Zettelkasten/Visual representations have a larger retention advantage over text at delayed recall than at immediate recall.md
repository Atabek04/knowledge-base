### The core finding

<mark style="background: yellow">The picture superiority effect</mark>: images are recalled and recognized better than words under most conditions — but the advantage is **small at immediate recall and large at delay**.

A controlled study (PMC7618346, N = 92) tested graphics vs. text vs. tables for statistical data across two time points:

- **Immediate recall:** Graphics error rate 9.4% vs. text 14.7% — difference non-significant (p = 0.43)
- **2-hour delay:** Graphics error rate 6% vs. text 27% — large significant effect (partial η² = **0.26**)

The visual advantage isn't just a minor boost — it grows with time while the text-based advantage fades.

---

### Why the advantage grows at delay

Text memory relies primarily on a single verbal code. That code decays following Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve — rapidly in the first hours, then more slowly.

Images, when stored via [[Dual coding theory shows that pairing images with verbal explanation creates stronger memory traces|dual coding]], create both a verbal label and a visual trace. Even if the verbal trace fades, the visual trace remains independently retrievable. Two degrading traces together remain more accessible than one degrading trace alone.

Standing (1973) demonstrated the power of visual memory: participants shown up to 10,000 photographs at 5 seconds each achieved **83% recognition accuracy** — visual recognition memory appears to have a nearly unlimited ceiling under normal encoding conditions.

---

### The concreteness constraint

Nelson, Reed & Walling (1976) added a critical nuance: the picture superiority effect depends on **sensory distinctiveness**, not mere image format.

When pictures were made highly schematically similar to each other (e.g., 50 photos of different chairs), the effect was **eliminated or reversed** at fast presentation rates. Abstract icons with low visual distinctiveness offer little more than words.

<mark style="background: pink">The visual advantage comes from rich, concrete, distinctive representations — not from any image shape.</mark>

A diagram of the actual nodes in a distributed system with named regions is more memorable than a generic "cloud" icon, because the sensory specificity of the former creates a richer visual trace.

---

### The fabricated statistics to avoid

The widely-cited numbers — "we remember 10% of what we read, 65% of what we see" — are **not from any peer-reviewed study**.

They trace to a 1967 article by D.G. Treichler in a trade publication (*Film and Audio-Visual Communications*), which provided no citations, no methodology, and no data. Edgar Dale's Cone of Experience (1946) contained no percentage claims — those were added by others decades later without any empirical basis.

Use actual effect sizes: **partial η² = 0.26** for visual vs. text retention at delay (PMC7618346); **d = 0.63–0.72** for integrated text+diagram vs. separated (Ginns 2006 meta-analysis; Schneider et al. 2018 meta-analysis).
