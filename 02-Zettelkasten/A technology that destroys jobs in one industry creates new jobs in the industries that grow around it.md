When a technology wipes out an occupation, the lost jobs are visible at once, while the jobs it creates appear later, elsewhere, and under names nobody had heard of. Historical cases show how that second half happens.

### Glossary

- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Creative destruction</b>: the process by which new technologies and firms destroy old industries while creating new ones.</mark> The economist Joseph Schumpeter named it in 1942: the *destruction* is itself *creative*.
- **Complementary industry**: an industry that grows because another product exists and needs it, as petrol stations need cars.
- **New work**: job specialties that did not exist before, such as a webmaster or a solar panel installer.

### Cars in the 1920s

As cars displaced horse travel, blacksmiths and stable hands lost their work. Around the car grew new industries to serve "the motoring public": roadside motels and fast-food restaurants (Autor, ["Why Are There Still So Many Jobs?"](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Why%20Are%20there%20Still%20So%20Many%20Jobs_0.pdf), JEP 2015, citing Jackson 1993).

The chain has three links:

1. <b>The new product</b> needs makers: car factories.
2. <b>The product creates needs of its own</b>: fuel, repair, roads, insurance.
3. <b>The product changes how people live</b>: people travel further, so they need places to sleep and eat on the road.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">The largest job gains from a technology usually come from the needs it creates and the way it changes life, not from making the technology itself.</mark>

### Cheaper food across the 20th century

Farm machinery cut agriculture from 41% to 2% of US jobs between 1900 and 2000. Because food got cheaper, households spent a smaller share on it (42.5% in 1901, 13.1% in 2002–03, BLS), and the income freed up paid for manufactured goods, housing, health care and education, each with its own workforce. That spending pattern is [[As incomes rise the share spent on food falls, so spending shifts toward housing, services and new goods|Engel's law]].

---

### Most of today's jobs are new work

Autor, Chin, Salomons and Seegmiller traced US job titles from 1940 to 2018 and found that the majority of 2018 employment was in job specialties introduced after 1940. New work shifted from middle-paid production and clerical jobs (1940–1980) to high-paid professional and low-paid service jobs (since 1980) (["New Frontiers", NBER w30389, 2022](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389)).

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">The jobs a technology destroys are easy to count in advance; the jobs it creates mostly cannot be named in advance, so forecasts tend to overstate net job loss.</mark> The reverse risk is also real: the new jobs may pay differently and suit different people from the ones destroyed.
