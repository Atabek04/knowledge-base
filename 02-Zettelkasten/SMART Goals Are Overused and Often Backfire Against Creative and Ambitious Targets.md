SMART goals — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound — became the dominant framework for goal setting in corporate and personal productivity contexts. The problem is that the research backing for SMART as a superior method is weak, and several of its criteria actively work against the goals that matter most.

### What the Research Actually Shows

A large-scale study on New Year's resolutions found that participants taught SMART goals reported *less* success than those using alternative approaches.

A 2024 study in educational psychology found SMART goals are **no more effective for creative performance** than "do your best" goals or open-ended exploratory goals.

A systematic review of goal-setting in physical activity found no consistent benefit from specificity compared with vaguer goal forms. The specificity advantage, central to SMART, is not reliably replicated.

### The Specific Problems

**"Achievable" caps ambition.** The emphasis on realistic, attainable goals creates an artificial ceiling. Research on challenging goals consistently shows that harder goals, when genuinely committed to, produce better outcomes than easy ones.

**Specificity causes tunnel vision.** Highly specific goals focus attention so narrowly that people neglect unmeasured aspects of performance. Employees hit their metric while the underlying purpose deteriorates.

**SMART is inconsistently defined.** A review of physical activity interventions found 34 different terms used to represent the five SMART criteria. A rehabilitation review found 55. The framework is not a precise tool — it is a loose mnemonic applied differently by everyone who uses it.

**It ignores the "why."** SMART goals are output definitions. They describe what success looks like but say nothing about why it matters. Without meaning, execution decays when difficulty arrives. This is why [[Identity-Based Habits Outlast Outcome-Based Goals]]: identity anchors persistence; metrics do not.

### What Works Better

Research supports several alternatives or supplements to SMART:

- **Challenging goals** over achievable ones, when the person has the skills
- **Learning goals** over performance goals, when the task is novel
- **Implementation intentions** (if-then plans) paired with any goal type — this is the WOOP approach
- **Process goals** over outcome goals for creative or skill-based work

The most effective goal-setting combines a clear direction (the "why"), a challenging target, and a concrete plan for handling the obstacles.
