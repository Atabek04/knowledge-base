Every mathematics textbook in English names the two halves of a fraction constantly and explains them once, early, in a chapter most readers skip. Anyone who learned mathematics in another language meets `3/4` fluently but hits the words "numerator" and "denominator" cold.

Both names are descriptive, so neither has to be memorised as a bare label.

---

### The two numbers and the line

In `3/4`, written stacked:

- <b>numerator</b> — the <b>top</b> number, `3`. It <i>numbers</i> how many parts you take.
- <b>denominator</b> — the <b>bottom</b> number, `4`. It <i>denominates</i>: it names what kind of part you are counting — quarters, fifths, hundredths.

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">The numerator counts how many parts you take; the denominator names how large each part is.</mark>
- <b>fraction bar</b> (formally the <i>vinculum</i>) — the line between them, which means division. `3/4` and `3 ÷ 4` are the same instruction.

#### Name as mnemonic

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Denominator — <b>d</b>enominator is <b>d</b>own.</mark> The one that shares the letter is the one on the bottom.

The deeper hook is "denomination", the same word used for currency. A 20-note and a 5-note are different denominations — different <i>kinds</i> of unit. The denominator does exactly that job: `1/4` and `1/5` are different denominations of a whole, and the numerator then counts how many of them you hold.

This is also why `1/4 + 1/5` cannot be added as written — that is adding two different units, like adding a length in metres to one in feet.

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Fractions must be converted to a common denominator before they can be added or subtracted.</mark>

---

### Reciprocal

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">The reciprocal of a fraction is that fraction flipped — numerator and denominator swap places.</mark> The reciprocal of `3/4` is `4/3`.

A number multiplied by its reciprocal always gives `1`, which is precisely why dividing by a fraction is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal.
