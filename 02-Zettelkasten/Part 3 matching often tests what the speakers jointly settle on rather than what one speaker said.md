Part 3 of the IELTS Listening test is a discussion — typically two main speakers, sometimes with a tutor. The obvious way to prepare for it is to track who thinks what, and IDP names losing track of speakers as one of its seven common mistakes.

That preparation is right about the hazard and wrong about which one dominates.

### The two different Part 3 failures

**Attribution** — you caught the opinion and assigned it to the wrong person. Real, and the one everybody prepares for.

**Agreement** — you caught an opinion that was never the answer, because the question was asking what the speakers *concluded together*.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">A large share of Part 3 matching sets test the negotiated outcome, not any individual's view.</mark> My IELTS Classroom cites Cambridge 15 Test 2 Part 3 as a set where every answer turns on agreement.

If you are diligently maintaining two columns of opinions, one per speaker, you will answer such a question from the wrong data structure entirely. The tidier your notes, the more confidently you will be wrong.

#### Why the first opinion is usually the distractor

A discussion has a shape: someone floats a position, someone else challenges it, and they settle somewhere. <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">"First mention wins" is the single most expensive habit in Part 3, because the structure of a discussion guarantees the first mention is provisional.</mark>

This is [[Listening distractors work by stating an answer and then withdrawing it|the distractor mechanism]] again, distributed across two speakers instead of one — which makes it harder to notice, because no single person visibly corrects themselves.

### What to do instead

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Write down the words you are sure carry the answer while the audio runs, and do the matching to the option box after the recording stops.</mark>

This is deliberately low-tech, and that is the point. Matching during the audio means reading the option list during the audio, which is the attention cost measured in [[Keyword training backfires when attention shifts from the audio to the question paper|Field's protocols]]. Capturing raw words costs a glance; matching costs a paragraph.

#### The one place to spend time on the options anyway

Part 3 is the exception to reading [[Listening preview time helps mainly through the question stems while the answer options add little|stems before options]]. Here the options *are* the competing positions — the question is which one the discussion lands on, so an unread option list is an unread question.

Part 3 options are also unusually long and finely differentiated, which is what makes this part load so heavily on reading. Field found multiple-choice items "load heavily on to the reading skill because of their complexity"; IELTS Advantage says plainly that this is not only a test of listening.

#### The speaker grid is folklore

The widely circulated tactic of drawing a speakers × topics grid, or jotting initials beside each option, <mark style="background: #FF9E9EA6;">appears in no official or first-tier expert source and has never been validated.</mark>

It is plausible. It is also expensive in exactly the currency Field showed you cannot afford — attention on paper rather than on the audio — and it optimises for attribution, which is the lesser of the two failures.
