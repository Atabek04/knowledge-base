A widely-cited 2025 study links heavy AI use to weaker critical thinking. The finding is real — but *how* you read it matters more than the number, because the causation is not settled.

This sharpens the general claim that [[Using AI to bypass work causes skill atrophy while using it for inquiry builds skill|offloading thinking atrophies skill]] by showing where the evidence is strong and where it is only suggestive.

---

### The finding

Gerlich (Societies, 2025; n = 666, peer-reviewed) measured critical thinking with the validated Halpern assessment and found a <mark style="background: yellow">strong negative correlation (r = −0.68)</mark> between frequency of AI-tool use and critical-thinking score.

So the association is large and the instrument is sound.

---

### Why the arrow may run both ways

<mark style="background: pink">This is correlation, not established causation.</mark> The author himself flags the alternative reading:

> it is possible that individuals with weaker critical thinking skills are simply more inclined to rely on AI tools

#### Two readings, same data

- <mark style="background: green">AI → weaker thinking</mark> (offloading erodes the muscle), or
- <mark style="background: green">weaker thinking → more AI</mark> (people who reason less reach for the tool more).

The study cannot distinguish them. <mark style="background: cyan;">A strong correlation is a reason to investigate, not a proven mechanism.</mark>

---

### What not to carry forward

Two flashier sub-claims from the same paper failed independent verification and should *not* be cited: the cognitive-offloading "mediation" figures, and the claim that the youngest cohort is worst-affected. Keep only the headline correlation, with the causation caveat attached.
