Writing `park` where the answer key says `parks` loses the whole mark. The content was heard, the word was right, and the score is identical to having heard nothing at all.

This feels unfair, and it is worth understanding why it is not arbitrary — and why the question paper usually tells you the answer before the recording does.

### Why a wrong plural is simply wrong

Listening is clerically marked against a finite answer key. If `factors` is on the key and `factor` is not, `factor` is marked wrong. <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">There is no examiner judgement in the loop to appeal to and no partial credit to award.</mark>

Worth flagging honestly: this rule is universally asserted by every preparation source and is **not** stated on any official IELTS page. The mechanism above is what makes it credible, not a published policy.

#### You cannot hedge

`car(s)` is not an answer. Writing both forms does not cover both cases — it produces a string that is on nobody's key.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">The hedge feels like insurance and is actually a guaranteed zero, which makes it worse than committing to the wrong one.</mark>

### The free check nobody runs

The gapped sentence is printed in front of you during the preview, and English grammar leaks the number.

- "the ______ **are** stored in" — the verb forces a plural
- "**one** ______" — forces a singular
- "**some** forests" — must be plural, because *some forest* is ungrammatical

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Decide singular or plural from the stem during the preview, before the audio starts, and treat the recording as confirmation rather than as the source.</mark>

This is the highest-value case of [[Predict the answer slot rather than the answer word because a predicted word gets confirmed instead of heard|predicting the slot instead of the word]] — the number is fully determined by text you can already see, so predicting it commits you to nothing you might mishear.

#### Why you cannot rely on hearing it

Connected speech eats the plural `/s/`, especially before a consonant. Candidates routinely report writing *movement* for *movements* and hearing no difference on replay.

The determiner and the verb survive where the `/s/` does not. They are the evidence.

### The cases that still catch people

- **Compound nouns stay unmarked** — *student support*, not *students' support*. The first noun modifies and does not pluralise.
- **Some words have no singular** — *grassroots*.
- **A hyphenated compound counts as one word** against the word limit, and the hyphen is part of the spelling — see [[An over-limit Listening answer scores zero even when the content heard was correct|how the limit is counted]].

<mark style="background: #FF9E9EA6;">Not every plural error is a plural error — a candidate who wrote "groceries" for "grassroots" made a recognition error that the log would misfile as a grammar problem.</mark> The distinction matters because the two want different remedies: one wants the stem-grammar check above, the other wants [[Dictation with immediate comparison is the spelling remediation that matches the Listening task|dictation practice]].
