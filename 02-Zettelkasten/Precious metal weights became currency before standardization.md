Before coins were stamped by authorities, societies used weighed amounts of precious metals as money.

Gold, silver, and copper were valuable universally, so a measure of gold or silver was universally acceptable.

**Ancient Egypt example**: The "deben" measured weight of precious metals. A trader would carry a scale and verify the exact weight before accepting payment—no official stamp needed yet.

## Quranic Evidence

The Quran references this practice in **Surah Yusuf (12:20)**, describing Joseph's brothers selling him to merchants:

Arabic: وَشَرَوْهُ بِثَمَنٍۭ بَخْسٍۢ دَرَٰهِمَ مَعْدُودَةٍۢ

Translation: *"They sold him for a cheap price, just a few counted dirhams (silver coins)—only wanting to get rid of him."*

This shows that **dirhams** (measured silver weights) were already in use as currency in ancient times. The word "ma'duda" (معدودة) emphasizes they were **counted and weighed**—numbered for payment without official standardization.

## The Problem

Without standardization, each region used different weight systems and purity standards.

A merchant accepting gold had to:
- Verify the weight with their own scales
- Test purity (was it pure gold or mixed with copper?)
- Trust the seller wasn't cheating

Disputes were constant. Standardization solved this.
