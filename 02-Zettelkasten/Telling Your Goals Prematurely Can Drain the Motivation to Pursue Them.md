When you announce a goal to others, the act of being *seen* as the kind of person who pursues that goal can satisfy the underlying drive — before any real work has happened.

Peter Gollwitzer's 2009 study "When Intentions Go Public" found that participants who shared identity-related goals with others worked on them *less* than those who kept the goals private. The recognition itself acted as a partial reward.

## The Core Mechanism

The brain confuses **being acknowledged as X** with **being X**.

- You say "I'm going to run a marathon"
- Someone responds with admiration or encouragement
- Your identity-need is partly fed by their reaction
- The drive to actually train weakens

This is the same pattern as [[Planning feels like doing because the brain rewards anticipation with dopamine]] — the reward arrives before the work.

## Important Caveats

The effect is **not universal**. It applies most strongly to:

- **Identity goals** — goals where the telling itself is part of the reward ("I'm a writer", "I'm a runner")
- **Vague announcements** — broad statements that invite social validation
- **Strong commitment to the identity**, not weak commitment

The original study was small (~63 participants), pre-replication-crisis, and shows signs of p-hacking. Some replications failed. Derek Sivers, who popularized it in a TED talk, later issued a semi-retraction clarifying the scope.

## What Actually Holds Up

| Sharing style | Likely effect |
|---|---|
| Bragging to impress, vague identity claims | ↓ motivation |
| Sharing with an accountability partner who checks on you | ↑ motivation |
| Sharing process or system (not outcome) | neutral/↑ |
| Identity claims before doing the work | ↓ motivation |

The fix is not silence — it's choosing *who* you tell and *what* you tell them. Tell people who will track your execution. Share systems, not identities you haven't earned yet.
