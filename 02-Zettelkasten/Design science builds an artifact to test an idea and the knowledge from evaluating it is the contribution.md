Most research in the sciences observes something that already exists. Software and information-systems research often cannot, because the thing to be studied has to be built first. *Design science* is the research type for that case: *science* done by *designing* an *artifact* (a system, an algorithm, a method) and then evaluating whether it does what the underlying idea predicts (Hevner, March, Park & Ram, 2004, *MIS Quarterly*; Wieringa, 2014, *Design Science Methodology*).

> [!definition] Design science
> Research that builds an artifact in order to test an idea, and whose contribution is the knowledge produced by evaluating that artifact, never the artifact itself.

---

### Artifact as treatment, evaluation as contribution

Wieringa's word for the artifact is **treatment**, borrowed from medicine: the built thing is what you *treat* the problem with, the way a drug treats a disease. <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">The artifact is the treatment and the knowledge from evaluating it is the contribution; the drug is not the finding, the trial result is.</mark>

That is why a design-science report and a project report can describe the same codebase and get opposite grades. The project report stops at "we built it"; the design-science report asks "did the idea behind it hold when tested".

Hevner et al. give seven guidelines for the genre; the first two carry the whole idea: produce a viable artifact, and make it address a relevant problem. The third, rigorous evaluation, is the one that separates research from building.

---

### The bachelor trap

The common failure is design science without the science: <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">an artifact is built, the evaluation is skipped, and the report has no claim a reader could check.</mark> That is acceptable for a bachelor project, which grades execution, and fails a master's dissertation, which grades contribution.

The fix is not to build less; it is to decide *before building* what idea the artifact tests and what result would show the idea wrong.
