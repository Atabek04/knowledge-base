Part 4 of the IELTS Listening test is a single speaker delivering an academic talk, and the standard preparation advice is to listen to it the way you would listen to a lecture — follow the structure, catch the signposts, expect an introduction and a conclusion.

That advice is half right, and the half that is wrong is the half that gets people hurt.

### What Part 4 removes

Parts 1 and 3 are dialogues, and a dialogue hands you structure for free. Turn-taking marks the boundaries. A question-and-answer pair tells you when a point begins and ends. One speaker asking another to clarify repeats the content at no cost to you.

Part 4 has none of that. It also has <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">one preview block covering all ten questions and no mid-part pause</mark>, unlike Parts 1 to 3, which pause partway through to let you read the rest of the set.

So the reading has to be faster, and there is no second chance to do it.

### The part everybody gets wrong

A real lecture is redundant on purpose. The lecturer previews the outline, restates the important points in different words, and summarises at the end. If you miss something, the structure gives it back to you.

Field is explicit that the IELTS recording does not:

> The lecture mode relies quite heavily upon rephrasing and repetition to underline critical points; it also has a distinctive discourse structure in which the lecturer provides an outline at the outset and a summary at the end. Candidates hearing a short IELTS recording do not have the benefit of these features.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">Part 4 is a lecture compressed until the redundancy is gone. A point you miss is not restated later, so the recovery habit that works in a real lecture — wait, it will come round again — silently costs you the mark.</mark>

This makes [[A missed Listening answer becomes a cascade when you keep listening for information already gone|closing a lost question fast]] more urgent here than anywhere else in the paper: there is no pause to re-anchor in and nothing will repeat to help you.

### What replaces the missing structure

Signposting, and the question paper itself.

Signposts are the only remaining marker of where you are: sequence markers ("first", "next", "finally"), cause-and-effect language ("led to", "resulted in"), explanatory transitions ("the primary reason for"). IDP names missing them as a diagnosed Part 4 error.

#### The note-completion skeleton is a map, not ten gaps

Field notes, as a criticism of the test design, that the gap-fill format "has the unfortunate effect of ... providing *gratis* a great deal of the structure of the lecture which it should be the listener's responsibility to construct".

For a candidate that criticism is a gift. <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Read the note-completion form in the preview block as the talk's outline — what are the headings, what is the argument shape — rather than as ten isolated blanks.</mark> It is the outline the speaker will not give you.

### The uncomfortable finding

Field's high scorers on Part 4 often could not say afterwards what the lecture had been about. Several were aware of it; one said he was choosing words rather than following meaning.

<mark style="background: #FF9E9EA6;">Word-level matching scores well on Part 4 while producing almost no comprehension.</mark> As a candidate that is a legitimate exploit and you may use it. It is worth knowing that it is an exploit — it does not transfer to real academic listening, and Field's report exists partly to recommend the test be changed so that it stops working.

The exploit also has the failure mode described in [[Keyword training backfires when attention shifts from the audio to the question paper|the attention-switching note]]: a match locates an answer without verifying you heard it correctly.
