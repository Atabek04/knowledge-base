### The finding

Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014) analyzed **6.9 million video-watching sessions** across MIT/edX/Harvard/Berkeley courses. Video length was the single strongest predictor of engagement.

- Videos ≤ 3 minutes: learners watched essentially to completion
- Videos 6–9 minutes: engagement dropped to roughly 50% of total length
- Videos > 9 minutes: learners rarely completed them

<mark style="background: yellow">The practical ceiling is 6 minutes.</mark> Beyond that, a growing fraction of learners never reach the questions, summaries, or examples placed later in the video.

---

### Why this constrains retrieval design

An [[Interpolated retrieval testing after each video segment produces larger retention gains than restudying the same content|interpolated retrieval question]] placed at minute 10 of a 15-minute video is reached by only a fraction of learners. Chunking into 5–6 minute segments ensures every retrieval moment is actually encountered.

This is also why a single end-of-lecture test is far less effective than distributed segment tests — distribution guarantees coverage across the natural dropout curve.

---

### The drop is attentional, not motivational

Risko et al. (2012) showed that attention to lecture content declines as a function of time on task, regardless of content quality. The drop past 6 minutes is a normal neurological depletion curve. Short segments reset it.

Khan Academy's average 6–9 minute videos and their practice-after-each-concept structure directly implement this constraint.

---

### Structuring longer topics

A 30-minute lesson should not be one video. It should be 5–6 videos of 5–6 minutes each, with a practice task after each before the next unlocks.

This is Mayer's [[Mayer's Multimedia Learning Principles reduce cognitive load through twelve evidence-based design rules|Segmenting Principle]] (d = 1.36) applied at the series level rather than within a single animation.
