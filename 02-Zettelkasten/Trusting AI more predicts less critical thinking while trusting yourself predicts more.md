Whether a knowledge worker checks an AI's output depends less on the task than on where their confidence sits.

A Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon survey found two kinds of confidence pulling in opposite directions.

---

### The survey

Lee and colleagues (CHI 2025) surveyed 319 knowledge workers about 936 real examples of using generative AI at work, asking when they engaged critical thinking and how much effort it took.

#### Two confidences, opposite effects

- **Confidence in the AI** went with *less* critical thinking.
- **Confidence in their own ability** went with *more*, even though those workers reported it as more effort.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Self-confidence predicts more checking because a person who trusts their own judgment has a standard to measure the AI's answer against.</mark> Without that standard, accepting the output is the only option left, which is [[Automation bias makes people accept a machine's recommendation without verifying it|automation bias]] in its everyday form.

---

### Where the thinking moved

The workers did not stop thinking; the thinking changed job. The survey describes three shifts:

1. from gathering information to **verifying** it
2. from solving the problem to **integrating** the AI's response
3. from executing the task to **stewarding** it

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Task stewardship is overseeing and taking responsibility for work that an AI executed.</mark> It still needs judgment, but judgment is built by doing the execution, which is exactly what the shift removes.

---

### Limits of the finding

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">The data is self-reported and correlational: it measures how much critical thinking people say they did, not a test of their thinking.</mark> The same causation caveat applies as in the [[Heavy AI use correlates with weaker critical thinking but the causal arrow may run both ways|Gerlich correlation]]: people who think less critically may simply trust AI more.
