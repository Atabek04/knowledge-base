A habit is not just a repeated action. It is a neurological loop that, once encoded, runs without conscious decision-making.

Charles Duhigg's "The Power of Habit" (2012) drew on MIT neuroscience research to describe the structure of this loop: **cue → routine → reward**.

---

### The Three Elements

**Cue** — A trigger that tells the brain which habit to run. It can be a time of day, a location, an emotional state, another person, or an action that precedes the behavior.

**Routine** — The behavior itself. This is the part that varies: it can be mental, physical, or emotional.

**Reward** — What the brain gets from completing the loop. The reward is what signals to the brain that this loop is worth remembering and automating.

Over time, cue and reward become linked through anticipation. The brain begins to **crave** the reward as soon as it detects the cue — before the routine even starts. This craving is what makes habits automatic and hard to break.

---

### Why This Matters for Goal-Work

The key insight: habits are not acts of will. They are pattern-completions.

Once a habit loop is wired, the brain doesn't deliberate. It detects the cue, fires the routine, and collects the reward. This is why brushing teeth requires no decision — the loop runs automatically.

This is also why goal-based effort is fragile. Every time you rely on a fresh decision to do the work, you create an opportunity for [[Akrasia Is the Ancient Name for Knowing What's Right and Doing the Opposite|akrasia]] and [[Present Bias Makes Future Goals Lose to Immediate Distraction by Default|present bias]] to intercept. A well-designed habit loop removes the decision point entirely.

---

### The Golden Rule of Habit Change

Duhigg's central practical finding: **you cannot extinguish a habit — you can only replace the routine**.

Keep the cue. Keep the reward. Swap the routine.

This explains why cold-turkey approaches fail. The brain still detects the cue and craves the reward. Willpower alone is fighting a wired anticipation loop. The only durable solution is inserting a new routine between the old cue and the original reward.

---

### Building New Habit Loops

To install a new habit, you need:
1. A reliable, consistent cue (don't vary it)
2. A routine simple enough to start without motivation
3. A reward immediate enough for the brain to associate it with the behavior

The routine must be small enough to execute even on bad days. [[Shrinking the Action to Absurdity Removes the Brain's Resistance to Starting]] describes why this matters — friction before the first step determines whether the loop ever fires.
