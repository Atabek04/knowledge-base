Hermann Ebbinghaus demonstrated in 1885 that memory decays exponentially after encoding. The decay is fastest in the first hours and days, then slows. Goals are no different from any other memory: without reactivation, they become progressively harder to retrieve and act on.

This is not a failure of commitment. It is the normal behavior of a memory system optimized for relevance.

### How Goal Activation Works

In cognitive models of memory, a goal's activation level determines how accessible it is when you are making decisions. Activation is boosted by:

- Seeing a cue associated with the goal
- Thinking about it deliberately
- Retrieving it through review or reflection

When none of these happen, activation decays. Once it falls below threshold, the goal is effectively invisible to the decision-making process — even if you could recall it if asked directly.

The result: you do not decide to abandon your goals. You simply stop noticing opportunities to act on them.

### The "Out of Sight, Out of Mind" Problem

Research on attention and intention confirms that goals require ongoing cognitive accessibility to influence behavior. When a goal is not recently activated, the brain does not flag goal-relevant information in the environment as worth attending to.

This is why people who set annual resolutions in January find them completely absent from their thinking by March — not because the goal stopped being important, but because it stopped being active.

### Scheduled Re-Exposure Is the Solution

The fix is not more willpower or more motivation. It is structural: build scheduled review points that re-activate goals before they fall below threshold.

This is the core function of [[Weekly Review Is the Keystone Habit That Keeps Long-Term Goals Alive]]. Each review is a retrieval event that resets the decay clock.

Daily-level re-exposure — such as choosing your [[The Daily Highlight Forces a Single Goal-Linked Task Into Each Day|Daily Highlight]] — is even more powerful. A goal activated daily never decays far enough to become invisible.

### The Physical Environment as a Trigger

External cues can substitute for deliberate recall. A goal written on a whiteboard in plain sight gets activated every time you see it. This is passive re-exposure — not review, but it maintains a baseline level of activation that prevents total decay.

The implication: keep goals in your environment, not just in your memory.
