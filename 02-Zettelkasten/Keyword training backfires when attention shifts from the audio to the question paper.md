Underline the keywords. Every IELTS preparation course teaches it, and it works often enough that nobody questions it. What almost no source mentions is that the technique has a measured downside, and that the downside grows the more diligently you apply it.

### What the protocols showed

Field's IELTS-commissioned study had candidates report their thinking while taking a Part 4 test. Two findings from those transcripts matter here.

On the mechanics of using the question paper as a guide:

> It became evident that using test items in this way to direct the listening process involved a great deal of switching of attention between task sheet and recording. It was also sometimes counter-productive in that it led to participants missing a piece of information when they were consulting written text.

And on strategy training specifically:

> The training was by no means always beneficial; indeed, it quite often led to a dependence on the written text ... which reduced the amount of attention given to the spoken signal.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">Time spent reading during the audio is not free — it is bought with the audio you did not hear while your eyes were down.</mark>

### Why this is not an argument against previewing

The preview happens in the pause, when there is no audio to lose. That is why it is nearly cost-free and why [[Listening preview time helps mainly through the question stems while the answer options add little|the stems are worth reading first]].

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">The cost appears only when reading and listening overlap.</mark> The distinction is not preview versus no preview — it is whether the reading finishes before the recording starts.

#### The practical form of the rule

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Mark one or two keywords per question, not the question.</mark> The purpose of a mark is to let a glance re-locate you, and a glance is cheap only if there is one thing to glance at.

An underlined sentence needs to be re-read, and re-reading is where the audio goes missing.

### The deeper failure it enables

Keyword dependence does not only cost attention. It changes what you are doing from listening to matching, and matching produces confident wrong answers.

Field recorded candidates who located the right region of the recording without understanding it — two interpreted *low frequency* as meaning infrequent. Worse, some built whole interpretations on a single misheard word: three reported on shark *machines* where the audio said *meshing*, and one misheard *beach* as *breed* and took the entire lecture to be about shark breeding.

<mark style="background: #FF9E9EA6;">A keyword match tells you where the answer is. It does not tell you what was said, and it cannot warn you when you have misheard the keyword itself.</mark>

This is the same mechanism that makes [[Predict the answer slot rather than the answer word because a predicted word gets confirmed instead of heard|predicting the exact word]] dangerous: both replace hearing with confirming.
