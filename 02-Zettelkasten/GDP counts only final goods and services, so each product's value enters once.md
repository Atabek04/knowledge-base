Claims like "AI will add 7% to world output" are claims about one number: a country's **GDP**. Reading them requires knowing what that number counts and what it deliberately leaves out.

### Glossary

- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>GDP (gross domestic product)</b>: the total market value of all final goods and services produced inside a country in one year.</mark> *Gross*: before subtracting wear on machines. *Domestic*: inside the country. *Product*: what is produced.
- **Goods**: physical things that are produced and sold (bread, cars). **Services**: work done for someone (a haircut, a lawyer's advice).
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Final good</b>: a good bought by its last user, not to be turned into something else.</mark>
- **Intermediate good**: a good bought to be used up in making another good, like flour bought by a bakery.
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Double counting</b>: counting the same value twice, once on its own and again inside the price of the product it went into.</mark>
- **Value added**: what one stage of production adds: its sales minus what it paid for intermediate goods.

---

### Why only final goods count

A mill sells $500 of flour to a bakery. The bakery turns it into 1,000 loaves and sells them at $2 each, for $2,000.

GDP is $2,000, not $2,500. The bread is the **final good**; the flour is an intermediate good whose $500 is already inside the bread's $2,000 price. Adding it again is what economists call **double counting**.

The same holds for a car: $400 of tyres sold to a car maker, then a $20,000 car sold to a family, adds $20,000 to GDP, not $20,400.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">The price of a final good already contains the value of every intermediate good used to make it, so counting intermediate goods as well double counts them.</mark> GDP counts spending on final goods only; a carmaker's purchase of parts is excluded, and only the finished car counts ([Wikipedia: Gross domestic product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product)).

#### The same total, counted by value added

The mill adds $500. The bakery adds $2,000 minus $500, which is $1,500. Together: $2,000.

Summing the value added at every stage gives the same GDP as summing final sales; statisticians call this the production approach.
