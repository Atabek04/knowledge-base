The classic counterexample to "automating a task eliminates the workers who do it" is the bank teller — but the full story has a second act that the optimistic version usually omits.

It is a concrete historical case for the principle that [[Jobs survive automation when they aren't worth the compute, not because humans are better|a job survives or dies on economics, not on whether a machine *can* do the task]].

---

### Act one: automation grew the jobs (1990s-2010)

As ~400,000 ATMs spread across the US, tellers per branch fell (from ~20 to ~13 between 1988 and 2004).

Yet because each branch got cheaper to run, banks opened <mark style="background: green">43% more urban branches</mark> — so <mark style="background: yellow">total teller jobs rose</mark>, from ~500k to ~600k, growing a bit faster than the labor force.

The task was automated; the workers were *not* eliminated. Their role shifted toward relationship and sales work the machine couldn't do.

---

### Act two: the trend reversed (post-2010)

<mark style="background: pink">The optimistic story stops too early.</mark> Teller employment then fell — from ~332k (2010) to ~235k (2016) — as mobile and smartphone banking closed branches.

As one summary put it: <mark style="background: cyan;">the ATM didn't kill teller jobs, but the iPhone did.</mark>

#### The lesson

Automation of a single *task* can expand a role for years, then a later shift in the *whole channel* can collapse it. Cite act one only as a historical pattern — not as proof that automation always grows employment, and not via a simple "cheaper-per-unit always expands demand" mechanism, which doesn't hold up.
