### What it is

The Method of Loci (classical antiquity, attributed to Simonides of Ceos) encodes ordered information by placing items at specific locations along a familiar physical route — a building, a commute, a room sequence — then retrieves them by mentally walking the route.

**Four steps for vocabulary:**
1. Choose a familiar route with distinct waypoints (loci)
2. Assign each vocabulary item to a specific locus
3. Create a vivid, interactive image connecting the word's meaning to that location
4. Retrieve by mentally walking the route in order

Example: to memorize "ubiquitous" at your front door — imagine a thousand identical copies of yourself pressing through the doorframe simultaneously, everywhere at once.

---

### Why it works — spatial encoding

Humans evolved strong spatial memory (hippocampal navigation circuits). Binding new information to pre-existing spatial anchors leverages this system rather than competing with working memory limits.

This addresses the same bottleneck described by [[Cognitive load theory explains why working memory bottlenecks determine learning speed|cognitive load theory]]: by offloading sequential memory to spatial structure, you bypass the ~7-item working memory ceiling.

The mechanism is the same as [[Keyword mnemonic links a foreign word to a vivid mental image via a sound-alike bridge|keyword mnemonics]] (vivid encoding), but the palace provides an *external structure* for ordering and retrieval that keyword mnemonics alone cannot.

---

### Best use cases

- Ordered word lists (domain vocabulary, declension patterns, numbered sequences)
- Exam cramming where a large set must be recalled quickly
- <mark style="background: cyan">ROI is highest for sets of 50+ items</mark> — the palace construction overhead amortizes at scale
- Competition-level memorization (memory athletes routinely memorize thousands of items this way)

---

### Limitations

- <mark style="background: pink">Loci can be overwritten</mark> — reusing the same palace for a new set causes interference with previously stored content
- Retrieval is sequential; poor for random-access recall of individual words
- Building a palace for semantically random word sets (e.g., a frequency-list vocabulary set) feels artificial and slow
- Does not encode pronunciation — purely a meaning-to-form retrieval tool
