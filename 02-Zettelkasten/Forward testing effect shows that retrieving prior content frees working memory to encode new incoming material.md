### What it is

The <mark style="background: yellow">forward testing effect</mark> is a phenomenon where being tested on earlier material doesn't just consolidate that material — it actively improves how well learners absorb the *next* new segment, even though that segment was never tested.

A 2025 controlled study (PMC12109270) isolated it cleanly: students tested on video segments 1 and 2 scored significantly better on segment 3 content. The review condition (re-reading prior content) produced no forward benefit.

---

### Mechanism

Successful retrieval appears to clear the working memory buffer. When prior knowledge is actively consolidated into long-term memory through recall, working memory is freed to encode incoming material rather than holding half-processed residue from the previous segment.

Review keeps prior content "in view" but does not consolidate it, so the residue remains and competes with encoding of new material.

---

### Application across lesson structure

The forward testing effect extends beyond individual videos. Opening a lesson with questions on *prior session content* is not mere review — it measurably primes encoding of the new session's content.

For a video series: starting Video 2 with 3 questions on Video 1 material simultaneously consolidates Video 1 and improves uptake of Video 2. For a language course: beginning each lesson with a short vocabulary recall from the previous session improves retention of the new session's vocabulary.

The interval between the original viewing and the retrieval test also matters — [[Spaced repetition schedules vocabulary review at expanding intervals matched to the forgetting curve|spaced review]] amplifies the consolidation effect.
