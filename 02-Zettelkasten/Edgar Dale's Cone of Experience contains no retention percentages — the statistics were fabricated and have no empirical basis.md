### What Dale's Cone actually said

Edgar Dale's <mark style="background: yellow">Cone of Experience</mark> (1946, *Audio-Visual Methods in Teaching*) is a visual taxonomy — a classification of instructional media from most concrete at the base (direct purposeful experience) to most abstract at the apex (verbal symbols). It was explicitly a *visual analogy*, not a measurement instrument.

Dale devoted six pages in the 1969 revised edition to *"Some Possible Misconceptions"*, warning specifically against treating the cone as a rigid or prescriptive hierarchy.

**The cone contained no percentages. No retention data. No research claims.**

---

### Where the numbers came from

The widely-circulated "Learning Pyramid" statistics — e.g., "we remember 10% of what we read, 50% of what we see and hear, 90% of what we do" — originate from **D.G. Treichler** in a 1967 trade article (*Film and Audio-Visual Communications*: "Are You Missing the Boat in Training Aids?").

Treichler worked at Mobil Oil. His article provided:
- No citations
- No methodology
- No source for the figures

Researcher Michael Molenda investigated the origin and concluded the figures appear to have been invented or inherited without empirical basis. The National Training Laboratories Institute (NTL), which circulated an alternative version called the "Learning Pyramid," later stated they could **"no longer locate the source of the original information."**

The percentages appear across sources in contradictory versions — some show 20%, some 30%, some 50% for the same modality — itself evidence they were never measured.

---

### Why this matters

Educators and trainers cite these numbers with authority in conference slides, training materials, and curriculum design documents — typically without knowing their origin is fabricated.

<mark style="background: pink">Building instructional decisions on these figures is building on nothing.</mark>

Mayer's multimedia learning research provides actual measured effect sizes: the modality principle (d ≈ 1.02), the spatial contiguity effect (g = 0.63), the segmenting principle (d = 1.36). These replace the invented percentages with real data.

---

### What is supported

The *direction* of the underlying claims is supported — images + words > words alone; active processing > passive reception; generation > study. But these come from Paivio's dual coding experiments, Mayer's multimedia research, and the generation effect literature — not from the cone.
