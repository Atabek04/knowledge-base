### What it is

<mark style="background: yellow">Information-gap theory</mark> (Loewenstein, 1994) holds that curiosity is not a personality trait but a triggered *state* — activated when attention fixes on the distance between what you know and what you want to know.

Naming an open question before answering it is not rhetorical warmup. It generates a neurological state that changes how the brain encodes everything that follows.

---

### The neuroscience

Gruber et al. (2014, *Neuron*) induced high vs. low curiosity states using trivia questions, then measured brain activity and memory for *incidental* unrelated images shown during the wait.

Curious states activated the substantia nigra/VTA and nucleus accumbens — the same dopamine reward circuits involved in hunger and financial reward.

Participants in high-curiosity states remembered those incidental images significantly better, even though the images were unrelated to the trivia topic. <mark style="background: cyan">An open information gap boosts encoding of everything in the learner's attention, not just the target content.</mark>

---

### The Zeigarnik mechanism

Incomplete tasks are held in active memory and rehearsed subconsciously until resolved (Zeigarnik effect, 1920s). A question asked at the start of a lesson remains "pending" throughout, extending effective engagement without deliberate effort.

This is why cliffhanger endings in series increase session-to-session retention — the unresolved gap carries forward as a background process.

---

### Application: replace topic announcements with gap-creating openings

❌ "Today we'll cover the subjunctive."
✓ "By the end of this video you'll understand why native speakers say *espero que vengas* but not *espero que vienes* — and why that switch immediately marks you as a learner."

The second version names the gap (why does the verb form change?) before it is closed. The learner enters the lesson already activated to find the answer.

The pattern applies equally to any domain: programming ("By the end you'll understand why this 5-line function can silently corrupt data"), history ("You'll see why the side with the larger army lost decisively"), or system design ("You'll find out why this seemingly simple requirement brought down a major service").
