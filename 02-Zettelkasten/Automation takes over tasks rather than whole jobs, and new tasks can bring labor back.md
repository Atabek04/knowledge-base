"AI will replace lawyers" treats a job as one indivisible thing a machine either can or cannot do. Labor economists instead split every job into the tasks it is made of, and ask which of those tasks a machine takes over. This approach is the **task model**.

### Glossary

- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Task</b>: one unit of work inside a job, such as counting cash or answering a customer.</mark>
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Task model</b>: the view that a job is a bundle of tasks, and that automation takes over tasks, not whole jobs.</mark>
- **Routine task**: a task done by following explicit rules, so a computer can be programmed to do it.
- **Nonroutine task**: a task needing judgement, problem-solving or complex communication, which has no full rulebook.
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Displacement effect</b>: machines take over tasks workers used to do, shrinking labor's part of production.</mark> Labor is *displaced*, moved out of its place.
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Reinstatement effect</b>: new tasks that humans do better are created, pulling labor back into production.</mark> Labor is *reinstated*, put back.

---

### Who built the task model

Autor, Levy and Murnane started it in "The Skill Content of Recent Technological Change" (*Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2003). They argued that computers substitute for workers on tasks that follow explicit rules, and complement workers on nonroutine problem-solving and complex communication. From 1960 to 1998, computerization went with less routine work and more nonroutine cognitive work, even within the same occupations ([paper PDF](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/the%20skill%20content%202003.pdf)).

Acemoglu and Restrepo extended it in "Automation and New Tasks" (*Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 2019) by adding the creation of new tasks ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.33.2.3)).

---

### Partial automation of a job

A bank teller's day has four tasks: counting out cash, taking deposits, answering questions, and selling loans. A machine that does the first two leaves the job standing, now made of the last two.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Automating some of a job's tasks changes what the job consists of; the job disappears only if every task in it is automated.</mark>

---

### Displacement against reinstatement

#### Displacement can go all the way

Nothing stops the next machine from taking the remaining tasks too. Once questions and loan sales move to an app, the teller has no tasks left, and the service becomes cheaper because the machine's output is abundant.

#### Reinstatement pulls the other way

Every wave of technology also creates tasks that did not exist before: managing a bank's app, advising on new financial products. Acemoglu and Restrepo attribute the slower US employment growth of the last three decades to faster displacement combined with weaker reinstatement than in earlier decades.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">Whether automation lowers total employment is not settled by which tasks a machine can do; it depends on whether new tasks are created as fast as old ones are displaced.</mark>
