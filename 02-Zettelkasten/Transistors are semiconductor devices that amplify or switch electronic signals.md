A transistor is a semiconductor device that uses a small input signal to control a larger output signal, functioning as an amplifier or electronic switch.

There are two main categories:

Bipolar Junction Transistors (BJT):
- Three terminals: base, collector, emitter
- Small current at base controls larger current between collector and emitter
- Current gain allows a weak signal to control strong signal

Field-Effect Transistors (FET):
- Three terminals: gate, source, drain
- Voltage at gate controls current between source and drain
- Gate requires virtually no current (voltage-controlled, not current-controlled)

Transistors operate in two practical modes relevant to digital circuits:
- Saturation: fully "on," current flows freely with minimal voltage drop (acts as closed switch)
- Cutoff: fully "off," negligible current flows (acts as open switch)

This two-state operation (on/off) directly maps to binary (1/0), making transistors the ideal physical substrate for binary logic.

Why transistors revolutionized computing compared to vacuum tubes:
- Solid-state construction: no vacuum to maintain, no filament to burn out
- Smaller: millimeters vs. inches
- Lower power: milliwatts vs. watts
- More reliable: theoretical lifetime of decades
- Faster switching: nanoseconds vs. microseconds
- Cheaper to manufacture: semiconductor production scales economically

Modern processors contain 50+ billion transistors on a piece of silicon smaller than a postage stamp. Each transistor operates identically to transistors from the 1950s—the difference is in quantity, density, and speed.

Transistor miniaturization (fitting more into less space) directly enabled Moore's Law and the exponential growth of computing power.
