What people spend money on is not fixed: it changes as they get richer, in a pattern regular enough to have a name. Knowing the pattern tells you which industries grow as an economy grows, and where workers displaced by cheaper food ended up.

### Glossary

- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Engel's law</b>: as income rises, the share of income spent on food falls, even though total food spending still grows.</mark> Named after the statistician Ernst Engel, who found it in household data in 1857.
- **Spending share**: the percentage of a household's total spending that goes to one category.
- **Engel coefficient**: a country's or household's food spending share, used as a rough measure of how well-off it is.
- **Necessity**: a good people buy first and cannot cut much, like food. Its share falls as income rises.
- **Luxury** (in economics): a good whose share of spending rises as income rises, such as travel, health care or higher education. It need not be glamorous.

---

### Engel's pattern

A family that earns $1,000 and spends $500 on food gives food 50% of its spending. If its income doubles to $2,000, it eats a bit better, say $700 of food, but food is now only 35%. The other $1,300 goes on everything else.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Food spending grows more slowly than income because a person can eat only so much, so each rise in income moves a larger share to other goods.</mark> This is the household version of diminishing marginal utility.

In Engel's own data, the food share fell from about 73% for the poorest families to about 57% for the richest. The UN treats a food share above 59% as poverty and below 30% as wealth ([Wikipedia: Engel's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engel%27s_law)).

### US household spending over a century

| Category | 1901 | 2002–03 |
|---|---|---|
| Food | 42.5% | 13.1% |
| Clothing | 14.0% | 4.2% |
| Housing | 23.3% | 32.8% |
| Everything else | about 20% | about 50% |

Source: [BLS, 100 Years of U.S. Consumer Spending](https://www.bls.gov/opub/100-years-of-u-s-consumer-spending.pdf).

"Everything else" grew from a fifth to half of spending: cars and petrol, electricity and appliances, health care, education, insurance, travel, entertainment, and later phones and internet. Most of these barely existed in 1901, and each became an industry employing millions.

#### Reading the table

Food and clothing are necessities, so their shares collapsed. Housing rose because people spent their extra income on bigger, better-equipped homes. The growth went to categories that did not exist or were luxuries a century earlier.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">A falling share does not mean falling spending: Americans spend far more dollars on food today than in 1901; food simply grew more slowly than income.</mark>
