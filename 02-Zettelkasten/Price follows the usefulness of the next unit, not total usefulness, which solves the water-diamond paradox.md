Water keeps people alive and diamonds do not, yet water is almost free and diamonds are expensive. Economists call this the **water-diamond paradox**, and solving it explains how prices work in general.

### Glossary

- **Scarcity**: having less of something than people want. Diamonds are scarce; tap water in a city is not.
- **Abundance**: the opposite of scarcity, so much supply that one more unit is barely missed.
- **Commodity**: a product whose units are interchangeable, so buyers choose only on price (wheat, crude oil, tap water). "Commoditized" means a product has become this way, and its price has been pushed down.
- **Value in use**: how useful a thing is for living. Water's is huge.
- **Value in exchange**: what a thing can be traded for, which is its price. Water's is tiny.
- **Paradox**: a statement that seems to contradict itself but turns out to be true.
- **Marginal**: at the *margin*, the edge. It means "one more unit" (or one fewer) on top of what you already have.
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Marginal utility</b>: the extra satisfaction from the next unit of a good.</mark> *Utility* means usefulness, so this is the usefulness of the unit at the edge.
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Diminishing marginal utility</b>: each extra unit of the same good is worth less than the one before.</mark>

---

### Smith's puzzle

Adam Smith stated it in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776, Book I, ch. 4), in the chapter "Of the Origin and Use of Money." His point was that nothing is more useful than water, yet water buys almost nothing, while a diamond buys a great deal.

Smith concluded that value in use and value in exchange are two different things. He did not explain *why* they come apart.

Plato, Copernicus, John Locke and John Law had touched the same puzzle earlier ([Wikipedia: Paradox of value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox_of_value)).

---

### Why the next unit decides the price

The answer came in the 1870s, when William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras each independently developed the idea of **marginal utility** ([Wikipedia: Marginalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginalism)).

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">By marginal utility, the price of a good follows the usefulness of the next unit, not the total usefulness of the whole supply.</mark>

#### The same glass of water, two prices

At home with a working tap, you already have all the water you need. The next glass is used to wash a cup or is simply not drunk, so you would pay almost nothing for it.

Lost in a desert for two days, the next glass may decide whether you live. You would pay anything you have.

The water is identical. What changed is how much you already have, and so the **marginal utility** of the next glass.

#### Diamonds sit at the other end

There are so few diamonds that each one meets a want nobody has filled yet, so the next diamond is still worth a lot to a buyer.

Water's *total* usefulness is enormous, but there is so much of it that the *next* litre is almost worthless.

#### Diminishing marginal utility

Under **diminishing marginal utility**, each extra unit of the same good is usually worth less than the one before: the first glass for a thirsty person, then the second, the fifth, the twentieth. Böhm-Bawerk illustrated this in 1891 with a farmer and five sacks of grain. The first sack feeds him, the second makes him strong, and the last feeds parrots for fun.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">"Essential" does not mean "expensive": a thing can be vital to life and still cheap if there is plenty of it.</mark>
