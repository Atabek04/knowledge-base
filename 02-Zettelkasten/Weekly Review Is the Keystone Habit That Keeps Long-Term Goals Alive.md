David Allen calls the Weekly Review "the critical success factor" of his GTD system. Without it, task lists grow stale, the system loses credibility, and goals silently disappear from daily attention.

It is not just a planning session. It is the mechanism that closes the loop between what you intend to do and what actually happens.

### What the Weekly Review Does

Allen structures it in three phases:

- **Get Clear** — process every loose end: emails, notes, scraps, open loops
- **Get Current** — review all projects, next-action lists, and waiting items
- **Get Creative** — step back and think about whether you are working on the right things

The review moves you from reactive mode (responding to whatever demands attention) to intentional mode (choosing where your effort goes).

### The Cognitive Mechanism Behind It

Unfinished tasks create persistent mental tension — the Zeigarnik Effect. Your mind holds incomplete loops in working memory, generating background anxiety that drains focus even when you are not consciously thinking about the task.

The Weekly Review discharges this tension by moving everything into a trusted external system. Once captured and processed, the brain stops holding on.

### Why Skipping It Is Catastrophic

Goals set in January do not execute themselves in February. Without a weekly reconnection point, long-term goals lose their grip on daily decisions. The gap between intention and execution widens quietly, week by week.

The review is the scheduled re-exposure that prevents [[Goals Fade Without Scheduled Review Because Activation Decays Over Time|goal decay]].

### The Minimum Viable Review

A full review takes 60-90 minutes. But even 15 minutes of "get current" — scanning your project list and setting three priorities for the week — is enough to prevent total drift.

**The rule:** If you cannot do the full review, do the minimum. Something beats nothing.
