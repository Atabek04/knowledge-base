### What it is

<mark style="background: yellow">Interpolated retrieval testing</mark> means embedding short-answer questions at the boundary of each video segment — not just at the end — so learners recall what they just watched before watching the next segment.

Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013) divided a 21-minute video into four segments, each followed by 6 short-answer questions. At the final assessment, the tested group scored **90%** vs. **76%** for those who restudied vs. **68%** for untested controls. Effect size d = 1.38 for tested vs. untested.

Restudying produced *no significant advantage* over watching once without any active engagement. Retrieval effort — not re-exposure — is the active ingredient.

---

### Why segment-level testing beats end-of-video testing

Testing only at the end means most learners drifted before reaching it. The Szpunar study measured mind-wandering via thought probes and found the tested group reported significantly less attention drift throughout — a separate benefit from memory consolidation.

A 2025 replication (PMC12109270) isolated the [[Forward testing effect shows that retrieving prior content frees working memory to encode new incoming material|forward testing effect]]: being tested on segment 2 measurably improved uptake of segment 3, even though segment 3 was not tested. Successful retrieval clears working memory for new encoding.

---

### What counts as retrieval

Only content-relevant questions work. The same 2025 study tested four conditions after each segment: episodic (recall what you just watched), review slides, autobiographical recall, and semantic recall (name animals).

Only episodic retrieval improved learning of the next segment (d = 0.642). Telling learners to "take a moment to reflect" or "recall a related experience" produced **zero benefit** (p = 0.594 for the semantic condition).

The question must test the video's own content.

---

### Implementation

- Place questions after every 5–6 minute segment, not only at the end
- Use open-ended or completion format over multiple choice — generative reconstruction outperforms recognition
- Questions need not be graded for accuracy — completion credit is sufficient to drive the effect
- The pause also functions as an attention reset, separate from the memory benefit
