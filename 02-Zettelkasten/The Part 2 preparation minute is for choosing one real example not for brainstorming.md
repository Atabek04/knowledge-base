IELTS publishes what the minute before the long turn is for, and the advice is narrower than most candidates treat it: <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">take the first example you think of and can describe in English</mark>. The stated reason is that test takers who invent something usually run out of things to say.

The mechanics from the same guidance: ask about an unclear word at the *start* of the minute, write notes in English, in phrases rather than sentences, in the order you intend to speak them. The notes are framed as a safety harness, not a script — and both card and notes stay in view throughout.

The bullet points on the card are a guide. <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">They are not a checklist and covering all of them is not required</mark>; the thing being sampled is extended speech.

Being cut off at two minutes is a good outcome — it proves you can speak at length. Stopping early is not a discrete penalty, but it is a lost sample, and the fluency band is an average over the whole test.

<mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">A real memory beats an impressive invented one</mark>, because invented content forces language-related hesitation, the one kind band 8 does not permit.
