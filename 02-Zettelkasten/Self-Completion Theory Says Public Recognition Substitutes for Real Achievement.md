Self-completion theory (Wicklund & Gollwitzer, 1980s) proposes that people pursuing an identity goal — being a musician, a scholar, an athlete — collect **symbols** that prove the identity to themselves and others.

Symbols can be real (a published paper, a finished marathon) or social (someone acknowledging "you're a writer"). The brain treats both as evidence toward the same identity.

## Why This Matters for Goal-Sharing

When someone hears your goal and *responds* — congratulates you, takes you seriously, treats you as that person — they hand you a free symbol. The identity-need quiets down.

This is why announcing a goal can feel so satisfying. You collected the reward without paying the cost.

- Real achievement → real symbol → identity reinforced
- Public announcement → social symbol → identity reinforced *almost as much*
- Brain doesn't fully distinguish the two

## The Trap

The drive to pursue an identity goal comes from feeling **incomplete** in that identity. Symbols reduce the incompleteness. So premature symbols reduce the drive to do the real work.

This is the mechanism behind [[Telling Your Goals Prematurely Can Drain the Motivation to Pursue Them]] and overlaps with [[Planning feels like doing because the brain rewards anticipation with dopamine]] — both are cases where the brain accepts a cheap substitute for the real thing.

## Practical Implication

If you want to *do* the thing, deny yourself the symbols until you've earned them.

- Don't call yourself a writer until you've published
- Don't post the gym selfie before the workout
- Don't tell people you're "learning Arabic" — show them after you can speak it

The discomfort of being incomplete is the engine. Don't switch it off prematurely.
