### The claim

Michael Long's Interactional Hypothesis (1981, 1996) argues that *interaction* — specifically the negotiation of meaning when communication breaks down — provides acquisition conditions that passive input cannot replicate.

When a learner and interlocutor negotiate meaning, the conversation automatically modifies itself to make input comprehensible *and* draws attention to problematic forms — two acquisition prerequisites at once.

---

### Negotiation of meaning — the mechanisms

| Move | Who makes it | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Clarification request** | Listener | "Sorry, what do you mean?" → speaker reformulates |
| **Confirmation check** | Listener | "You mean X?" → verifies mutual understanding |
| **Comprehension check** | Speaker | "Do you follow?" → speaker modifies if not |
| **Recast** | Interlocutor | Rephrases learner's ill-formed utterance in correct form without interrupting meaning |

Recasts are especially significant: the interlocutor provides the correct form *while keeping the conversation moving*, delivering implicit corrective feedback without explicit grammar instruction.

---

### Why interaction beats passive input

Passive input (reading, listening without interaction) requires the learner to *already* know that something is wrong.

Interaction *creates* the moment of difficulty and immediately provides the resolution. This is the Socratic dynamic applied to language acquisition — the [[Socratic questioning uses six question types to probe thinking rather than deliver content|Socratic questioning]] principle extended to form-focused feedback.

---

### Evidence

Gass & Varonis (1985) first documented how negotiation sequences produced vocabulary acquisition. Mackey & Goo's (2007) meta-analysis of 28 studies found interaction with negotiation produced significantly larger gains in vocabulary and grammar over comprehensible input alone (d ≈ 0.6–0.8).

---

### Application

**For language learners:** prioritize conversations with interlocutors who will negotiate (native speakers, tutors) over passive consumption alone. Even 15 minutes of negotiated conversation generates more acquisition signal than an hour of passive listening.

**For teachers:** structure activities to *require* communication gaps — information-gap tasks, jigsaw activities — so negotiation arises naturally. If both students already know the answer, there is no gap to negotiate.
