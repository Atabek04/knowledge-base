### What it is

The Affective Filter Hypothesis (Krashen, 1982) states that even when input is comprehensible, negative emotional states raise a psychological "filter" that blocks acquisition from proceeding.

Krashen's core formula: acquisition requires <mark style="background: yellow">comprehensible input at i+1</mark> (slightly above current level) *plus* a low affective filter.

If either condition fails, acquisition stalls.

---

### The mechanism

When the filter is <mark style="background: pink">**high**</mark>: input arrives, the learner may intellectually understand it, but it does not get acquired — it doesn't stick.

When the filter is <mark style="background: green">**low**</mark>: input passes through freely and acquisition proceeds naturally.

| Raises the filter (blocks) | Lowers the filter (enables) |
|---|---|
| Anxiety, fear, embarrassment | Curiosity, positive motivation |
| Low self-confidence | High self-confidence |
| Being put on the spot publicly | Low-stakes, private practice |
| Unexplained jargon, missing prerequisites | Clear framing, prior context |

---

### What happens when learners hit unexplained terms

When a learner encounters an unexplained term mid-lesson, the affective filter rises.

They stop processing forward. Attention diverts to the unknown term. A spiral of confusion or disengagement begins.

This is directly why [[Advance organizers give learners a cognitive hook before new content arrives|advance organizers]] and pre-teaching vocabulary matter: they prevent the filter spike before it happens.

---

### Important caveat

<mark style="background: cyan">The "filter" metaphor is empirically weak.</mark> McLaughlin (1987), Lightbown & Spada (2006), and Liu (2015) note it lacks direct neurological measurement and is unfalsifiable as stated.

The *practical insight* — that anxiety suppresses learning and calm confidence supports it — is well-supported in broader cognitive and motivation research (test anxiety literature, self-determination theory).

Use the model as a design heuristic, not a neurological claim.

---

### Application to instruction design

- Pre-teach all new vocabulary and jargon *before* it appears in context
- Use low-stakes practice before high-stakes assessment
- Normalize partial understanding — don't let unexplained terms become blockers
- Define terms at first use; never assume shared vocabulary in a technical course
