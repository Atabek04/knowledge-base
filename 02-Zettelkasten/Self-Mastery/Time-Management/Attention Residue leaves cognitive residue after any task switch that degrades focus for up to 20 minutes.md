When you switch away from a task — even for 30 seconds — part of your attention stays stuck on it. You return physically, but cognitively you are still half-processing what you just left. This is Attention Residue.

The trap is that it feels like you've moved on. You closed Telegram. You're back to the code. But your brain hasn't closed the tab.

### Why it matters

Deep focus doesn't survive interruptions the way we assume. Every time you check a notification, open YouTube, or glance at LinkedIn, you don't just lose those seconds — you lose the 10–20 minutes it takes to rebuild concentration afterward.

This means a 2-minute scroll can cost a 20-minute recovery window. The cost is invisible, which is why it keeps happening.

---

### How it works

The brain keeps an open loop on unfinished tasks. When you switch away mid-task, it maintains a background thread trying to resolve the incomplete work. That thread competes with whatever you're trying to focus on next.

The heavier the first task, the stronger the residue. Complex analytical work leaves more residue than simple mechanical tasks.

---

### Research backing

Sophie Leroy (2009) demonstrated through controlled experiments that subjects who were interrupted mid-task performed measurably worse on subsequent tasks — even when they believed they had fully redirected their attention. The residue persisted whether or not the first task was completed.

---

### How to apply it

**Before starting a work block**, write one sentence: *"I left off at X, next step is Y."*

This offloads the open loop. Your brain stops holding the thread because it's written down. The residue drains before it starts.

**Physical separation beats willpower**: put your phone in another room — not face-down, not on silent — another room. Every visible notification is an open invitation for a switch.

**Do not check anything** between sitting down and 25 minutes in. No "quick checks." There is no such thing as a quick check.
