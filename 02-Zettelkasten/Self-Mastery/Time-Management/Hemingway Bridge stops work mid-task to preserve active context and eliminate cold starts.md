Hemingway's rule: stop writing while you still know exactly what comes next. Never stop at the end of a section — stop in the middle of a thought, mid-sentence if needed.

Applied to any knowledge work: end every session at a deliberate "bridge point" — a moment where the next step is completely obvious — rather than at a natural completion boundary.

### Why it matters

Stopping at a completion point feels satisfying but creates a cold start. Next session begins with: "Now what?" The energy cost of reorienting can trigger procrastination before you've written a single line.

Stopping mid-task feels uncomfortable — and that discomfort is the mechanism. Your brain holds the unfinished thread, keeping the context alive.

---

### How it works

This is the **Zeigarnik Effect** (Bluma Zeigarnik, 1927): the brain retains incomplete tasks more readily than completed ones. Waiters remember orders that haven't been served; once the order is delivered, it fades.

By stopping at an unresolved point, you exploit this. Your brain stays lightly engaged with the problem during rest — not stressfully, just persistently. You return already oriented.

---

### Research backing

Zeigarnik's original experiments showed that interrupted tasks were recalled roughly twice as well as completed ones. The effect persists in cognitive work: unfinished problems continue to activate neural circuits during downtime, which is why solutions sometimes arrive during a walk or shower.

---

### How to apply it

**At the end of every work session**, write 2–3 sentences:
- What I just finished
- Exactly where I stopped
- The next concrete action (not "continue feature" — "write the validation method for the input field")

This is the bridge. It takes 90 seconds and eliminates the orientation cost at the start of the next session.

**Never stop at the end of a chapter, a feature, or a lesson.** Stop at a natural transition point *inside* a unit. Completion creates closure. Closure creates cold starts.
