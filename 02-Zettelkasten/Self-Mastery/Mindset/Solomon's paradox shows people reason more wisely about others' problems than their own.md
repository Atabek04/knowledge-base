Most people give a friend sound, balanced advice about a crisis, then handle the same crisis badly when it lands on them. Psychology calls this gap Solomon's paradox.

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Solomon's paradox is the self-other asymmetry in wise reasoning: people reason more wisely about other people's problems than about their own.</mark>

---

### What counts as wise reasoning

The research scores wisdom on four habits of thought:

- admitting the limits of what you know
- considering the other side's perspective
- looking for a compromise
- recognising that the situation can change

About a friend's problem, people do all four. About their own, they narrow: one perspective, high certainty, no room for compromise.

---

### Research backing

Igor Grossmann and Ethan Kross, *Psychological Science* (2014).

Participants imagined a partner's infidelity, either happening to themselves or to a friend. The "friend" condition produced reliably wiser reasoning than the "self" condition.

The gap appeared in younger adults (20 to 40) and older adults (60 to 80) alike, so age did not make people wiser about their own lives.

A second condition removed the gap: participants who reflected on their own problem from a distanced, third-person view reasoned as wisely as they did about a friend's.

---

### Why the wisdom already exists

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">The asymmetry shows the capacity for wise reasoning is already present, because it works on a friend's case; what fails is applying it when your own stake is involved.</mark>

So adding knowledge does not close the gap. The Islamic tradition names the same failure as [[Ibn al-Qayyim traces misguidance to shubuhat corrupting knowledge and shahawat corrupting the will|a corruption of the will, not of knowledge]]. The Qur'an states it in Fatir 35:8: the one whose evil deed is made attractive to him sees it as good.

The fix follows from the diagnosis: [[Self-distancing through third-person reflection restores wise reasoning about your own problems|self-distancing]] borrows the outsider's view, and [[Al-Ghazali gives four ways to see your own faults because the nafs hides them from itself|al-Ghazali's four ways]] bring in outsiders who see what you cannot.

---

### The name rests on a claim Islam rejects

The paradox is named after the biblical account of Solomon, who is said to have judged wisely for others while his own life ended in idolatry.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">Sulayman (peace be upon him) is a prophet, and the Qur'an clears him of disbelief, so the finding is sound but the story behind its name is not.</mark> Al-Baqarah 2:102: wa ma kafara Sulayman, "Sulayman did not disbelieve."
