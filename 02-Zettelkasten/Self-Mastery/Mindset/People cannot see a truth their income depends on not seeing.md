Smart, honest people regularly defend things that turn out to be plainly false. The usual cause is not stupidity or lying, but money: their living depends on the false thing staying true.

Upton Sinclair, an American writer, put it in one line in *I, Candidate for Governor: And How I Got Licked* (1935):

> It is difficult to get a man to understand something, when his salary depends on his not understanding it.

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Sinclair's law: when a person's income depends on not seeing a truth, they resist seeing it.</mark>

---

### Why it is not lying

A liar sees the truth and hides it. The person Sinclair describes never fully sees it.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Self-interest works upstream of belief: it decides which evidence feels convincing and which feels weak, so the conclusion arrives already shaped and feels honestly held.</mark>

This is why the bias survives intelligence. A clever mind is better at building arguments, and it builds them for whichever side pays.

It is the same failure as [[Solomon's paradox shows people reason more wisely about others' problems than their own|Solomon's paradox]]: the judgment works fine until the person's own stake is involved. The Islamic tradition calls this [[Ibn al-Qayyim traces misguidance to shubuhat corrupting knowledge and shahawat corrupting the will|shahawat corrupting the will]], where desire, not missing information, bends what a person accepts.

---

### Documented cases

| Case | Who had the stake | What they would not see |
|---|---|---|
| Tobacco, 1950s to 1990s | Cigarette firms | Their own papers showed smoking caused cancer and addiction; they denied it publicly for decades |
| Leaded petrol, 1920s to 1970s | Robert Kehoe, industry-funded scientist | He defended lead as safe for about 40 years; Clair Patterson, who pushed for a ban, lost funding |
| Sugar, 1960s | Sugar Research Foundation | Paid Harvard scientists to blame fat instead of sugar for heart disease (revealed in *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2016) |
| Asbestos, 1930s onward | Johns-Manville | Knew it caused lung disease and kept selling it |
| Climate, 1970s to 1980s | Exxon | Its scientists predicted warming accurately; the company then funded doubt about climate science |
| OxyContin, 1996 onward | Purdue Pharma and paid doctors | Marketed and prescribed it as rarely addictive |
| 2008 financial crisis | Rating agencies paid by the bond issuers | Rated junk mortgage bonds AAA |
| Slavery | Southern politicians and clergy | Called it a "positive good" (John C. Calhoun, 1837) |

In none of these were the people unintelligent. The income gave them a reason not to look.

---

### Weighing a claim by the claimant's stake

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Before trusting an opinion, ask what the person gains from holding it, and discount it in proportion to that gain.</mark>

A stake does not make a claim false. It means the claim needs evidence from someone without the stake. [[AI CEOs have an incentive to overstate job replacement|AI CEOs forecasting job replacement]] is a current example.

#### Turning the test on yourself

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">The bias is invisible from the inside, so the person least able to spot it in you is you.</mark>

Ask which of your own beliefs your job, your status, or your past choices would make costly to drop. Those are the beliefs to test hardest, ideally through [[Self-distancing through third-person reflection restores wise reasoning about your own problems|self-distancing]] or [[Al-Ghazali gives four ways to see your own faults because the nafs hides them from itself|an outsider who has no stake]].
