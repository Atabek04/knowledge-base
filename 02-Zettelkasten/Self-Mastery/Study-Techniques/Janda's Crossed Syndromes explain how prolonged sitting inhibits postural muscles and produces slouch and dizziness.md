When a muscle stays shortened for a long time, the nervous system suppresses the muscle on the opposite side. This is called reciprocal inhibition. Vladimir Janda identified two predictable deactivation patterns that appear in sedentary populations — Upper and Lower Crossed Syndromes.

### Why it matters

The slouch and dizziness a desk worker feels are not laziness or tiredness. They are the result of specific muscles being neurologically switched off. Fixing posture by "sitting up straight" is fighting the symptom. The inhibited muscles must be reactivated.

---

### How it works

**Lower Crossed Syndrome** — sitting all day keeps the hip flexors shortened and overactive. The glutes and deep abdominals on the opposite side get inhibited. Result: anterior pelvic tilt and lower back compression.

**Upper Crossed Syndrome** — a forward-head screen posture shortens the chest and upper trapezius. The deep neck flexors and lower trapezius get inhibited. Result: forward head, rounded shoulders.

The dizziness component comes from degraded **proprioception**. The deep neck flexors contain dense proprioceptive receptors that tell the brain where the head is in space. When they are inhibited, the brain's spatial calibration degrades — producing the foggy, unsteady feeling.

---

### Research backing

Janda documented these inhibition patterns across sedentary patients in clinical research throughout the 1980s. He showed they appear consistently regardless of age and are directly linked to desk-based work postures. The patterns are now standard in physiotherapy and sports rehabilitation.

---

### How to apply it

The fix is activation before a work session — not stretching, but firing the inhibited muscles:

- **10 chin-tucks** (deep neck flexor activation) — pull chin straight back, hold 3 seconds
- **20 glute bridges** — lying on back, drive hips up, squeeze glutes at the top
- **Wall angels** — for lower trapezius and shoulder retraction

Do this before sitting down to work. It resets the inhibition pattern and holds for 60–90 minutes of subsequent desk work.

Salah already contains elements of this: ruku and sujood involve spinal extension, hip loading, and cervical repositioning — which partially interrupt both crossed syndromes passively throughout the day.
