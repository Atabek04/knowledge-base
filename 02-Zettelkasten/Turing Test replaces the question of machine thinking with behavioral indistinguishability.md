Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" opened with the question: "Can machines think?" — then immediately rejected it as too vague.

He replaced it with the Imitation Game: if a human interrogator cannot reliably distinguish a machine's responses from a human's, then demanding proof of "real thinking" is philosophically meaningless.

This is a form of behaviorism applied to intelligence — process doesn't matter, only output does.

The implication is radical: Turing argued that any system producing indistinguishable intelligent behavior deserves to be called intelligent, regardless of what's happening inside.

Critics (especially Searle) argue this conflates behavior with understanding. A system can produce the right output for entirely the wrong reasons — and the reasons are exactly what thinking requires.
