A grade can answer two different questions: "how do you compare with your classmates?" or "what can you do?" Which question a school asks decides what students do to earn the grade.

### Norm-referenced grading

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Norm-referenced grading, or grading on a curve, assigns grades by rank within the group, so a grade says how a student compares with classmates, not what they know.</mark> The *norm* is the group itself.

The **curve** is the bell-shaped normal distribution: few low scores, most in the middle, few high. The teacher fixes the shares in advance, for example the top 10% get an A.

A strong student with 85/100 in a strong class can get a C, while 60/100 in a weak class can earn an A.

#### Why it blocks cooperation

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Under a curve, a classmate's progress lowers your rank, so helping others costs you and learning becomes a competition.</mark>

It fits selection, where seats are limited: university admission by rank, competitive grants.

---

### Criterion-referenced grading

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Criterion-referenced grading compares each student with a fixed standard set before the test, so every student who meets it gets the grade, however many that is.</mark> The *criterion* is the standard. A driving test works this way.

If all 30 students score 95, all 30 get an A, and helping a classmate costs nothing.

#### Bloom's argument against the curve

Bloom (1968), in "Learning for Mastery", argued the normal curve describes chance outcomes. Teaching is purposeful, so if it works, most students should master the material and the scores should bunch near the top.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">A criterion grade is only as honest as its test: if students can pass without the skill, through cheating or an AI doing the task, everyone gets an A and the A means nothing.</mark>
