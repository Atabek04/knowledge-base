### The finding

Learner-generated diagrams produce better transfer than instructor-provided diagrams — but the advantage depends on *how* and *when* the learner generates them.

A 2025 meta-analysis (*Educational Psychology Review*, learner-generated vs. instructor-provided graphic organizers) confirmed the generation advantage with an important structural insight: students performed best when they <mark style="background: yellow">drew their own version *before* seeing the expert diagram</mark>, then used the provided diagram as corrective feedback rather than a template to copy.

---

### Why generation comes first

When a provided diagram appears first, students often experience an **illusion of understanding**. Processing an expert's well-organized diagram feels fluent. That fluency creates false confidence — the diagram has done all the organizational work, so the learner's schema formation is shallow.

When the learner generates first:
- Gaps in understanding become visible in the sketch itself
- Working memory must construct a model, not passively receive one
- Errors in the self-generated diagram become *productive* — the mismatch with the correct diagram is precisely where learning happens

This is a specific instance of the generation effect: producing an answer (even an incorrect one) before receiving the correct answer strengthens encoding of the correct answer more than receiving the correct answer from the start.

---

### The quality caveat

Self-generation backfires without feedback. Learners who generate without ever seeing a correct version consolidate wrong mental models. The sequence matters:

1. **Generate** — attempt the diagram from memory or reading
2. **Compare** — receive the expert or correct version
3. **Correct** — revise the self-generated diagram; the correction process is itself encoding

Drawing quality during step 1 is irrelevant. An inaccurate sketch that generates strong error-correction in step 3 outperforms a beautiful copy of a provided diagram.

---

### Connection to worked examples and fading

Instructor-provided diagrams are the visual equivalent of fully worked examples — high support, low generation demand. Self-generated diagrams are the visual equivalent of open problem-solving — high generation demand, maximum schema formation.

[[Backward fading removes final worked example steps first to preserve context while building independent problem-solving skill|Backward fading]] applies here: begin with provided diagrams for brand-new material, gradually shift to partially-complete diagrams to fill in, then to full self-generation with feedback.
