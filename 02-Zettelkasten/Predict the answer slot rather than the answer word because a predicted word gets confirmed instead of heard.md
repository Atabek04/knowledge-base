Predicting the answer before the recording starts is one of the most widely taught IELTS Listening techniques, and unusually for this field, no expert source disputes it. The dispute is about *what* you predict — and getting that wrong turns the technique into a documented failure mode.

### What is safe to predict

The **slot**: everything about the answer that the question paper already constrains.

- The kind of information — a name, a date, a phone number, a percentage, a price, a place
- The word class — noun, verb, adjective
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Singular or plural, which the stem's own grammar usually settles before you hear anything</mark>
- The rough semantic field

A `$` printed in the gap means listen for money. "The ______ are stored in" forces a plural. Neither of these is a guess about content; both are read straight off the page, and they narrow what you are listening for without committing you to anything.

This is also the cheapest defence against the plural error, which costs a full mark on a word you heard perfectly well — see [[An over-limit Listening answer scores zero even when the content heard was correct|the mechanical zero]].

### What is not safe to predict

The **word**.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">A predicted word does not stay a hypothesis. It becomes the thing you listen for, and then the thing you hear.</mark>

Field's think-aloud protocols recorded candidates "assisted or misled by their knowledge of the topic" and by "their intuition as to what might be a current angle on the topic". The same study found people constructing elaborate interpretations from a single word they had misrecognised — a candidate who heard *breed* for *beach* understood an entire lecture as being about shark propagation.

Prediction at the word level supplies exactly the raw material for that: an expectation strong enough to be confirmed by an ambiguous sound.

#### The distinction stated once

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">A prediction is a filter for what to attend to. It is not a hypothesis to be confirmed.</mark>

The moment you catch yourself pleased that the audio said what you expected, you have stopped listening and started matching — the same collapse described in [[Keyword training backfires when attention shifts from the audio to the question paper|the attention-switching note]].

### Where nobody draws this line

IELTS Jacky comes closest, capping prediction at "type" rather than word and noting that the actual word is only occasionally predictable. IDP endorses word-class prediction for Part 4 note completion.

But <mark style="background: #FF9E9EA6;">no teacher source explicitly warns that content prediction produces confirmation bias — that objection exists only in the research, aimed at a degenerate form of a technique the same sources recommend.</mark>

#### Not every kind of prediction stays valuable

Vandergrift and Tafaghodtari's training study, in which prediction is one of four metacognitive processes taught, found the benefit **concentrated in the less-skilled listeners**.

<mark style="background: #FF9E9EA6;">That is a real limit on this technique: generic prediction training is close to exhausted once comprehension is strong, and the surviving value is narrower than the blogs suggest.</mark>

What survives at a high level is the part that is not a listening aid at all — <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">grammatical prediction is an error-prevention device, and it keeps paying regardless of how well you hear.</mark> Predicting the semantic field pays least and risks most, because a strong prior is exactly what makes you hear the expected word.

#### It changes shape in the later parts

In Parts 1 and 2 the slot is usually concrete: a number, a name, a time. In Parts 3 and 4 you are more often predicting the *direction* of an opinion or an argument than the category of a word.

That is a weaker, looser form of prediction, and it should be held more loosely still — Part 3 in particular punishes an early commitment, because [[Part 3 matching often tests what the speakers jointly settle on rather than what one speaker said|the first opinion voiced is usually the distractor]].
