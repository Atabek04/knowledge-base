### What it is

Metacognition (John Flavell, 1979) is "thinking about your own thinking" — the capacity to monitor, evaluate, and regulate your own cognitive processes during learning.

<mark style="background: yellow">The self-directed learner's core metacognitive question: "Am I actually learning, or am I just feeling productive?"</mark> These are not the same state.

---

### Two components (Flavell)

**Component 1 — Metacognitive Knowledge**
What you know *about* learning, about yourself as a learner, and about specific tasks and strategies.

- *Person knowledge:* your strengths, weaknesses, preferred conditions, prior knowledge in a domain
- *Task knowledge:* understanding that some tasks are harder than others; recall is harder than recognition
- *Strategy knowledge:* knowing which study methods work and when (e.g., retrieval practice beats re-reading)

**Component 2 — Metacognitive Regulation**
Active control of your own cognitive processes. Three phases:

- *Planning:* selecting strategies, allocating time, setting goals before studying
- *Monitoring:* checking comprehension during study — "Do I actually understand this or just recognize it?"
- *Evaluation:* assessing outcome after study — "Could I reproduce this? Where did I go wrong?"

---

### The Dunning-Kruger connection

Kruger & Dunning (1999): those at the 12th percentile of performance believed they were performing at the 62nd percentile.

The mechanism is metacognitive: the skills required to perform a task well are the *same* skills required to evaluate whether you performed it well.

<mark style="background: cyan">Critical finding: training in the skill simultaneously improved both performance and metacognitive accuracy.</mark> You break the Dunning-Kruger trap by practicing more deliberately and testing self-assessment against objective feedback — not by trying to think more clearly in the abstract.

### Practical strategies

| Strategy | How |
|---|---|
| **Pre-assessment** | Before studying a topic, write down what you think you know. Reveals existing gaps. |
| **Comprehension monitoring** | After reading a section, close it. "Can I reproduce the core idea? What would I explain poorly?" |
| **Exam wrappers** | After a mock interview or LeetCode contest: which problems did I fail, why did I think I knew them, what was the actual gap? |
| **Confidence calibration** | Rate confidence (1–5) on each flashcard before revealing the answer. Track whether confidence predicts accuracy. |
| **Study journal** | Weekly: what did I learn, what confused me, is my current strategy working? |
| **Avoid familiarity bias** | Never test comprehension immediately after reading. Wait 30+ minutes; immediate re-reading is corrupted by short-term memory. |

### Connection to other frameworks

Metacognition is the meta-layer that governs [[Retrieval practice strengthens memory by recalling information rather than re-reading it|retrieval practice]], [[Interleaving mixes problem types during practice to build pattern recognition over in-session fluency|interleaving]], and [[Deliberate practice builds expert mental representations through focused effort at the edge of current ability|deliberate practice]].

Without metacognitive monitoring, you can apply all three techniques in a comfortable, low-growth way and not notice.

[[Desirable difficulties make learning harder in ways that improve long-term retention over short-term fluency|Desirable difficulties]] require metacognitive training to trust — students must consciously understand why difficulty is a learning signal, or they will abandon difficult techniques for comfortable ones.
