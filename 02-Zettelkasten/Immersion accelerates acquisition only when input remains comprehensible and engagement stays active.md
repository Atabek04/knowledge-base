### What immersion does well

Language immersion — sustained daily exposure to a target language rather than formal study — provides:

- Massive comprehensible input if the learner can function in the environment
- Constant pragmatic pressure to communicate → output generation
- Naturalistic interaction → negotiation of meaning (see [[Interactional Hypothesis explains why negotiated conversation accelerates acquisition beyond passive exposure|Interactional Hypothesis]])
- Cultural and contextual embedding of vocabulary that classroom study cannot replicate

---

### Where immersion fails — Swain's evidence

Swain's French immersion studies (1985) showed that years of content-based immersion instruction produced learners with excellent comprehension but persistent grammatical errors in production.

Without *focused attention* to specific forms, certain grammatical features are never acquired — learners fossilize at a functional but inaccurate plateau. Comprehensible input alone does not force precision.

**The key insight:** immersion maximizes *exposure quantity* but does not guarantee *noticing quality*. Features the learner consistently misses in input will not be acquired regardless of volume.

---

### What research says about the optimal balance

No controlled study demonstrates pure immersion superiority over structured study + high-input exposure for *adult* learners. Adults benefit from three components:

1. **Explicit instruction** for irregular forms and grammar rules (faster than discovering from input alone)
2. **Massive comprehensible input** (immersion-style) for vocabulary and pragmatics
3. **Output practice with feedback** for production accuracy

**Krashen's counterpoint:**
Krashen argued that explicit grammar learning becomes "monitored" competence (slow, deliberate) — not "acquired" competence (automatic, fast). Explicit learning and implicit acquisition are distinct systems; the first does not convert into the second. This remains an open debate in SLA.

---

### Practical heuristic

<mark style="background: cyan">Use structured study for *what* to notice; use immersion-style input for *how much* exposure.</mark>

Explicit study without massive input is slow — you learn about the language without acquiring it.
Massive input without occasional explicit attention misses fossilizable features — you plateau at functional inaccuracy.

The two approaches are complementary, not competing.
