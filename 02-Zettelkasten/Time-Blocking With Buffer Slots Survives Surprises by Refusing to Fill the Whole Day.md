Most time-blocking fails not because the concept is wrong, but because people fill 100% of the day with scheduled work — leaving no room for the inevitable.

Cal Newport's version of time-blocking builds in **buffer blocks**: short, empty slots (typically one mid-morning and one mid-afternoon) that act as shock absorbers.

When an urgent request appears, it lands in a buffer block — not on top of an anchor block.

---

### Why Full Calendars Break First

A calendar at 100% capacity is a fragile system.

One unexpected meeting, one support request, one distraction — and the entire structure collapses.

When the day breaks, most people abandon the plan entirely rather than rebuild it, because rebuilding feels like admitting failure.

---

### Buffer Blocks Are Not Wasted Time

A buffer you don't use becomes bonus time for overflow work or recovery.

A buffer you do use saves the rest of the day.

Either outcome is a win. The cost of holding 30-40% of the day empty is small; the cost of not holding it is total schedule collapse.

This is the same logic as leaving RAM headroom in a system — not because you plan to use it, but because you know spikes happen.

---

### How to Implement

- Divide the day into anchor blocks (deep work, meetings) and buffer blocks (empty slots)
- Schedule anchor blocks first, filling no more than 60-70% of available hours
- When a surprise hits, absorb it into the nearest buffer block
- If both buffers fill, Newport recommends redrawing the day's plan — not abandoning it, just revising it in writing

This habit of revising rather than scrapping connects to [[Daily Self-Accounting Closes the Gap Between Plans and Execution]]: the plan is a living document, not a contract.

---

### The Deeper Shift

Leaving space feels unproductive. That discomfort is the point.

The urge to fill every slot is the same cognitive trap as [[Planning feels like doing because the brain rewards anticipation with dopamine]] — a packed calendar signals productivity without guaranteeing it.

Buffer blocks force you to trust execution over the appearance of a full schedule.
