Not all breaks restore cognitive capacity. The type of activity during a break determines whether directed attention actually recovers.

This distinction comes from **Attention Restoration Theory (ART)** — Stephen and Rachel Kaplan, University of Michigan, 1989.

---

### Two Kinds of Attention

**Directed attention** — deliberate, effortful focus. Used for reading, coding, writing, solving problems. Fatigues with sustained use. This is what the [[Vigilance decrement causes sustained attention and accuracy to degrade within 20 minutes of monotonous focus|vigilance decrement]] depletes.

**Involuntary attention** — effortless, stimulus-driven engagement. Activated by inherently interesting or aesthetically rich stimuli: a tree, a view out the window, natural sounds, gentle movement. Does not fatigue in the same way.

ART proposes: recovery from directed attention fatigue requires **shifting to involuntary attention**. The directed attention system gets a rest while the brain stays gently engaged through involuntary channels.

---

### What Works During a Break

Activities that activate involuntary attention and allow directed attention to recover:

- **Walking** — especially outdoors or near greenery. Short 5-min walks reliably reduce fatigue markers.
- **Looking out a window** — passive visual engagement. Even urban windows work, but natural scenes (trees, sky, water) produce stronger restoration.
- **Light stretching or movement** — increases cerebral blood flow and reduces cortisol; see also [[Prolonged sitting without movement breaks reduces cerebral blood flow and degrades executive function]].
- **Breathing exercises or brief meditation** — deactivates the task-positive network and reduces stress hormones.
- **Casual, low-stakes conversation** — social connection without cognitive load.

---

### What Fails During a Break

Activities that keep directed attention engaged produce no restoration:

- **Social media / news** — high novelty, continuous stimulation, emotional triggers. The brain is working, not resting.
- **Checking email** — task-switching, decision-making, unresolved items create new open loops.
- **Short video content** — continuous rapid cuts prevent the mind from settling into involuntary engagement.
- **More reading** — same directed attention channel, no recovery.

These activities feel like rest because they're lower stakes than work. They are not rest. They are *different work*.

---

### Duration

**Micro-breaks (1–3 min):** reduce acute fatigue, lower stress markers, improve mood. Not sufficient for deep restoration.

**Short breaks (5–15 min):** effective for directed attention recovery if used correctly (see above). Sufficient between Pomodoro sessions.

**Long breaks (15–30 min):** needed after 4+ sessions of accumulated fatigue. Align with [[Scheduling by Energy Peaks Makes Interruptions Less Costly|ultradian troughs]].

A 2022 PMC meta-analysis on micro-breaks found significant positive effects on well-being and performance — but only when the break activity was genuinely restorative, not stimulating.

---

### The Restoration Paradox

The most effective break activities are often the ones people feel guilty about: sitting still, staring at nothing, going for a walk when there's "still so much to do."

The guilt is misplaced. These are not wasted minutes. They are the mechanism by which the next focused hour becomes possible.
