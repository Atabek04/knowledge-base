Ego depletion is the idea that willpower is a finite resource that drains with use and must be replenished through rest. Roy Baumeister's 1998 study established this as a major finding in psychology.

The study design: participants who first resisted eating cookies (requiring willpower) gave up faster on a later puzzle than those who hadn't been depleted. Willpower looked like a muscle — exhaustible.

---

### The Replication Problem

Large preregistered replication attempts failed to reproduce the effect.

A multi-lab study involving hundreds of participants found no significant main effect of ego depletion. People who had performed self-control tasks did not show measurably worse performance on subsequent tasks. The foundational finding did not hold up under rigorous scrutiny.

This doesn't mean self-control doesn't fluctuate. It means the original model — willpower as a glucose-based reservoir that depletes like a battery — is probably wrong or dramatically oversimplified.

---

### Dweck's Alternative: Willpower as Belief

Carol Dweck, Veronika Job, and Gregory Walton proposed a different model in 2010: ego depletion only affects people who **believe willpower is limited**.

Their finding: people who held a "nonlimited" theory of willpower — who believed that mental effort does not deplete their resources — showed no ego depletion effect. People who believed willpower was limited did show it.

The implication: what you believe about willpower shapes how much of it you have.

Note: subsequent preregistered replications of Dweck's moderation effect have also returned mixed results. The science is genuinely unsettled. But the directional finding — that belief about willpower predicts performance — has been robust enough to influence practical guidance.

---

### What This Means in Practice

The practical upshot cuts in two directions:

1. Treating willpower as unlimited produces better outcomes than treating it as depletable. Believing you have more may give you more.
2. Designing systems that don't rely on willpower is still wise — not because willpower is guaranteed to deplete, but because removing the requirement entirely is more reliable than any belief-based upgrade.

The muscle metaphor was probably always a metaphor. The real constraint on self-control may be more motivational and attentional than metabolic.
