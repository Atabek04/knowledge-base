Most evidence about AI and skill comes from learners: people who never build a skill because the tool did the work while they were acquiring it.

A 2025 medical study showed the second, more uncomfortable harm: experts losing a skill they had already mastered.

---

### Formation versus erosion

The two harms look alike but work on different people.

- **Blocked formation**: a learner never acquires the skill. The [[AI-assisted learners score about two letter grades lower with debugging the most degraded skill|Anthropic coding trial]] measured this.
- **Erosion**: an expert who had the skill loses some of it.

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Deskilling is the loss of a skill a person already had, caused by a tool regularly doing that task for them.</mark>

---

### The colonoscopy study

Romańczyk and colleagues (Lancet Gastroenterology and Hepatology, 2025) followed experienced endoscopists at four centres in Poland after an AI system that highlights polyps on screen was introduced.

They tracked the adenoma detection rate (the share of colonoscopies in which the doctor finds at least one precancerous growth), a standard quality measure for this procedure.

In the three months before the AI arrived, the doctors' detection rate was about 28%. After three months of routine AI use, their rate on colonoscopies done *without* the AI had fallen to about 22%.

---

### Why an expert loses ground

The AI flags suspicious tissue, so the doctor's own visual search stops being the thing that finds polyps.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">When a tool reliably does the searching, the expert stops practising the search, and the unpractised skill decays even in someone with thousands of procedures behind them.</mark>

It is the omission side of [[Automation bias makes people accept a machine's recommendation without verifying it|automation bias]] turned into a habit: if the machine did not flag it, nobody looked.

---

### Limits of the finding

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">The study is observational, comparing before and after rather than randomizing doctors, so other changes over the same months could explain part of the drop.</mark>

The authors still call it the first documented case of AI deskilling clinicians on a patient-relevant task.
