Photolithography is the manufacturing process that creates integrated circuits by using light to transfer precise patterns onto silicon wafers, enabling production of billions of transistors at microscopic scales.

The photolithography process:

1. Substrate preparation: silicon wafer is cleaned and coated with a photoresist (light-sensitive material)

2. Mask exposure: a photomask (template with transparent and opaque regions) is placed over the wafer, and ultraviolet light is shone through it

3. Development: the wafer is treated with chemicals that dissolve either exposed or unexposed photoresist (depending on resist type), creating a pattern on the wafer surface

4. Etching: the wafer is exposed to chemicals or plasma that remove material not protected by photoresist

5. Resist removal: remaining photoresist is stripped away, leaving etched patterns in silicon

6. Repeat: layers are deposited and patterned repeatedly to build the 3D structure of a chip

Multiple layers:
- Metal: conducts signals between transistors
- Oxide: insulates metal layers
- Doped silicon: forms transistors and junctions

Resolution challenge:
- Wavelength of light used in photolithography limits minimum feature size
- 1970s: 10 micrometers could be reliably produced
- 2024: 3-5 nanometers (5nm means transistor gate length ~5nm)
- 2025+: 2 nanometers being introduced

Why light wavelength matters:
- Diffraction: light spreads around obstacles smaller than its wavelength
- You cannot create features smaller than approximately half the wavelength
- UV light: 365nm wavelength → roughly 180nm minimum features
- Deep ultraviolet (DUV): 193nm wavelength → roughly 100nm minimum features
- Extreme ultraviolet (EUV): 13.5nm wavelength → roughly 7nm minimum features

EUV manufacturing challenges:
- EUV light is absorbed by nearly everything (even air)
- Masks must be in vacuum
- Extremely expensive (EUV lithography machines cost $150+ million each)
- Few manufacturers capable of producing 3nm and smaller chips

Cost scaling:
- Photolithography equipment cost increases exponentially at smaller feature sizes
- Each new generation requires new equipment investment (billions of dollars)
- This creates economic incentive to consolidate manufacturing (only TSMC, Samsung, Intel can afford it)

The end of classical photolithography:
- Below ~2 nanometers, quantum effects and manufacturing cost constraints limit further miniaturization
- Future approaches: stacking (3D transistor arrangements), new materials, new manufacturing paradigms

Photolithography is ultimately the limiting factor in Moore's Law—as features approach atomic scales, maintaining yield (percentage of working chips) and reducing cost per transistor becomes exponentially harder.
