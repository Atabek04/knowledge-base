### What shadowing is

Shadowing is a technique where the learner simultaneously listens to and repeats native-speaker audio, staying approximately 0.5–1 second behind the speaker's voice — not waiting for sentences to end, but tracking in real time.

Described by Alexander Arguelles (comparative linguistics researcher); popularized in Japanese self-study communities (Khatzumoto/AJATT) for its effects on prosody and listening comprehension speed.

**What "shadow" means in practice:**
Audio plays → learner immediately vocalizes what they hear, overlapping with the audio. The learner is not translating; they are producing output based purely on phonological input at native speed.

---

### What it trains

1. **Prosody** — rhythm, stress, intonation patterns that distinguish natural speech from textbook speech
2. **Listening parsing speed** — forces processing at native rate; you cannot slow down the speaker
3. **Phonological loop engagement** — active vocalization consolidates phonological representations more than passive listening
4. **Articulatory automaticity** — repeated shadowing of the same audio builds fluent motor patterns for speech production

Shadowing is *not* primarily a vocabulary tool — it does not encode meaning. Words encountered in shadowing sessions need separate meaning acquisition through input comprehension or deliberate study.

---

### Evidence base

Shadowing is under-researched in formal SLA literature compared to other techniques. The strongest evidence comes from Japanese EFL contexts (Hamada, 2014, 2016) showing improvements in listening comprehension scores and pronunciation accuracy.

The mechanism overlaps with **imitation learning** in motor-speech research: the articulatory-phonological loop that mirrors heard speech is the same neural system used for production. Shadowing forces this loop into deliberate, conscious operation.

---

### When to use it

- After achieving basic comprehensibility (<mark style="background: pink">B1+</mark>) — below that, insufficient prior phonological knowledge makes tracking at native speed cognitively overwhelming
- With *familiar* material (audio you've already comprehended) — shadowing unfamiliar content at B2 defeats the prosody-focus purpose
- As a complement to [[Comprehensible input at i+1 requires graded exposure sequenced by difficulty to drive acquisition|comprehensible input]]: shadow familiar material, use comprehensible input for new vocabulary and grammar
- Specifically targeted at accent reduction, spontaneous fluency, and listening speed

---

### Practical workflow

1. Find an audio segment you understand (podcast, audiobook, TV episode) — 30–60 seconds
2. Listen once without shadowing to confirm comprehension
3. Shadow 3–5 repetitions of the same segment
4. Move to new material only when the segment feels automatic
