Binary representation uses only two distinct symbols—0 and 1—to encode all information processed by computers.

This contrasts with the decimal (base-10) system humans naturally use, which requires 10 distinct symbols (0-9). Binary is base-2, requiring only two symbols.

In binary:
- Each digit position represents a power of 2
- The rightmost digit is the "ones" place (2^0 = 1)
- The next digit left is the "twos" place (2^1 = 2)
- Then the "fours" place (2^2 = 4), and so on
- The decimal number 5 is represented as 101 in binary: (1×4) + (0×2) + (1×1) = 5

Binary is fundamentally suited to electronic computers because:
- Electronic switches naturally have two stable states: on/off, high voltage/low voltage, magnetized/demagnetized
- Mapping these two physical states to binary digits (0 and 1) is straightforward and robust
- Two-state systems are more reliable than multi-state systems in noisy electrical environments

Alternative number bases were considered but rejected for practical reasons:
- Ternary (base-3) would require three distinct voltage levels, making circuits sensitive to noise
- Decimal required complex circuits to represent 10 distinct states reliably
- Binary's simplicity meant fewer errors and simpler circuit design

The choice to use binary is not arbitrary—it emerges from the physics of electronic components and the engineering requirement for reliable, noise-resistant signal representation.
