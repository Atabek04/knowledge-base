### The leverage point

Knowing Latin/Greek roots, prefixes, and suffixes turns isolated word learning into a leveraged strategy: understanding one root (*port* = carry) makes *transport, import, export, portable, porter, deportation* immediately decodable — six words for the price of one morpheme.

Nation (2001) estimates English has ~14,000 word families. A learner who knows the ~100 most productive Greek and Latin morphemes can decode or approximate the meaning of thousands of academic and technical words without explicit study.

---

### How it differs from keyword mnemonics

[[Keyword mnemonic links a foreign word to a vivid mental image via a sound-alike bridge|Keyword mnemonics]] are a *temporary encoding bridge* — used to get a word into memory, then the bridge fades.

Morphological awareness is a *permanent decoding tool* — it doesn't just help remember a word; it helps *infer the meaning of words never seen before*.

The analogy for a backend engineer: keyword mnemonics are caching a lookup result; morphological knowledge is owning the hash function.

### High-value morphemes (partial list)

| Root/Affix | Meaning | Example words |
|---|---|---|
| *chron* | time | chronology, synchronize, anachronism |
| *bio* | life | biology, biome, symbiosis |
| *graph* | write/record | autograph, paragraph, photograph |
| *port* | carry | import, transport, portable, deportation |
| *spec/spect* | see | inspect, spectator, perspective |
| *mit/miss* | send | transmit, permission, dismiss |
| *vert/vers* | turn | convert, reverse, subvert |
| *scrib/script* | write | describe, manuscript, prescribe |

### Application for instruction

- Teach roots explicitly as their own vocabulary items — they compound across the entire lexicon
- When introducing new technical vocabulary, surface the etymology: *"synchronous comes from syn- (together) + chronos (time) — same time"*
- For advanced learners: reduces dictionary dependency and builds confidence with academic and professional vocabulary
- <mark style="background: cyan">Particularly high ROI for academic and technical English</mark> — the Academic Word List (Coxhead, 2000) has heavy Latin/Greek morphology

---

### Caveat

<mark style="background: pink">False cognates and semantic drift make etymology unreliable as the sole decoding strategy.</mark>

*Terrible* and *terrific* share *terr-* (frighten) but diverged in meaning. *Awful* originally meant "full of awe" (positive); *nice* originally meant "ignorant." Use morphological roots as a first hypothesis, then verify in context.

The tool is powerful for *new word inference*, not as a substitute for confirming meaning through context.
