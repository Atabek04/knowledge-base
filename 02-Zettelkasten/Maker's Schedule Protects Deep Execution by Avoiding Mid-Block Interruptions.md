Paul Graham's 2009 essay identifies two incompatible ways of organizing time.

**Manager's schedule**: the day is divided into one-hour slots. Fitting in a meeting just means finding an open slot. Interruptions are a logistics problem, not a productivity problem.

**Maker's schedule**: programmers, writers, and creators need time in units of half a day at minimum. Creative and technical work requires long uninterrupted runs to load context, reach depth, and produce anything meaningful.

### Why a Single Meeting Destroys a Maker's Day

If a maker has a meeting at 2pm, the afternoon is effectively gone.

The two-hour block before the meeting is too short to get deep into anything — the approaching meeting occupies the background. The two-hour block after is a cold start, not a continuation.

One meeting creates two fragments, neither large enough for real work. The maker gets a full afternoon on the calendar but produces nothing equivalent to a full afternoon of output.

---

### The Asymmetric Cost

Managers experience meetings as cheap: a one-hour slot among other one-hour slots. Makers experience the same meeting as expensive: a half-day of output destroyed.

This mismatch explains a common organizational failure. Managers schedule meetings freely without understanding the cost. Makers accept them reluctantly but rarely say no, because the cost is invisible in calendar terms.

### Defending the Maker's Schedule

Graham's prescription is to batch all meetings into one end of the day — mornings or afternoons — and keep the other half completely clear.

This is the structural form of what [[Deep Work Requires Blocking Time Before Reactive Work Fills the Day]] describes as time-blocking. Deep work needs not just a block, but a block protected from fragmentation.

[[Killing the Decision Removes Willpower from the Execution Equation]] applies here too: negotiating meeting times in the moment, one by one, exhausts decision energy. A standing rule ("no meetings before noon") removes the decision entirely.
