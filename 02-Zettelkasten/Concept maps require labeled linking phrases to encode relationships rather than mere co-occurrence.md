### What makes a concept map distinct

A <mark style="background: yellow">concept map</mark> (Novak, Cornell University, 1972 — based on Ausubel's assimilation theory) is a **directed network where every edge carries a labeled linking phrase** that forms a readable proposition with its two nodes.

Example proposition: `photosynthesis` → *produces* → `oxygen`

This is not decoration. The linking phrase is the entire cognitive point — it forces the learner to articulate *how* two concepts relate, not just that they do.

Without labeled edges, a diagram is a **mind map**: a radial tree where all connections implicitly mean "is associated with." Mind maps are fast to make and useful for brainstorming but encode no relational structure.

---

### The five structural requirements of a good concept map

Novak & Cañas (2008) specify:

1. **Focus question** — the map answers a specific question; scopes what belongs
2. **Hierarchical layout** — most general concept at top, progressively specific below
3. **Labeled propositions** — every edge must carry a linking phrase
4. **Cross-links** — connections between different branches are the highest-value elements; they show that the learner sees relationships across the whole structure, not just within each branch
5. **Parking lot** — concepts awaiting placement live here, preventing premature closure

A map optimized for learning is not maximally dense. More nodes ≠ better learning. The cross-links between branches signal integrative understanding that linear outlines and mind maps cannot represent.

### Evidence for concept map construction vs. passive study

Nesbit & Adesope (2006) meta-analysis of 55 studies (5,818 participants, Grade 4 to postsecondary): concept map **construction** consistently produced better outcomes than merely studying provided maps, and outperformed passive study and re-reading across science, psychology, statistics, and nursing.

A 2024 STEM meta-analysis (37 studies): overall effect size ES = 0.63 vs. control. Physics/earth science showed the strongest effect (ES = 1.04).

### Concept maps vs. mind maps: when to use each

| | Concept Map | Mind Map |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Understand and represent relationships | Generate ideas, organize loosely |
| **Edge labels** | Required — propositional phrases | Absent |
| **Structure** | Directed network with cross-links | Radial tree, single root |
| **Best for** | Organized review, knowledge integration, assessment | Brainstorming, early exploration |
| **Limitation** | Requires prior knowledge to generate accurate links | Cannot represent causality or direction |

Eppler (2006): concept maps and mind maps are **complementary tools** — neither is universally superior. Content type and goal determine the fit.

---

### When concept maps fail

Concept mapping backfires when learners lack sufficient prior knowledge — they cannot generate accurate linking phrases and may encode misconceptions with visual authority. The tool is most powerful for **organizing and integrating known material**, not for encountering brand-new content from scratch.

Krieglstein et al. (2022): low structural salience (unclear spatial layout) increases disorientation (medium effect, ηp² = .07), which then elevates extraneous cognitive load (β = 0.80, p < .001). A badly structured concept map actively hurts learning.
