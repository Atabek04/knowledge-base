The official assessment guide states the same measure three times, once for each error type it lists: the criteria assess <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">the density and communicative effect of errors</mark> — in spelling, in word formation, and in grammar.

Density, not total. A long essay with eight errors and a short one with five are not ranked by the raw number.

Communicative effect, not error class. An error that leaves the meaning intact is cheaper than one that makes the reader re-read, whatever the grammar textbook says about their relative severity.

Two consequences that cut against normal proofreading instinct:

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Repeating one error type across an essay is worse than making several different one-off slips</mark> — a repeated error has high density in its category and reads as an absent rule rather than a lapse.

<mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">Trimming a weak sentence you cannot fix is a legitimate move.</mark> Removing the sentence lowers the density and costs nothing elsewhere, provided the idea it carried survives somewhere.

Source: [ielts.org — IELTS Writing Key Assessment Criteria (PDF), p.4](https://ielts.org/cdn/Guides/ielts-writing-key-assessment-criteria.pdf)
