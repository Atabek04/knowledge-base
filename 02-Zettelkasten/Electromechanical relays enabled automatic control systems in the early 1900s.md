The electromagnetic relay, invented in the 1830s for long-distance telegraph signals, became the foundation for electromechanical computing devices in the early 1900s.

A relay is a switch controlled by an electromagnet: when current flows through a coil, it creates a magnetic field that pulls a physical switch closed, allowing current to flow in a separate circuit. When current stops, a spring returns the switch to open position.

This switching behavior could represent binary states (open=0, closed=1) and could be connected in networks to perform logical operations. A relay can be thought of as the first electronic logic gate.

In 1920s-1940s, engineers built large relay-based machines for telephony switching and tabulation. IBM's Mark I (completed 1944) used over 700 miles of wiring and 3,500 electromechanical relays to perform mathematical calculations. It weighed 30 tons and required constant maintenance because relays frequently failed.

Relays were significant improvements over purely mechanical devices because:
- Electricity controlled the switching, allowing rapid operation (milliseconds vs. seconds)
- Relay networks could implement complex logic sequences
- Distance between components no longer mattered (wired together, not geared together)

However, relays had fatal limitations: they wore out quickly, consumed substantial power, and were physically large. Their speed (~100 operations per second) was insufficient for the computing demands that emerged after World War II.

The need to replace relays with faster, more reliable switches led directly to the vacuum tube revolution.
