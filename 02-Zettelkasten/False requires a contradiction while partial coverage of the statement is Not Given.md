The decision rule is three-way but only two of the branches are symmetrical. True means the passage expresses the same idea as the statement. False means the passage <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">contradicts</mark> it. Not Given means the information needed to decide is absent.

The single most useful discriminator at this level: <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">if part of the statement is supported and the rest is simply absent, that is Not Given, not False</mark>. Absence is not opposition.

The commonest ways candidates convert a Not Given into a False:

Over-inference from a nearby sentence — the passage says comedians draw on situations, the statement says *personal* situations, and nothing tells you whether they were personal.

Real-world knowledge. The judgement is against the passage only.

Missed qualifiers — *all*, *some*, *most*, *may*, *always* change the truth value of the statement.

Keyword overlap. Shared words are not shared meaning, and they are placed deliberately.

<mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">The default when uncertain is Not Given</mark>, because the alternative is spending minutes hunting for something that was never there. Note that Yes/No/Not Given questions — which test the writer's views rather than information — use the same technique but different answer words, and writing TRUE where YES is required is marked wrong.

Sources differ on one point: some allow False for "a different idea", others restrict it to direct contradiction. The strict reading is safer.
