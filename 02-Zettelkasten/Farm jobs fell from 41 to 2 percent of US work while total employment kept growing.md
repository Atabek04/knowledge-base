Agriculture is the largest automation in history measured by jobs. It shows what happens when a technology displaces most of an economy's workforce over a century.

### Glossary

- **Agriculture**: farming, growing crops and raising animals. From Latin *ager*, "field".
- **Workforce**: everyone working or looking for work in a country.
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Income elasticity of demand</b>: how much spending on a good changes as people's incomes rise.</mark> Food has low elasticity: richer people do not eat proportionally more. Health care has high elasticity.

---

### The size of the shift

In 1900, 41% of the US workforce worked in agriculture; by 2000, only 2% did, mostly because of automated machinery and other farm technology ([Autor, "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs?", JEP 2015](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Why%20Are%20there%20Still%20So%20Many%20Jobs_0.pdf)).

Those workers did not become permanently unemployed. Over the same century the US workforce grew, and employment moved into manufacturing, services, health care and education.

---

### Where the displaced workers went

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Cheaper food left households with income to spend on other goods, and that new spending created demand for workers in other sectors.</mark>

Food demand has low income elasticity, the pattern called [[As incomes rise the share spent on food falls, so spending shifts toward housing, services and new goods|Engel's law]]. US households spent 42.5% of their budget on food in 1901 and 13.1% in 2002–03 (BLS). The money freed up went elsewhere, and each new spending category needed workers (Autor 2015):

- <b>Manufactured goods</b>: cars, refrigerators, washing machines, radios; factory jobs, then sales and repair jobs.
- <b>Housing</b>: bigger homes with electricity and plumbing; construction workers, electricians, plumbers.
- <b>Health care</b>: doctors, nurses, hospital staff, pharmacists; its spending share keeps growing as incomes rise.
- <b>Education</b>: families could afford to keep children in school longer; teachers, then universities.
- <b>Leisure and travel</b>: restaurants, hotels, cinemas, tourism.

#### A chain reaction: horses to motels

As cars displaced horse travel in the 1920s, blacksmiths and stable hands lost work. Meanwhile roadside motels and fast-food restaurants grew to serve drivers, alongside petrol stations, car mechanics and road builders (Autor 2015).

A displaced job and a created job are rarely the same job, in the same place, for the same person.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">The economy as a whole absorbed displaced farm workers over decades; this says nothing about how fast any one displaced worker found new work, or at what wage.</mark>
