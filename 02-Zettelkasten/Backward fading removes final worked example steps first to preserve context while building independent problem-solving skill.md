### The problem fading solves

Fully worked examples are optimal for novices. But presenting only examples never requires independent production, so no independent skill forms.

The naïve transition — show examples, then assign open problems — creates a sudden scaffold collapse: the support is removed all at once, and learners experience cognitive overload as if they had never seen examples.

<mark style="background: yellow">Backward fading</mark> bridges this gap by gradually withdrawing scaffold while preserving orientation.

---

### The technique

Renkl, Atkinson & colleagues (2002, 2004) established the correct fading direction:

1. **Fully worked example** — all steps shown and annotated
2. **Final step removed** — learner must supply only the last move
3. **Final two steps removed** — learner supplies the last two moves
4. **Open problem** — no scaffold

<mark style="background: cyan">Remove from the end, not the beginning.</mark> Removing early steps forces the learner to start without orientation — recreating the novice's means-ends overload from scratch. Removing final steps preserves the problem setup while requiring production of the concluding move, where pattern inference is most meaningful.

---

### Completion effect

Partially-worked examples (halfway through the solution) produce equivalent learning outcomes to fully worked examples with higher active engagement. This is the natural resting state of a well-designed fading sequence — learners spend most of the intermediate phase on completion problems, not full examples.

---

### Adaptive vs. fixed fading

Adaptive fading (paced to per-learner quiz performance) outperforms fixed-schedule fading. If a learner correctly supplies the final step, advance to removing two steps. If errors occur, return to fully worked. The schedule should track the learner, not the curriculum calendar.

---

### Application

For grammar instruction, procedural skill, algorithm explanation, or any domain where learners must produce an operation sequence:
- Begin every new pattern with 2–3 fully worked examples
- Follow with 2 backward-faded completion problems
- Reach open problems only after completion problems show consistent success
- Return to worked examples if error rate rises at a new difficulty level
