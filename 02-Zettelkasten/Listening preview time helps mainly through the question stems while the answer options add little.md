Before each part of the IELTS Listening test the recording pauses and you are given time to look at the questions. Every preparation source agrees you should use it. Almost none of them tells you what to read *first* when the time runs out before the questions do — which is the situation most candidates are actually in.

There is an experiment that answers this, and it points somewhere slightly different from the standard advice.

### The experiment

Yanagawa and Green (2008) compared four preview conditions on a multiple-choice listening test: full preview (stem plus options), **stem-only**, **options-only**, and no preview at all.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Full preview and stem-only preview performed about the same, and both beat options-only and no preview.</mark> The benefit of previewing came almost entirely from access to the question stems; the options added only a marginal amount on top.

That is the whole finding, and it converts directly into a priority order.

### What this means when time runs out

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Read the instructions and the word limit, then the stems. If the clock beats you, let it beat you on the options rather than on the stems.</mark>

The reasoning is that a stem tells you what kind of information to listen for and where you are in the recording. An option is only useful at the moment of choosing, which is after you have already heard the relevant passage — so a stem you failed to read costs you the answer, while an option you failed to read costs you some speed.

#### The options have a second window

The choice is not actually "read the options now or never". <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">For any question after the first two there is usually live audio before that question's content arrives — the speakers setting up the next topic — and that dead air is where the later options can be read.</mark>

So the habit is *read options late*, not *skip options*. Three short options in ten seconds of set-up is achievable; nine options before the recording starts is not.

The caveat from [[Keyword training backfires when attention shifts from the audio to the question paper|the attention-switching finding]] still applies — this is reading during audio, which is never free. It is affordable only because set-up talk carries no answers.

#### Two honest limits on this

The study was a general multiple-choice listening test, **not IELTS**. IELTS Part 3 options are unusually long and finely differentiated, which is exactly the property that makes surviving on stems alone hardest.

And the ordering is an inference from the research, not advice any expert source actually gives. <mark style="background: #FF9E9EA6;">No IELTS teacher recommends skipping the options — the research licenses a priority when time is short, not a rule to leave them unread.</mark>

Part 3 is where you should override it and pay for the options, because in Part 3 the options *are* the competing opinions and the question is which speaker held which — see [[Part 3 matching often tests what the speakers jointly settle on rather than what one speaker said|what Part 3 is really asking]].

### Why the teachers say otherwise

IELTS Liz, IELTS Advantage and IDP all instruct candidates to read the options and underline what differentiates them. That is not wrong — it is the correct move when time allows.

It is worth noticing, though, that <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">none of the teacher-authored preview protocols cites any evidence at all.</mark> They are experienced-practitioner heuristics, which is not nothing, and is also not the same thing as a finding.

The related trap is that reading more is not automatically better: attention spent on the question paper is attention taken off the audio, and that cost is measured — see [[Keyword training backfires when attention shifts from the audio to the question paper|the cost of reading while listening]].
