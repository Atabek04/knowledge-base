Richard Hamming's 1986 talk *You and Your Research* (Bell Communications Research) asks why most researchers with equal talent do work that nobody remembers. His answer is that they choose problems badly, and that the choice is a skill, not luck. The test he put to colleagues at lunch: what are the important problems in your field, and why are you not working on one of them?

---

### Two halves, and the one people forget

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">An important problem, in Hamming's sense, is one that matters and on which you have an attack: a plausible route to progress that you actually hold.</mark> Everyone remembers the first half. The second is what separates a career from a wish.

*Attack* is Hamming's word, and it is not a solution. Nobody begins with a solution. An **attack** is what makes an *attempt* possible: a theory that predicts which lever to pull, an instrument that can pull it, a method, a dataset or an access that others lack. Time travel matters and nobody holds an attack on it; that makes it big, not important. Hamming had a computer when most of Bell Labs did not; Shannon had a mathematical theory of information; each was an attack on problems everyone else could only describe.

---

### Measuring a problem versus intervening on it

The clearest sign of a missing attack is a literature that can measure a failure but not change it. A study that documents motivational decay in a gamified course holds a route to *observe* decay (a course, a questionnaire, a time series) and none to *intervene* on its cause. An attack on the same problem needs a theory of why the decay happens, which names the lever, and an instrument in which that lever can be pulled separately from the others. <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">A theory that says why the failure happens plus an instrument that can act on that cause is what turns a documented problem into an attackable one.</mark>

---

### Why the attack decides the question

The [[A gap is worth researching only if its answer settles a larger question the field already cares about|so what test]] asks whether the answer would matter; Hamming's test asks whether you can get to an answer at all. A question that passes the first and fails the second produces a thesis that matters and cannot be finished. <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Choose the question at the intersection: it must matter to the field and you must already hold the route in.</mark>
