The transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947 and refined throughout the 1950s, replaced vacuum tubes as the fundamental electronic switch in computers.

A transistor uses semiconductor materials (primarily silicon and germanium) to control current flow through a small doped crystal. Unlike vacuum tubes, transistors have no filament to heat, no vacuum to maintain, and no moving electrons in space.

There are two main types:
- Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT): uses a small base current to control larger collector-emitter current
- Field-Effect Transistor (FET): uses an electric field to control current flow through a channel

Transistors offered massive improvements:
- Size: millimeters vs. inches (vacuum tubes)
- Power consumption: milliwatts vs. watts per component
- Heat dissipation: dramatically reduced cooling requirements
- Reliability: no filaments burning out; solid-state means fewer failure modes
- Speed: microseconds to nanoseconds (faster than tubes)
- Lifetime: theoretical indefinite operation (in practice, decades)

The first transistorized computer, the TX-0, was built at MIT in 1956. It was vastly smaller and more reliable than ENIAC, though still room-sized by today's standards.

By 1960, transistors had almost completely displaced vacuum tubes in new computer designs. This transition fundamentally changed computing economics: computers became feasible for universities, corporations, and eventually individuals.

However, transistors still had to be wired together individually, making circuits complex and expensive to manufacture. The next breakthrough—the integrated circuit—would further revolutionize computing.
