IELTS states this without hedging: answers over the word limit are marked as incorrect. So is writing two answers when one was asked for — <mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">even if one of them is right, no mark is awarded</mark>.

These cost the same one mark each as a genuine comprehension failure, which makes them the cheapest marks in the exam to recover.

The rules that decide them:

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">All words count, articles and prepositions included.</mark> Under a ONE WORD rubric, *the bus* fails. The commonest cause is copying words that sat before or after the gap.

Hyphenated words count as one — *check-in* is a single word, officially.

"NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER" is asymmetrical: words plural, number singular. It permits two words, or one word plus a number, or a number alone.

Both UK and US spellings are accepted, but spelling and grammar errors do lose marks, and you should not rephrase what you heard.

Plurals have no official statement, but the strict teacher rule — *project* marked wrong where *projects* was required, and bracketed forms like *car(s)* not accepted — follows from the official grammar clause. <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">The grammar of the sentence stem is the intended disambiguator.</mark>
