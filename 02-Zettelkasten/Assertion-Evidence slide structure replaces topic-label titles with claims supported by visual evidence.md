### What it is

Assertion-Evidence Structure (Michael Alley, Penn State) fixes the two structural problems of conventional slides:

1. **Topic-label titles** ("Results", "Wind Energy") — name a subject, make no claim
2. **Bullet-list bodies** — list items without demonstrating relationships or reasoning

The fix:
- The **title becomes a complete sentence** stating the main message (the assertion)
- The **body becomes visual evidence** — a photograph, diagram, graph, or table that proves the assertion

---

### The research

2011 ASEE/IJEE study (N=110 engineering students):

- AES group: **59% comprehension**
- Conventional group: **42% comprehension** — a 17 percentage point gain, p < 0.01
- Misconceptions in conventional group were ~10× higher than AES group
- AES group reported lower perceived cognitive load
- Gains held at delayed post-test (retention, not just immediate recall)

Creating AES slides also deepened content understanding in the presenter — forcing the assertion makes the creator clarify their own thinking.

---

### Why it works cognitively

AES aligns directly with four of [[Mayer's Multimedia Learning Principles reduce cognitive load through twelve evidence-based design rules|Mayer's principles]]:

- **Coherence:** No filler bullets — only content that supports the assertion
- **Signaling:** The sentence title tells the audience what to look for in the visual
- **Multimedia:** Visuals replace text lists; verbal channel handled by the speaker
- **Redundancy avoided:** Speaker explains; slide shows evidence — they are not duplicates

---

### 5 rules for restructuring a deck

1. <mark style="background: yellow">Write the assertion first.</mark> "What is the one claim I am making here?" Write it as a complete sentence (8–12 words).
   - ~~"Solar Energy"~~ → "Solar panel costs have fallen 90% since 2010."
2. The assertion must be falsifiable — if it can't be proven wrong, it's a topic label, not an assertion
3. Replace the body with one piece of visual evidence — one graph, one diagram, one image, not three half-explained charts
4. The speaker carries the verbal load — slides without a live speaker need different design (handouts, not slides)
5. If a slide makes no claim, question whether it's needed

---

### Online vs offline

**Offline:** Sentence headline + one visual; speaker carries all explanation; slides are intentionally lean.

**Online (async video):** Add brief on-screen annotations to the visual — the speaker is not physically present to decode it; light redundancy is acceptable here only.
