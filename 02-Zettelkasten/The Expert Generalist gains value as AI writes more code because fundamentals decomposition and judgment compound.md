If the role is shifting from writing code to directing it, the natural question is *who thrives in that role*. The leading expert answer is the "Expert Generalist" — and the claim is that their value **rises** as AI writes more code, not falls.

This names the winner inside the broader shift where [[The software engineer's role is shifting from writing code to specifying reviewing and orchestrating it|the job moves from authoring code to orchestrating it]].

---

### What an Expert Generalist holds

Martin Fowler (July 2025) and Thoughtworks name three durable assets:

- <mark style="background: yellow">Core concepts and patterns</mark> of programming — fundamentals that transfer across any language or framework.
- A knack for <mark style="background: yellow">decomposing complex work into small, testable pieces</mark>.
- The ability to <mark style="background: yellow">collaborate</mark> — with other programmers *and* with the people the software is for.

#### Why "generalist," not "specialist"

The name resolves the breadth-vs-depth question: <mark style="background: cyan;">it's not shallow breadth, it's broad fundamentals plus the judgment to go deep on demand.</mark> AI handles the narrow, codified specialism; the human supplies the cross-cutting judgment AI lacks.

---

### Why the value compounds

These three assets are exactly what's needed to *define, review, and orchestrate* AI output well. The more code AI generates, the more leverage sits with whoever can decompose the problem, judge the result, and connect it to real needs.

<mark style="background: green">When everyone can produce mediocre code instantly, the scarce edge is judgment</mark> — the same reason [[AI agent building is the foundational career skill of the next thirty years|the human bar rises rather than falls]].

---

### The honest caveat

<mark style="background: pink">This is influential expert opinion (Fowler / Thoughtworks), not measured consensus.</mark> It's one well-argued camp, and it happens to align with what the skill studies imply — but it's a bet on where value goes, not a proven outcome.
