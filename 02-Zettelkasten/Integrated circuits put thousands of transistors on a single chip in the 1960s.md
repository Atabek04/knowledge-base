The integrated circuit (IC), independently invented by Jack Kilby (Texas Instruments) and Robert Noyce (Fairchild Semiconductor) in 1958-1960, combined multiple transistors and other components on a single semiconductor die.

Before ICs, transistors, resistors, and capacitors were soldered together individually—tedious, expensive, and prone to failure at connection points. This "tyranny of numbers" made complex circuits impractical.

An IC etches circuit patterns directly into silicon using photolithography. Multiple transistors, gates, and interconnections are created as a single unit, eliminating individual connections.

Early ICs (1960s) contained dozens to hundreds of transistors. Through the application of Moore's Law (the observation that transistor density doubles approximately every two years), IC complexity exploded:
- 1971: Intel 4004 microprocessor (2,300 transistors)
- 1978: Intel 8086 (29,000 transistors)
- 1989: Intel 386 (1 million transistors)
- 2024: Modern processors (50+ billion transistors)

This exponential growth fundamentally changed computing:
- Manufacturing cost per transistor dropped exponentially, making computers affordable
- Power consumption per operation decreased dramatically
- Reliability increased (fewer connections meant fewer failure points)
- Speed increased as components were physically closer together

ICs enabled the minicomputer revolution of the 1970s, the personal computer revolution of the 1980s, and eventually ubiquitous computing in smartphones and IoT devices.

Modern computers are essentially massively complex ICs—the fundamental switching components (transistors) are identical to 1960s designs, but their density and integration have advanced beyond recognition.
