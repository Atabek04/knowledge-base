University lectures from MIT and other top schools have been free online for two decades, yet a degree still opens doors that the same knowledge learned alone does not. Two theories explain what a degree is worth, and they predict different futures for free learning.

### Human capital: the degree as skill

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Human capital theory says education pays because it raises skill: graduates earn more because they can do more.</mark> If this were the whole story, the same skill learned from free videos would earn the same.

---

### Signaling: the degree as proof

Spence (1973), The Quarterly Journal of Economics, argued that an employer cannot see ability directly before hiring, so it looks for a costly sign of it.

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Signaling theory says a degree pays because it is hard to earn, so finishing one proves ability and persistence to an employer, whatever was learned along the way.</mark>

#### The sheepskin effect

Earnings jump in the year a diploma is completed, beyond what one more year of study would explain (Hungerford and Solon, 1987, The Review of Economics and Statistics). The diploma itself, the *sheepskin* it was once printed on, carries value.

#### Employers still screen on the degree

Large US firms publicly dropped degree requirements, yet fewer than 1 in 700 hires in 2023 actually changed (Sigelman, Fuller and Martin, 2024, Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School).

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Free knowledge raises human capital but sends no signal, because nobody can verify what a self-learner actually mastered.</mark>

---

### What a certificate can and cannot replace

In a hiring experiment, a MOOC certificate beat no credential by a wide margin but lost to every traditional degree (Rivas, Baker and Evans, 2020, AERA Open). In a field experiment with 800,000 certificate earners, sharing certificates on LinkedIn raised new employment by 6%, most for learners with weak prior job prospects (Athey and Palikot, 2024, arXiv preprint).

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">A certificate complements a degree but does not yet substitute for one; its value depends on whether employers trust the assessment behind it.</mark>

A signal is only as strong as its test, so a verified mastery exam carries more weight than a course-completion badge.
