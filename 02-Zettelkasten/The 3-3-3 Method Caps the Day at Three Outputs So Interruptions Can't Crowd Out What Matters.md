Oliver Burkeman, in *Four Thousand Weeks*, proposes a deliberately modest daily template:

- **3 hours** of deep, uninterrupted work on the most important project
- **3 shorter tasks** (urgent items, errands, quick deliverables)
- **3 maintenance activities** (health, relationships, admin)

The constraint is the point. By capping the day at nine named outputs, the method forces you to choose rather than accumulate.

---

### Why Caps Protect Against Interruptions

Uncapped task lists grow to absorb every interruption as a new item.

When your day is bounded — three things only — an urgent surprise is not added to an infinite list but **displaces one of the three shorter tasks**.

This makes the trade-off visible: "If I handle this urgent request, which of my three shorter tasks drops?"

That clarity is the defense. You are not saying yes to everything; you are trading consciously.

---

### The 3-Hour Deep Work Anchor

The first three hours are non-negotiable before anything reactive.

Research cited by Burkeman's interpreters suggests 90 minutes of deep focus roughly doubles productivity; 180 minutes quadruples it (McKinsey analysis).

Three hours is enough to move the most important project forward meaningfully every single day, which compounds over weeks far more than scattered multi-hour sprints.

This mirrors the logic of [[MIT — Doing Your Most Important Task First Prevents Reactive Work From Filling the Day]]: anchor the day on output before opening the inbox.

---

### Why "Just Three" Is Psychologically Protective

Most productivity systems fail by being too ambitious.

A list of 20 tasks guarantees incompletion, which creates guilt, which erodes discipline over time.

The 3-3-3 method guarantees that on any day you complete the template, you have objectively succeeded — regardless of what else happened.

This connects to [[Identity-Based Habits Outlast Outcome-Based Goals]]: the identity of "someone who does deep work every morning" is built by winning small, defined days, not perfect ones.

---

### Handling Overflow

Tasks that don't fit in the three shorter slots get rescheduled, not abandoned.

Burkeman is explicit in *Four Thousand Weeks* that humans have finite time and must accept radical limitations — the list will never be empty.

Embracing that limitation is what makes the method sustainable.
