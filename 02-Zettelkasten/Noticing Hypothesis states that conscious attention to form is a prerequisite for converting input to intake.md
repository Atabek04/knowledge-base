### The distinction: input vs. intake

Richard Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis (1990) draws a sharp line between two concepts:

- **Input** — what is heard or read (environmental language)
- **Intake** — what is actually processed for acquisition

<mark style="background: yellow">Only noticed input becomes intake.</mark>

Linguistic features that the learner does not consciously attend to — even if they appear frequently in the input — are not acquired. Awareness is a *necessary* condition for acquisition of specific forms.

---

### What "noticing" requires

Schmidt is careful to specify: noticing requires *focal attention*, not metalinguistic analysis.

A learner doesn't need to know grammar terms. They need to register: "that construction sounds different from what I would have said" — a brief, conscious moment of recognition that something exists.

This is why explicit instruction helps: it *primes* the learner to notice specific forms in subsequent input. After a lesson on English past perfect, learners begin noticing it in authentic reading — and acquisition accelerates. The instruction doesn't teach the form; it sets up the noticing trigger.

---

### Evidence

Schmidt's (1990) longitudinal study of his own Portuguese acquisition in Brazil: forms he explicitly noticed in input were acquired; forms he didn't consciously attend to — despite frequent exposure — were not acquired. Influential though anecdotal.

Subsequent lab research (Leow, 1997; Simard & Wong, 2001): learners who reported higher noticing scores in think-aloud protocols showed significantly better acquisition of target forms, independent of input frequency.

---

### This challenges Krashen's acquisition model

Krashen argued acquisition happens *subconsciously* from comprehensible input — the learner doesn't need to attend to form, only to meaning.

Schmidt's counter: the comprehension threshold is necessary but not sufficient. Comprehensible input that the learner processes only for meaning — without noticing specific forms — does not lead to acquisition of those forms. This explains plateau effects: learners can comprehend a construction for years without acquiring it.

---

### Connection to Output Hypothesis

Swain's [[Output Hypothesis states that producing language forces learners to notice gaps that input alone cannot reveal|Output Hypothesis]] describes one mechanism that *creates* noticing moments: production failures surface what the learner can't produce, triggering attention to the missing form.

The two hypotheses describe different *triggers* for the same underlying mechanism — both increase noticing of form.

---

### Implication for vocabulary

When reading or listening, it is more effective to *stop and register* unknown words — even briefly — than to keep reading passively.

<mark style="background: cyan">Passive reading of unknown words produces exposure without intake.</mark> The word enters the sensory stream but not the acquisition system.

[[Sentence mining builds Anki cards from real input to preserve the contextual meaning that isolated words lose|Sentence mining]] works precisely because it forces a moment of noticing: you stop, register the word, and build a card from it.
