Cal Newport defines **deep work** as professional activity performed in a state of distraction-free concentration that pushes your cognitive capabilities to their limit. It creates new value, improves your skill, and is hard to replicate.

**Shallow work** is the opposite: non-cognitively demanding, logistical tasks often performed while distracted. Email, quick replies, status updates, routine meetings. They feel like work but create little lasting value.

### The Asymmetry

Deep work is rare and valuable. Shallow work is common and replaceable.

Yet the default workday fills itself with shallow work. Email arrives continuously. Notifications interrupt. Colleagues expect rapid responses. If you don't actively protect time for depth, shallow work expands to fill every hour.

---

### The Time-Blocking Solution

Newport's prescription is to schedule every hour of the workday in advance. Assign specific blocks to deep work before reactive tasks can claim that time.

Deep focus has a ceiling — Newport caps hard concentration at **3 to 4 hours per day**. Beyond that, cognitive quality degrades. The goal is not more hours, but protected hours.

A single uninterrupted 3-hour block produces more meaningful output than a full day of fragmented 30-minute slots. This mirrors what [[Maker's Schedule Protects Deep Execution by Avoiding Mid-Block Interruptions]] describes from a structural perspective.

### Connection to Prioritization

Deep work is Eisenhower Q2 work by definition. It's important, not urgent, and it requires the same scheduling discipline.

Without a block on the calendar, shallow work wins by default. See [[The Eisenhower Matrix Reveals That Urgent Tasks Crowd Out Important Ones]].

[[Environment Design Shapes Behavior More Reliably Than Willpower]] explains why the physical and digital setup matters: removing shallow-work triggers (phone, browser tabs, notifications) from the deep-work environment is more reliable than relying on willpower to resist them.
