### The core finding

<mark style="background: yellow">Generative note-taking</mark> — any method that forces the learner to paraphrase, reorganize, or re-represent content rather than transcribe it verbatim — consistently produces better comprehension and transfer than verbatim capture.

Peper & Mayer (1978, 1986): note-taking that requires conceptual encoding ("generative") produced significantly broader and more transferable learning than transcription-style note-taking. This held across multiple studies and content types.

---

### The handwriting vs. laptop debate

Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014, *Psychological Science*) found that laptop note-takers showed **14.6% verbatim overlap** vs. 8.8% for longhand writers, and performed worse on conceptual questions one week later.

**Important caveat:** Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson (2019) did not replicate the effect in a direct replication study. The paper's popularity has outrun its evidential support.

The robust, replicated claim is not "handwriting is better than typing" — it is: <mark style="background: pink">**verbatim transcription is worse than forced paraphrase, regardless of medium.**</mark>

A laptop user who paraphrases will outperform a handwriter who transcribes. The medium is incidental; the processing depth is causal.

### What generative note-taking looks like in practice

- Converting lecture points into your own questions (Cornell cue column)
- Summarizing a section in 2 sentences before moving on
- Drawing a rough diagram of what was just described
- Pausing a video and writing what you remember before re-watching

All of these force the same cognitive operation: actively reconstructing meaning rather than passively capturing symbols.

---

### Cornell notes: why the format works

Cornell notes improve outcomes not because of the physical template but because the structure embeds two generative operations:

1. **Cue column** — requires reducing notes to retrieval questions → spaced retrieval practice
2. **Summary section** — requires synthesis after capture → consolidation

A 2025 RCT (*Frontiers in Psychology*, n = 134, 5-week intervention, 4-week retention test): Cornell notes produced significantly higher retention than unstructured sentence notes (η²p = 0.116 for group effect). The only active ingredient traceable to the format is the embedded retrieval structure.

---

### Sketchnoting: mechanism real, specific claims unverified

Sketchnoting activates the same generative mechanism as any other non-verbatim format: selecting, organizing, and re-representing content. The emotional and engagement benefits are documented (2024 intervention study).

The specific statistics widely circulated ("31% more retention from sketchnotes") have no traceable peer-reviewed source. The components of sketchnoting each have independent empirical support — drawing (Wammes 2016), spatial organization (dual coding theory), active selection (Peper & Mayer) — but sketchnoting as a specific unified practice has minimal direct RCT evidence.
