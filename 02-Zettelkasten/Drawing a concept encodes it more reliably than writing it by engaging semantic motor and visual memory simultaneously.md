### What it is

<mark style="background: yellow">The drawing effect</mark> (Wammes, Meade & Fernandes, 2016): participants who drew a word during encoding recalled **more than twice** as many items as participants who wrote the same word repeatedly.

This held across 7 experiments — with encoding times as short as 4 seconds, longer list lengths, and a variety of comparison conditions. Drawing quality was irrelevant. Crude stick figures worked as well as detailed sketches.

---

### Why it works: three simultaneous channels

What makes drawing so effective is not that it is "visual." Each alternative strategy activates fewer channels:

| Strategy | Semantic | Motor | Pictorial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Writing the word | ✓ | weak | ✗ |
| Mental imagery | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| Viewing a picture | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| **Drawing** | **✓** | **✓** | **✓** |

The researchers specifically ruled out each individual explanation (Experiments 3–5): elaborative processing alone does not explain it; mental imagery alone does not explain it; picture superiority alone does not explain it. Only the combination of all three produces the full effect.

The motor component is not incidental — Longcamp et al. (2008) showed that handwriting activates motor cortex areas that aid recognition and memory, independent of the visual quality of what was produced.

---

### The boundary: drawing works for text input, not visual input

A critical caveat: the drawing advantage applies when the *source* is verbal (words, text, definitions).

When the source is already visual (animations, diagrams, video), drawing during study adds no significant benefit over a control condition — because visual-to-visual translation does not cross representational formats, and the learner is not forced to reconstruct meaning from a different code. Summarizing in words after a visual lesson outperforms drawing after a visual lesson.

<mark style="background: pink">Draw when studying text. Write summaries when studying visuals.</mark>

---

### Application to note-taking

- When reading a textbook: convert key concepts to rough sketches as you go — even before you fully understand them
- The generation attempt itself matters more than the quality of the drawing
- Use the draw-then-compare approach: sketch the concept, then check the textbook diagram and correct your sketch
