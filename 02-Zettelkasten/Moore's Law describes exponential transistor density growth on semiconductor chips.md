Moore's Law, articulated by Gordon Moore (Intel co-founder) in 1965, states that the number of transistors on an integrated circuit doubles approximately every two years (or every 18-24 months by some formulations).

This observation was initially empirical—Moore noticed the trend in existing data and predicted its continuation. It proved remarkably accurate for over 50 years, though showing signs of slowing as physical limits of silicon manufacturing are approached.

Moore's Law has profound implications:
- If transistor count doubles every 2 years, capacity increases 1,000-fold per decade
- Manufacturing cost per transistor has dropped exponentially, following an inverse trajectory
- Speed improvements have generally followed as transistors become smaller and closer together
- Power consumption per transistor has decreased as well

However, Moore's Law is not a physical law like gravity—it is an observation about manufacturing capabilities and economic incentives. As transistor sizes approach atomic scales (~5nm in 2024 production, approaching fundamental quantum limits), continuing the trend becomes progressively harder.

Obstacles to maintaining Moore's Law:
- Heat dissipation: smaller transistors placed densely generate enormous heat
- Quantum tunneling: electrons can unexpectedly pass through insulating barriers at nanometer scales
- Manufacturing cost: precision required at atomic scales is extraordinarily expensive
- Physical limits: cannot place transistors smaller than atoms

The semiconductor industry has adapted by improving 3D stacking (multiple transistor layers), heterogeneous integration (combining different types of chips), and specialized designs (GPUs for graphics, TPUs for AI) rather than pure transistor count increases.

Understanding Moore's Law is essential to understanding why computing has advanced so dramatically and why future advances require new approaches as the law reaches physical limits.
