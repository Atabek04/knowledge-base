### The counterintuitive finding

Grouping related words together for instruction — colors together, furniture together, synonyms together — is one of the most common vocabulary teaching practices. It also consistently *hurts* acquisition in controlled studies.

**Research:**
Tinkham (1993, 1997) and Waring (1997) compared learners who studied semantic sets (near-synonyms, antonyms, category members) against learners who studied unrelated word lists. The semantic-set group showed <mark style="background: pink">significantly lower retention on immediate and delayed tests</mark>.

---

### Why interference occurs — the fan effect

When words share semantic features (chair, table, desk, sofa — all furniture), they activate overlapping memory networks. During retrieval, the correct word must compete against its category neighbors for activation.

The more similar the items, the stronger the competition. This is the *fan effect* in associative memory: adding more associations to a concept *slows* retrieval of any single association.

For near-synonym sets (begin / start / commence / initiate), interference is strongest because there is no perceptual or contextual anchor to differentiate the words — they share almost all semantic features.

---

### The exception — thematic clustering

<mark style="background: cyan">Thematic clusters are neutral or slightly positive for retention.</mark>

Words that co-occur in the same situation (*beach: sand, wave, sunscreen, lifeguard, towel*) don't compete for the same schema slot — each word plays a distinct *role* in the same scene. They anchor to a shared context rather than to a shared semantic feature.

Semantic similarity → interference.
Contextual co-occurrence → complementary.

---

### Practical rules for vocabulary instruction

| Avoid | Use instead |
|---|---|
| Colors grouped together | Thematic scenes (classroom objects, kitchen vocabulary) |
| Number words together | Story-embedded vocabulary |
| Synonym/antonym pairs in early study | Sentence mining from authentic input |
| Cardinal directions as a set | Domain-specific functional clusters (airport, medical) |

<mark style="background: green">**Rule of thumb:** Can the words in this group *appear in the same sentence doing different jobs?* If yes → safe to group. If they're all doing the same job → don't group.</mark>

---

### When semantic grouping works

After words are *already acquired* individually, grouping them for comparative analysis (meaning distinctions, register, collocations) is valuable and appropriate. The interference effect is strongest during *initial encoding*, not during post-acquisition refinement.
