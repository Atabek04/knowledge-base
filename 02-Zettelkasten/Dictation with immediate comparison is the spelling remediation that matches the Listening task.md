A misspelled IELTS Listening answer scores zero even when the word was heard perfectly. That makes spelling a peculiar kind of weakness: it is invisible in your comprehension, invisible in your confidence, and fully visible in your raw score.

The obvious remedy is to study a list of commonly misspelled words. It is close to the least effective thing available, and there is a better-evidenced alternative that also happens to rehearse the exam task itself.

### Why a word list is the wrong tool

<mark style="background: #FF9E9EA6;">No published "commonly misspelled IELTS Listening words" list is derived from actual answer keys.</mark> Cambridge does not release answer-key frequency data, so every such list — including the ones with impressive counts in the title — is a teacher's impression from practice material.

More importantly, a generic list spends most of its time on words you already spell correctly. The words that cost you marks are yours, and they repeat.

### The mechanism that makes spelling stick

Ehri's work on **orthographic mapping** describes how spellings are actually stored: by bonding graphemes to phonemes, not by visual memorisation. The practical consequence she draws is that <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">a word learned as sound alone, or as shape alone, is not learned — the two have to be bound together.</mark>

That is exactly what a Listening answer demands. You receive a phonological signal and must produce an orthographic string, under time pressure, once.

#### Cover-Copy-Compare

The best-evidenced single spelling technique is also the simplest: look at the word, cover it, write it from memory, uncover and compare. It is self-managed, takes seconds per item, and reviews report the strongest effects when it is combined with feedback and spaced review rather than used alone.

#### Dictation

Dictation is Cover-Copy-Compare with the prompt replaced by audio, which is the version that matches the test. It forces phonological decoding and orthographic production at the same time, and the reconstruction effort is where the learning happens — not the exposure.

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Put the audio on the prompt side and the spelling on the response side, always. Never drill an IELTS word from its written form alone.</mark>

### The loop that works

1. **Transcribe rather than answer.** Take a Part 1 recording and write out the whole thing. Every error is a diagnostic.
2. **Log your own misses, tagged by error type** — double letter, silent letter, `ei`/`ie`, homophone, form change. The taxonomy matters because the remedy differs by type.
3. **Drill the log with audio-front, type-the-answer cards.** This is Cover-Copy-Compare in digital form, and the automatic character-by-character comparison is the unforgiving feedback step the research says the technique needs.
4. **Space the review**, because the fluency gains decay without it.

The error log is the actual product here. <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">Generic dictation practice without a logged error list produces hours of activity and no targeted repair — the same failure as running mocks without categorising the wrong answers.</mark> That parallel is not accidental; see [[Log wrong answers by error category because the score tells you nothing about what to fix|why the log is the intervention]].

#### Spelling out loud is a separate sub-skill

Names and addresses dictated letter by letter fail for their own reasons — letter-name confusions rather than orthographic ones, and the speaker's use of "double" for repeats. Drill it separately from word spelling; it does not improve as a side effect.

#### One caution about synthetic audio

Text-to-speech pronounces words cleanly. Real IELTS audio has connected speech, hesitation and overlap. TTS cards fix the *orthography* of a word you already misheard; they do not train the ear, and they should not replace real-audio dictation.

### Why this matters more on computer

The computer-delivered test replaces the paper version's ten minutes of transfer time with two minutes of review. On paper a weak speller can scrawl a phonetic guess and repair it during transfer; on computer what you typed while listening is effectively final — see [[Computer-delivered Listening removes the transfer time so typing accuracy becomes part of the score|the format's real cost]].
