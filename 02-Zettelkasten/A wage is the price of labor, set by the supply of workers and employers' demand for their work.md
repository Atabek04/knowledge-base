A salary looks like something an employer decides, but in a labor market it behaves like any other price. Whether AI lowers the value of knowledge work comes down to how it changes this price.

### Glossary

- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Wage</b>: the price paid for an hour (or a year) of a particular kind of work.</mark>
- **Labor market**: the market where workers sell their time and employers buy it.
- **Labor supply**: how many people are able and willing to do a kind of work at each wage.
- **Labor demand**: how much of that work employers want at each wage.
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Derived demand</b>: demand for workers that comes from demand for what they produce.</mark> It is *derived from* another demand: chefs are hired because people buy restaurant meals.
- **Substitute**: something that does the same job instead of a worker, so employers need fewer of them.
- **Complement**: something that makes a worker more useful, so employers want more of them. It *completes* the worker.

---

### How the wage is set

The wage follows the same rule as any price: it settles where the number of people offering that work equals the number employers want. [[Supply and demand push a price to the level where the amount offered equals the amount wanted|Supply and demand]] explains the balancing mechanism.

OpenStax gives a concrete case: in Minneapolis-St. Paul the nurse market balanced at about $85,000 a year with 41,000 nurses ([OpenStax, Principles of Economics 3e, 4.1](https://openstax.org/books/principles-economics-3e/pages/4-1-demand-and-supply-at-work-in-labor-markets)).

---

### What moves labor supply

- **Number of workers**: population growth, immigration, more people entering the field.
- **Training required**: the longer the training, the fewer people can supply that work.
- **Licensing and policy**: stricter licences cut supply; subsidised schooling raises it.

---

### What moves labor demand

#### Demand for the final product

Because labor demand is derived, a field's wages rise or fall with sales of what it produces.

#### Technology as substitute or complement

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Technology lowers demand for workers it substitutes for and raises demand for workers it complements.</mark> Word processors replaced typists, while computers raised demand for IT professionals (OpenStax 4.1).

The same technology can do both at once to different groups. OpenStax reports that information technology cut demand for low-skill workers and raised it for high-skill ones; the US college wage premium rose from about 30% in 1980 to about 59% in 2019.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">"Technology arrives, so wages fall" skips the key question: for this group of workers, is the technology a substitute or a complement?</mark>
