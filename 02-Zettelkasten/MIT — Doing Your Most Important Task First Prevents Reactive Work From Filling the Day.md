Leo Babauta (Zen Habits) defines a Most Important Task (MIT) as the one thing you most need to accomplish today.

The rule: **complete it before you open email, messages, or any reactive work.**

Most people do the opposite — they check email first, get pulled into responses, and discover at 3pm that the important work never happened.

---

### Why Reactive Work Always Wins Without an MIT

Reactive work (email, Slack, unplanned requests) carries social pressure and false urgency.

It provides quick, visible progress — responses sent, tasks acknowledged.

Important work, by contrast, is slow, uncomfortable, and often internally initiated.

Left in open competition, reactive work wins every morning.

The MIT method resolves this by **removing the competition**: important work runs first, before reactive work is even visible.

---

### How MIT Handles Urgent Surprises

If a genuine emergency appears before you finish your MIT, you face a real trade-off.

But Babauta's framing makes the cost explicit: you are choosing to delay your MIT, not accidentally missing it.

This shifts the dynamic from "I got derailed" to "I made a trade-off." The difference matters because you can evaluate trade-offs; you cannot fix derailments.

Most self-described "urgent" interruptions are not actual emergencies — they feel urgent because of external pressure.

Doing the MIT first creates **evidence** that the most important work got done even on chaotic days, which is the foundation the [[Daily Self-Accounting Closes the Gap Between Plans and Execution]] practice tracks.

---

### Choosing the MIT

Babauta recommends selecting 1–3 MITs the night before or first thing in the morning — before checking anything online.

One task is ideal. Three is the practical maximum.

The question is: "If I could only get one thing done today, what would make today a success?"

This connects to [[Killing the Decision Removes Willpower from the Execution Equation]]: the decision is made in advance, so morning willpower is spent on execution, not selection.

---

### MIT vs. 3-3-3 Method

The MIT method focuses purely on sequencing: important work before reactive work.

The [[The 3-3-3 Method Caps the Day at Three Outputs So Interruptions Can't Crowd Out What Matters|3-3-3 method]] structures the full day into three categories.

They are compatible: the MIT is the deep work anchor of the 3-3-3 morning block, completed before the shorter tasks and maintenance items begin.

---

### The Simplest Form of the Practice

1. The night before, write down one task that matters most tomorrow.
2. In the morning, start with that task before opening any communication.
3. When the task is done (or the morning block ends), open reactive work.

Everything else is detail.
