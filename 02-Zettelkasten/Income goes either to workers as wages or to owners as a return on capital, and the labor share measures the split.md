Every dollar a business earns ends up with someone. Economists sort the people who receive it into two groups: those paid for working, and those paid for owning. Who gains from automation depends on which group a person belongs to.

### Glossary

- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Labor</b>: human work, in time and effort, sold for a wage.</mark>
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Capital</b>: things built to help produce more, such as ovens, factories, software and GPU clusters.</mark>
- **Factors of production**: the inputs a business needs, mainly labor, capital and land. They are the *factors* that *produce* output.
- **Return on capital**: income earned for owning capital, as profit, rent or interest. It is what the owner gets *back* (*returned*) for putting the capital in.
- <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;"><b>Labor share</b>: the part of total income paid to workers as wages; the rest is the capital share.</mark>
- **Investment goods**: capital bought to produce more (machines, computers, buildings).

---

### Two ways to earn

A worker earns only while working. An owner earns as long as the thing they own keeps producing, whether or not they work themselves.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">When a machine replaces a worker, the wage that worker used to earn becomes return on capital for whoever owns the machine.</mark>

#### The bakery example

A bakery earns $100,000 a year after paying for flour. A hired baker gets $40,000, and the owner of the building and oven keeps $60,000. Labor share: 40%.

The owner then replaces the baker with a machine that costs $5,000 a year to run. The owner now keeps $95,000, the machine's maker gets $5,000, and the labor share is 0%.

---

### The labor share has been falling

Karabarbounis and Neiman found that the global labor share has fallen significantly since the early 1980s, in most countries and industries ([NBER w19136](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19136)).

They attribute about half of the decline to investment goods getting cheaper. Once computers and machines cost less, firms swapped workers for them, which is the bakery example at the scale of whole economies.

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">The owner keeps the baker's wage only while competitors do not cut bread prices; once every bakery has the machine, competition can pass part of the saving on to customers.</mark> Who ends up with the gain is a question about distribution, taken up in step 6 of the MOC.
