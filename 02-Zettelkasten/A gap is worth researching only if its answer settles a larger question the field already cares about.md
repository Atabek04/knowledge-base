A bachelor project is usually handed its task. A master's dissertation is *unsupervised research* in Booth's sense: nobody *supervises* the choice of question, so the student is graded partly on whether the question was worth asking (Booth, Colomb & Williams, *The Craft of Research*, 5th ed., ch 2, "From Topics to Questions"). Choosing badly is the most common way to do good work on a thesis that fails.

---

### A gap alone is not a reason

Nearly everything has never been studied. Nobody has measured whether spaced-repetition users who drink tea retain more than those who drink coffee; that is a real gap and a worthless question. <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">"Nobody has studied X" is necessary for a research question and never sufficient, because most unstudied things are unstudied for good reason.</mark>

---

### The so what test

Booth's ch 2 sequence runs topic → question → significance, and the third step is the test:

> I am studying ___, because I want to find out ___, in order to help my reader understand ___.

The last blank is the *so what*: <mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">a question is worth asking when its answer settles, or moves, a larger question that the field already cares about.</mark> If the blank cannot be filled with something a reader would recognise as mattering, the question is a topic, not a research question.

Tea versus coffee fails because nothing larger depends on the answer. "Do adaptive scheduling and gamification rescue each other's failure modes" passes because a yes turns the well-documented decay of gamified motivation from a description into something with a known fix, and a no rules out a proposal the theoretical literature has been making untested.

---

### Pure and applied so what

The larger question can be conceptual (the reader understands something better) or practical (the reader can do something better). Booth calls these *pure* and *applied* research problems, and they map onto the [[A research writer casts the reader as entertained, helped, or educated and only the last two are research|educated and helped reader roles]]. Either fills the blank; a question that fills neither is a hobby.
