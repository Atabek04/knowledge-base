### The claim

Time spent mastering fundamentals from first principles is repaid every time you learn something built on top of them.

Once you understand the base layer, high-level frameworks and libraries become <mark style="background: yellow">"just" specific arrangements of things you already know</mark>.

---
### The cooking analogy

Following recipes one by one teaches you those recipes and nothing more.

Instead, learn the *ingredients* — taste each alone, then in combinations.
Once you know how garlic, lemon, and tahini behave, you don't need a recipe for hummus *or* baba ganoush — you can reconstruct a whole class of dishes, and even stumble onto new ones.

<mark style="background: green">Ingredients are the first principles; recipes are the frameworks.</mark>
Master the ingredients and every recipe gets easier.

---
### Where to spend the effort

Apply first principles to the foundations that rarely change:

- **Formal math** — the language underneath algorithms and ML
- **Algorithms & data structures** — what every framework is built from
- **System design** — how components actually fit together

Frameworks and libraries change constantly.
Fundamentals don't — so deep investment there compounds.

---
### The career payoff

Engineers who can build things from scratch stand out to hiring managers.

Writing an emulator or a compiler by hand proves you understand the machine, not just an API.

<mark style="background: cyan">Deep foundations become long-term confidence — you trust you can figure out anything new, because you've built the layer beneath it.</mark>

---
### The trade-off

There's no shortcut to deep understanding.
This path is slower up front and pays off later — the opposite of tutorial-hopping.

It's the same bet as [[Finishing one DSA resource beats switching between multiple courses|depth over breadth]].
