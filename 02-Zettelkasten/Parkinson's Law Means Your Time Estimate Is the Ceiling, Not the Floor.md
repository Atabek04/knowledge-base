Cyril Northcote Parkinson observed in 1955: "Work expands to fill the time available for its completion."

This is not a motivational slogan. It is a behavioral tendency backed by how the brain responds to available slack.

### The Mechanism

When you give a task two hours, your brain will use two hours.

Not because the work requires it — because the open time invites elaboration, second-guessing, perfectionism, and distraction without urgency.

Give the same task 45 minutes and the brain prioritizes, cuts the unnecessary, and finishes.

---

### Why This Matters for Interruptions

If your schedule is padded with generous time blocks, every interruption looks harmless: "I still have time, I'll deal with this now."

That mental slack is what allows urgents to creep in without cost — because the task has plenty of room to absorb the delay.

Tight, committed blocks change the calculus. There is no spare time to donate to an interruption.

When you tell yourself "this ends at 11:00 no matter what," the interruption becomes genuinely costly — so you defer it instead of absorbing it.

---

### How to Use It

- Set a specific end time for every task before you start
- Make it shorter than you think you need — by about 20-30%
- Treat the end time as a hard constraint, not a target
- When interrupted: "I have a hard stop at 11:00, can we talk then?"

This is timeboxing — a direct application of Parkinson's Law as a productivity tool.

---

### The Failure Mode

Setting short times without changing behavior. Parkinson's Law works because the time pressure activates focus — but only if you treat the deadline as real.

Fake deadlines train you to ignore deadlines. Use external accountability where possible: schedule a meeting after your block, send the output to someone, make the constraint visible.

This connects directly to [[If-Then Plans Pre-Decide Your Response to Interruptions Before They Happen]] — the tight deadline is itself a pre-made decision about how long this takes.
