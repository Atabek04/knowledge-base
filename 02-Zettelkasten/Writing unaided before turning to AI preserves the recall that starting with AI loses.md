Most studies of AI and learning ask whether to use AI. An MIT Media Lab experiment also varied when, and the order turned out to matter.

---

### The experiment

Kosmyna and colleagues (MIT Media Lab, 2025) had 54 participants write essays over several sessions while wearing EEG caps, which record the brain's electrical activity.

Three groups: one used ChatGPT, one used a search engine, one used nothing (the "brain-only" group).

The brain-only group showed the strongest and most widespread neural connectivity, the search group came second, and the ChatGPT group the weakest.

ChatGPT users also remembered their own essays poorly: most could not quote a sentence they had written minutes earlier, and they reported the least sense of ownership over the text.

> [!definition] Cognitive debt
> The accumulated cost of letting a tool do the thinking: effort is saved during the task and paid back later as weaker memory, understanding, and ownership of the result.

---

### The ordering result

In a final session the groups swapped. Brain-only writers got ChatGPT; ChatGPT writers had to write alone.

The two sequences did not end in the same place. Participants who wrote unaided first and used ChatGPT afterwards showed higher connectivity and better recall than those who started with ChatGPT and then lost it.

<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Thinking first builds the memory and the structure, so the AI ends up editing work the brain already owns instead of supplying work it never built.</mark>

#### The same rule from another field

Radiology training uses the same ordering to fight [[Automation bias makes people accept a machine's recommendation without verifying it|automation bias]]: form your own read before seeing the machine's, or you lose the ability to disagree with it.

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Write the first draft, sketch the design, or state your hypothesis before opening the AI.</mark>

---

### Limits of the finding

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">It is a preprint with 54 participants, only 18 of whom completed the swap session, and the task was essay writing, not code.</mark> EEG connectivity measures engagement during a task, not intelligence.

The ordering result fits the other evidence, but on its own it is suggestive rather than settled.
