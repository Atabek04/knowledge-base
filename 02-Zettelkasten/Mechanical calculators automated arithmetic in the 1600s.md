The first mechanical calculators emerged in the 1600s, automating arithmetic operations without requiring constant mental oversight.

Blaise Pascal invented the Pascaline in 1642, which could perform addition and subtraction through a system of interlocking gears and numbered wheels. Operators turned dials representing different digits, and the machine handled carrying operations automatically.

Gottfried Leibniz developed the Stepped Reckoner (1673), which extended mechanical computation to multiplication and division by using a stepped cylinder (a gear with varying heights) that allowed repeated additions and subtractions.

These devices replaced mental arithmetic with mechanical processes. They were expensive, unreliable by modern standards, and limited to basic operations, but they demonstrated that arithmetic could be fully mechanized.

Mechanical calculators evolved throughout the 1800s and dominated scientific and business computation until the 1960s, when electronic calculators displaced them.

The key insight: if arithmetic could be mechanized, perhaps other forms of symbol manipulation could be too—leading eventually to programmable machines.
