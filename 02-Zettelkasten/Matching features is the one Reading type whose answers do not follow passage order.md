Most Academic Reading question types run in passage order, and candidates rely on that to keep their place. Matching features — statements attributed to researchers, categories or names — <mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">does not</mark>, which is why it burns time out of proportion to its mark count.

Two things make it tractable.

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">It is the one type where scanning genuinely works</mark>, because proper nouns have no synonyms. Everywhere else the answer is paraphrased and keyword-hunting fails; here the target word is literally on the page.

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">Do the names mentioned once first and the names mentioned repeatedly last.</mark> A single-mention name has exactly one candidate statement; a multi-mention name needs the statements narrowed down, and by then the single-mention ones are gone.

Two traps. Academic prose gives surnames only, so *Robert Smith* in the question list appears as *Smith* in the text. And extra distractor names are supplied that match no statement, with reuse permitted only if the rubric says so — which it may not.

The neighbouring type, matching sentence endings, has the opposite structure: its beginnings *do* follow text order, and its trap is grammatical. An ending can fit the grammar perfectly and the meaning not at all, because the endings are written to connect plausibly to the wrong ideas.
