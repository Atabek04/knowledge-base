In NUCLE — 1,397 annotated learner essays across 27 error types — determiners and articles are <mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">the single largest category, 14.8% of all annotated errors in training and 19.9% in the blind test set</mark>. That corpus is dominated by article-less first languages, so the finding generalises to the *absence of an article system*, not to any one language.

The L1 contrast has been tested directly: given the same English article task, L1-German learners rarely misused articles while L1-Russian learners fluctuated. The proposed mechanism is that learners without articles oscillate between marking definiteness and marking specificity.

Why this weighs so heavily at the 7-to-8 boundary is arithmetic. <mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">Article slips are distributed — roughly one opportunity per noun phrase, several per sentence</mark> — so they contaminate sentences at a high rate even when the syntax is faultless. Band 8 requires the majority of sentences to be error-free, and a writer with perfect complex structures and a one-in-three article slip rate cannot get there.

<mark style="background: #FFF3A3A6;">It is also the cheapest thing on the list to fix</mark>, because it is one rule set, not a general weakness. The highest-yield cases: generic plurals against the definite article (*students today* versus *the students today*), abstract uncountables with zero article (*research shows*), first mention *a* becoming second mention *the*, and institution nouns (*go to university*, *in prison*).
