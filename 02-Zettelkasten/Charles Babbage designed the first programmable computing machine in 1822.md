Charles Babbage, an English mathematician, conceived the Difference Engine (1822) and later the Analytical Engine (1837), the first designs for programmable computing machines.

The Difference Engine used a mechanical process called the method of differences to automatically compute mathematical tables by performing sequences of addition operations. It was never fully completed due to manufacturing limitations and cost overruns.

The Analytical Engine (1837) was more ambitious: it featured a control unit that accepted instructions via punched cards (inspired by Jacquard looms used in textile manufacturing), a memory to store intermediate results, and an output device. This architecture parallels modern computers with separate processing and memory components.

Babbage's designs were purely mechanical and lacked power sources beyond human cranking—he could not overcome the precision manufacturing constraints of the industrial era. However, his conceptual framework was sound.

Ada Lovelace, collaborating with Babbage, wrote detailed notes describing how the Analytical Engine worked and created the first algorithm intended to be processed by a machine, making her history's first computer programmer.

The Analytical Engine was never built in Babbage's lifetime, but it established the theoretical foundation that a machine could be instructed to perform arbitrary sequences of operations—the core principle of programmable computing.
