The first thing a research-methods book settles is what separates research from building, reading, or reporting. Booth, Colomb and Williams (*The Craft of Research*, 5th ed., Introduction and ch 1) give the working definition used throughout this vault.

> [!definition] Research (Booth)
> Research is gathering information to answer a question and so solve a problem, then reporting it as a **written argument** that something **not previously known** is now known, found out in a way **others can check**.

Booth's own one-liner in ch 1 is the first half (answer a question, solve a problem). The three-part test is the Introduction's account of what a research *report* has to be, compressed into one sentence.

---

### The three parts as a test

Each part is a separate way to fail, so apply them one at a time.

<b>Written argument.</b> A claim, reasons for it, and evidence behind the reasons. A description of what was done is not an argument, however thorough. Screenshots, an architecture diagram and a feature list describe; they claim nothing a reader could disagree with.

<b>Not previously known.</b> The claim must be new to the field, not just new to the author. "We built a memorisation app" was known to be possible before the project started.

<b>Others can check.</b> Protocol, data and analysis are exposed so a reader could rerun the reasoning and land on the same conclusion, or a different one. <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Checkability is what turns a private belief into a public finding: the reader does not have to trust the author, only the method.</mark>

---

### Two submissions, same work, opposite grades

Two master's students build the same app and run the same two-week study with 30 users.

- Student A submits the app: screenshots, architecture, feature list, "30 users used it for two weeks".
- Student B submits: "spaced-repetition scheduling reduces recall errors by 18% against fixed-interval review in 30 learners over two weeks", with protocol, data and analysis attached.

A fails all three parts: no claim, nothing new, nothing to rerun. B passes all three with the identical codebase. <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">The artifact is never the contribution; the checkable claim about it is.</mark>

The analogy from engineering: a pull request. The diff is the artifact. The description states the claim, the tests let a reviewer verify it without trusting the author. A PR with a diff and no description or tests is Student A.

---

### Why the definition matters early

A master's dissertation is graded on whether it *finds something out*, not on whether something was built, so every later step (question, method, statistics, defence) is a way of making one of the three parts hold. Reading the rest of the research-craft roadmap as "which part does this strengthen" keeps the whole thing coherent.
