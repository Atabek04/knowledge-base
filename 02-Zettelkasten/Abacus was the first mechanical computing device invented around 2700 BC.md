The abacus emerged in ancient Sumeria (circa 2700 BC) and independently in Egypt and China, representing humanity's first tool for performing arithmetic calculations mechanically.

It used beads or stones on rods or grooves to represent numeric values, where the position of each bead determined its place value (ones, tens, hundreds, etc.).

The abacus did not calculate on its own—it required a human operator who moved beads according to mental arithmetic algorithms. This marked the first step toward delegating computational labor to a physical device.

Roman merchants used the Roman abacus extensively for trade calculations. Chinese dynasties refined it into the suanpan, which persists in use today and can perform operations nearly as quickly as a calculator.

The abacus remained the dominant calculation tool for over 4,000 years, demonstrating that mechanical aids for computation predated electronic computers by millennia.
