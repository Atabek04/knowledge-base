Four rules are repeated constantly and appear in no official document.

<mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">"Never use I."</mark> Nothing in the criteria mentions person or pronouns, and the Task 2 rubric itself invites examples from your own knowledge or experience. The belief has a second-order cost: the "this essay will argue" dodge produces exactly the *no clear position* fault that caps Task Response.

<mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">"Always write 300+ words."</mark> The minimum is 250 and there is no upper limit, but length raises error density and consumes checking time. Around 270-280 is the teacher consensus.

<mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">"Use the passive, it sounds academic."</mark> No descriptor rewards the passive. It counts only as one item within a variety of structures, and reaching for it produces agent-less vagueness — the over-generalisation fault at band 7.

<mark style="background: #FFB8EBA6;">"Never use contractions."</mark> The sourced advice concerns *wanna* and *gonna*, not *don't*. Writing them out costs nothing, so the habit is harmless — but there is no evidenced penalty behind it.

<mark style="background: #ADCCFFA6;">The pattern is that each rule substitutes a surface feature for a criterion.</mark> None of them is what is being marked, and following them consumes attention that belongs on paragraph topics, specific support and error density.
