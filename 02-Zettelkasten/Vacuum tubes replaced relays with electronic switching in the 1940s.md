The vacuum tube (thermionic valve), invented by Lee de Forest in 1906, became the enabling technology for electronic computing in the 1940s-1950s.

A vacuum tube works by heating a filament (cathode) that emits electrons, which are attracted to a positively charged plate (anode). A control grid between them allows the voltage on the grid to turn the flow of electrons on or off, creating an electronic switch with no moving parts.

This was revolutionary because:
- Switching happened at the speed of electrons (~microseconds), 1,000+ times faster than relays
- No mechanical wear meant greater reliability (though tubes did burn out)
- Tubes could amplify signals, not just switch them on/off
- Heat was the main drawback—early computers generated enormous amounts and required elaborate cooling

ENIAC (Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer), completed in 1946, used 18,000 vacuum tubes to perform calculations. It weighed 30 tons, occupied 1,800 square feet, and consumed 150 kilowatts of electricity (similar to a modern house's total power draw).

ENIAC could perform 5,000 additions per second—revolutionary compared to mechanical machines. However, vacuum tubes failed frequently (on average, the machine had to replace a failed tube every 15 minutes of operation).

The vacuum tube era lasted roughly 1940s-1960s. Despite their limitations, tubes made possible the first true electronic computers and demonstrated that high-speed electronic computation was feasible.

By the late 1950s, limitations of tubes (heat, size, reliability, power consumption) became apparent as computing demands grew. The search for a replacement was urgent.
