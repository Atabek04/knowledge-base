## Teaching Methodology — Socratic Method Implementation

The AI teaches by **drawing out your understanding** through structured questions, not by presenting answers.

### Core Principles

**Never present solutions before validating problems.**

Always ask: "What evidence suggests this?" before accepting an assumption.

Use **progressive disclosure**—fundamentals before advanced concepts.

Connect new material to previously learned concepts.

Check understanding explicitly before proceeding.

### QA Format Specification

Questions and answers follow this pattern:

- Question on one line, answer on next (no "Q:" or "A:" prefixes)
- Bold **key terms** in both questions and answers
- `---` divider between QA pairs (visual separation)
- Maximum 3-5 QA pairs before pausing to check understanding
- Define **prerequisite terms** before using them
- End sections with: "Shall we move to [next topic]?"

**Example pattern:**

```
What is the **Jobs To Be Done** framework?
JTBD describes customer motivation using format: When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]. It grounds product decisions in customer context, not features.

---

Why does JTBD matter for your Arabic platform?
Instead of building "spaced repetition" (feature), you understand "When I have 20 minutes free, I want to make visible progress toward reading the Quran, so I stay motivated to practice daily" (customer motivation).

---

Can you write a JTBD statement for a Russian speaker trying to learn classical Arabic?
```

This structure mirrors how experts think: problem understanding → framework → application.

### Maieutic Teaching Approach

**Maieutic prompting** draws knowledge out through dialogue rather than downloading it.

The AI uses nested questions to help you discover answers yourself.

If you propose building an AI tutor, the AI asks:
- "What specific struggle would this solve?"
- "How are users currently addressing this?"
- "Have you observed this behavior?"
- "What would validate that this is worth building?"

You arrive at the answer through guided discovery, not explanation.

## The Mom Test Framework — Complete Methodology

**The core principle:** Ask questions so **insightful and behavioral** that even your mom (who loves you and wants to encourage you) can't lie to you.

Your mom's praise ("Your idea is great!") is worthless.

Her description of her own behavior ("I spent $40 last month on that Arabic course") is gold.

### The Three Rules

#### Rule 1: Talk About Their Life Instead of Your Idea

Stop pitching.

Stop explaining your vision.

Ask questions about **their actual behavior, pain points, and workarounds**.

**Bad approach:** "Would you use an app that teaches Arabic with gamification?"
- They'll picture their ideal version, not reality
- They'll try to be encouraging
- You learn what they think, not what they do

**Good approach:** "What are you currently using to learn Arabic? What frustrates you about it? How much did it cost? How often do you use it?"
- You understand real behavior
- You see actual pain (tone of voice, specific frustrations)
- You learn switching costs

**Behavioral indicator:** Are they describing what they actually do, or what they think they should do?

#### Rule 2: Ask About Specifics of the Past Instead of Generics or Future Opinions

**Past behavior is the strongest predictor of future behavior.**

Opinions about hypothetical futures are worthless.

**Bad:** "How much would you pay for a perfect Arabic app?" → they give imaginary number
**Good:** "What did you pay for your last Arabic course? How long did you use it?" → real data

**Bad:** "Do you think gamification helps learning?" → abstract opinion
**Good:** "Tell me about a time you used a gamified app. Did it keep you motivated? Why did you stop using it?" → real behavior

The further you go into past specifics, the more honest the answer.

Recent past (last 30 days) is most reliable.

Distant past ("I tried Spanish in college") is less useful for present context.

#### Rule 3: Talk Less and Listen More

You should speak 20% of the conversation. **Listen 80%.**

You're there to **learn**, not to convince.

If you're talking about your idea, you're learning nothing.

If you're uncomfortable with silence, that's good—silence forces them to fill it with truth.

Techniques for listening:
- Ask open-ended questions ("Tell me about...")
- Use silence (don't interrupt)
- Follow surprising comments ("That's interesting—tell me more")
- Take notes (shows you care, gives you thinking time)
- Play dumb ("I don't understand—explain that to me")
- Validate without agreeing ("Got it, that makes sense")

## The Mom Test Framework — Practical Implementation

### Five-Step Meeting Prep

Before scheduling a customer interview, prepare these five elements:

**1. Vision:** What's your high-level goal for this conversation?
Example: "Understand how Russian speakers currently learn Arabic and what stops them."

**2. Framing:** What's your current stage? What help do you need?
Example: "We're exploring a potential product. We're trying to understand the problem better."

**3. Weakness:** What specific knowledge gap are you trying to fill?
Example: "We don't know if current learners would switch platforms or if they're happy with existing solutions."

**4. Pedestal:** Why is this specific person valuable to talk to?
Example: "You mentioned you've tried three different Arabic learning methods—you have more experience than most."

**5. Ask:** What specific questions will you ask?
Write them down. Review them for leading questions.

### CRITICAL RULE: Don't Schedule Meetings Without Clear Questions

If your questions are vague ("How do you feel about language learning?") or leading ("Do you wish learning Arabic was easier?"), don't schedule.

You'll waste their time and learn nothing.

A good question reveals something unexpected.

## The Mom Test Framework — Practical Techniques

| Technique | Description | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Dig deep** | Ask "Why?" and "Tell me more" repeatedly | "You mentioned frustration—tell me more about that." |
| **Find the currency** | What are they paying? (time, money, effort) | "How many hours per week do you spend on Arabic now?" |
| **Zoom out** | Understand broader context | "What's your ultimate goal with Arabic?" |
| **Seek introduction** | Ask for referrals to others | "Who else do you know learning Arabic I could talk to?" |
| **Avoid compliments** | Deflect praise; return to listening | "Thanks! Now tell me about your experience with X." |
| **Observe behavior** | Notice what they do, not just what they say | Do they have the Arabic course open? Notes scattered around? |

### Red Flags (Bad Interview Signals)

If you observe these during a customer interview, **your data is unreliable**:

- They're complimenting your idea excessively ("That's brilliant!")
- You're doing most of the talking
- Getting opinions about hypothetical futures ("I would probably...")
- You feel "validated" (dangerous!) or excited by their response
- Nothing surprised you (means you weren't learning)
- Can't recall specific behaviors they mentioned
- They didn't ask clarifying questions (weren't genuinely interested)

The **best interviews** are uncomfortable—they contradict your assumptions.

## The Mom Test Framework — Validation Metrics

How do you know when you've actually validated a problem?

| Signal | Strong Evidence | Weak Evidence |
|--------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Currency (Money)** | They've already paid for similar solutions | "I would probably pay if..." |
| **Currency (Time)** | They spend 2+ hours/week on current solution | "I sometimes think about learning" |
| **Frequency** | Problem occurs daily or weekly | "I've thought about this for years" |
| **Emotion** | Visible frustration, animated discussion | Calm, intellectual, detached |
| **Workarounds** | DIY solutions or complex workflows to handle problem | "I just deal with it" |
| **Recent action** | Tried solving problem in last 30 days | "Haven't really looked into it yet" |
| **Switching cost** | Currently paying (or investing time) in solution | No skin in the game |

**Strong validation** has multiple signals stacked together.

"I paid $40 last month, I study every day before work, and I'm frustrated with the lack of grammar explanation" = **validated problem**.

"I think gamification in apps is cool" = **unreliable opinion**.

### When You've Validated Enough

You've talked to sufficient customers when:

- You've spoken with 15-30 people **in your specific target segment** (not just "people interested in languages")
- You hear **the same problems repeatedly** (you can predict what the next person will say)
- People have already **paid money or invested significant time** to solve the problem
- You understand the **job they're trying to accomplish** deeply
- You know **what existing solution they'll abandon** to use yours
- You can **articulate the problem in their words**, not yours

## Product Management Fundamentals

### PM vs PO: Role Differences

These roles are distinct and have different horizons, stakeholders, and artifacts:

| Aspect | Product Manager | Product Owner |
|--------|----------------|---------------|
| **Scope** | Strategic (vision, market, roadmap) | Tactical (backlog, stories, team) |
| **Time Horizon** | 6-12+ months | 1-3 sprints |
| **Stakeholders** | Executives, customers, market, users | Dev team, stakeholders, product manager |
| **Primary Artifacts** | Roadmap, strategy, OKRs, success metrics | Backlog, user stories, acceptance criteria |
| **Key Decision** | What should we build and why? | How do we build it? |

A startup founder often wears both hats initially.

The **PM** decides: "Spaced repetition is the riskiest assumption—we should validate this before building the AI tutor."

The **PO** writes: "As a learner, I want to see my vocabulary progress visualized, so I stay motivated."

### Core PM Responsibilities

- **Discovery:** Understand customer problems, market opportunities, validate assumptions with real people
- **Strategy:** Define vision, create roadmap, set OKRs, identify success metrics
- **Execution:** Ruthlessly prioritize, write clear requirements, coordinate across teams
- **Measurement:** Track key metrics, run experiments, decide whether to pivot or persevere

### Core PO Responsibilities

- **Backlog management:** Maintain prioritized product backlog, break down work into stories
- **Stakeholder alignment:** Communicate decisions, manage expectations, say "no" to scope creep
- **Team collaboration:** Facilitate sprint ceremonies, answer questions, accept completed work

## Key PM Frameworks

### 1. Jobs To Be Done (JTBD)

**Format:** When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].

This framework shifts focus from **features to customer goals**.

**Generic approach:** "Build spaced repetition feature."

**JTBD approach:** "When I have 20 minutes downtime, I want to make visible progress toward reading the Quran fluently, so I stay motivated to study despite full-time work."

The feature (spaced repetition) is one solution.

The job reveals what users truly value (progress visibility, motivation, achievable goals).

**For your Arabic platform:**
- When: Russian speaker has 15-20 minutes free
- Motivation: Wants to build real Arabic reading comprehension (not just vocabulary)
- Outcome: Can eventually read classical Islamic texts independently

This reveals what's actually important: **structured curriculum** (not random vocab), **comprehension tracking** (not just completion), **connection to real texts** (not abstract lessons).

---

### 2. Lean Startup — Build-Measure-Learn Cycle

The Lean Startup methodology minimizes risk by validating assumptions before building large features.

**Cycle:**
1. **Build:** Create minimum viable product (MVP)
2. **Measure:** Track user behavior and metrics
3. **Learn:** Validate or invalidate core hypotheses
4. **Repeat:** Pivot or persevere based on learning

**Critical principle:** You can't know if users will use your product until users actually use it.

Opinions, surveys, and focus groups are unreliable.

Real behavior is everything.

**For your Arabic platform:**
- Hypothesis: Users will pay $20/month for a structured Arabic course
- Minimum test: Show landing page to 50 target customers, collect email signups as proxy for interest
- Measure: Did 10%+ actually pay? What's current solution cost they're already paying?
- Learn: Is pricing right? Is problem severe enough?

This is cheaper than building a full platform first.

---

### 3. RICE Prioritization

**Formula:** Score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort

Used to rank features by expected value relative to cost.

| Component | Definition | Example |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| **Reach** | How many users will this affect? (in next quarter) | 1000 target users available |
| **Impact** | How much will it help each user? (1=small, 3=large) | Gamification = 2x (nice-to-have) |
| **Confidence** | How confident are you? (0.5=low, 1.0=certain) | Spaced repetition = 0.8 (validated) |
| **Effort** | How much work? (team weeks) | Spaced repetition = 2 weeks |

**Example scoring for Arabic platform:**

**Spaced repetition:**
- Reach: 1000 (all learners benefit)
- Impact: 3 (critical for retention)
- Confidence: 0.8 (research-backed)
- Effort: 2 weeks
- **Score: (1000 × 3 × 0.8) / 2 = 1200**

**AI tutor:**
- Reach: 1000 (all learners)
- Impact: 2 (nice but not critical)
- Confidence: 0.5 (uncertain if worth it)
- Effort: 6 weeks
- **Score: (1000 × 2 × 0.5) / 6 = 167**

**Visual progress dashboard:**
- Reach: 1000
- Impact: 2
- Confidence: 0.9 (users mention motivation)
- Effort: 1 week
- **Score: (1000 × 2 × 0.9) / 1 = 1800**

**Decision:** Build dashboard first (1800), then spaced repetition (1200), delay AI tutor (167).

---

### 4. Kano Model — Feature Classification

Not all features are equal.

The **Kano Model** categorizes features by their impact on satisfaction:

| Category | Definition | Example |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| **Basic (Threshold)** | Must-have; expected; users notice absence | Interface works without crashes |
| **Performance** | More is better; increases satisfaction | Faster lesson loading, clearer grammar explanations |
| **Delight (Exciters)** | Unexpected bonus; creates joy; differentiates | Animated character guides lessons, celebratory animations on milestone |

Confusing these categories wastes resources.

Building **delighters** before **basics** fails.

Building **basics** competitively means delighters become table stakes.

**For Arabic platform:**
- **Basic:** Curriculum follows proven textbook structure, lessons load fast, no bugs
- **Performance:** Flexible pacing (learners adjust speed), clear grammar explanations, progress tracking
- **Delight:** Animated narrator, achievement badges, leaderboard (community competition)

Start with basic + performance.

---

### 5. Pirate Metrics (AARRR)

Startup metrics follow the **Pirate Metrics** framework:

**A**cquisition → **A**ctivation → **R**etention → **R**evenue → **R**eferral

This sequence matters.

**You can't monetize users who don't return.**

**Acquisition** (bringing users) is cheap relative to **retention** (keeping them).

**For Arabic platform:**
- **Acquisition:** How many Russian speakers discover the platform? (content marketing, community partnerships)
- **Activation:** What % sign up? Complete first lesson? (onboarding quality)
- **Retention:** What % study at least 3x/week after 30 days? (core product quality)
- **Revenue:** What % convert to paying subscribers? (pricing, value communication)
- **Referral:** What % refer friends? (satisfaction, network effects)

**Critical principle:** Focus ruthlessly on retention before acquisition.

A platform with 1000 users studying 3x/week is more valuable than 10,000 users who tried once.

Retention validates product-market fit.
