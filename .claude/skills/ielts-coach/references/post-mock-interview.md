# The post-mock interview

Run this **immediately after a mock, before looking at the answer key** — or at the latest
before the error categories are assigned. It takes two minutes and it is not optional.

## Why it exists

The answer sheet records *what* was wrong. It cannot record *why*, and two of the most
expensive failure modes leave no trace in it at all:

- **A right answer that was a guess.** It scores the same mark as a heard answer and
  silently inflates the raw score. Untracked, it is a mark that will disappear on test day.
- **A wrong answer caused by pacing, not by hearing.** `lost-place` and a genuine
  `paraphrase` miss look identical in the key. They want opposite remedies — a timing rule
  versus comprehension drilling — so mislabelling one as the other wastes the next fortnight.

The candidate is the only source for both, and the memory decays within the hour. So the
interview comes before the analysis, not after it.

## Before anything else: capture the review screens

<mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">Tell the user to screenshot every review page before closing the result tab.</mark> Online platforms do not keep the per-question breakdown, and their PDF export is usually a certificate carrying the raw score and nothing else.

A closed tab means the wrong answers are gone permanently. The score survives and is the least useful part of the record — a mock whose errors were not captured has cost an hour and produced one number.

## The questions

Ask all four, in this order, in one message. Keep them short — this is a checkpoint, not a
reflection exercise, and a long form gets skipped by the third mock.

1. **Time management** — Did the ~30s reading time before each part feel enough? Which part
   did you run out of it in? Did you finish reading the questions before the audio started?
2. **Comprehension** — Was any part hard to *follow* as speech, separately from answering it?
   Which part, and was it the accent, the speed, or the topic?
3. **Spelling** — Any answer where you heard the word clearly and were unsure how to write it?
   Name the words, even the ones you got right.
4. **Audio speed** — Did the speech feel too fast to keep up with, or was the pressure coming
   from the questions rather than the audio?

Then one more, which catches the invisible marks:

5. **Guesses** — Which questions did you answer without actually hearing the answer? Numbers
   are enough.

## What to do with the answers

Fold them into the log entry, not into a separate document. Specifically:

- Every question the user names in **Q5** gets flagged in the log even if it was marked
  correct. Report the raw score **and** the score net of guessed-correct answers. The second
  number is the honest one.
- **Q1 and Q4 together** separate a pacing problem from a comprehension problem. If reading
  time ran out but the audio felt manageable, the fault is the prep-time routine, not
  listening ability — and the remedy is a technique change, not more mocks.
- **Q3** builds the personal misspelling list. Words the user *hesitated over* belong on it
  as much as words they got wrong; the hesitation is the signal that the spelling is not yet
  automatic, and it will fail under exam pressure.
- **Q2** naming a specific part repeatedly across mocks is the only reliable way to find a
  part-specific weakness, because raw scores are not broken down by part on most platforms.

## The carry-forward reminder

End every interview by writing a **pre-mock checklist of at most five lines** into the log
under the entry, derived from *this* mock's findings. Before the next mock, read the previous
entry's checklist back to the user first — that is the whole point of writing it.

Rules for the checklist: each line is an instruction the user can execute during the test
(*"read only the stems in prep time, never the options"*), never a goal (*"be more accurate"*).
Drop a line once two consecutive mocks show that category clean. Five lines is a ceiling, not
a target — three good ones beat five.
