# Speaking practice

Two things happen in this mode: generating practice, and auditing a transcript the user
brings back. Both are useful. A third thing — scoring the delivery — is not possible from
text and must be declined rather than approximated.

Official descriptors:
[Speaking band descriptors (PDF)](https://ielts.org/cdn/ielts-guides/ielts-speaking-band-descriptors.pdf)

## What you cannot do, and why to say so out loud

Pronunciation is a quarter of the Speaking band and it is not in the transcript. Measured:
transcript-only pronunciation assessment reaches 0.404 accuracy, against 0.728 when the
model is given engineered acoustic features. Prosody correlation stays at 0.243 even *with*
those features. And the obvious workaround fails — three different ways of writing pause
markers into a transcript produced effect sizes whose confidence intervals cross zero. The
fluency signal is in measured speech timing, not in how a pause is typed.

Holistic speaking scores from text look fine, but only because a single overall label is
dominated by grammar, vocabulary and coherence — the parts that *are* in the transcript.
A plausible-looking number is not evidence the pronunciation component was assessed at all.

So: decline pronunciation, fluency, intonation and accent judgements from text, say in one
line why, and move to what the transcript genuinely supports. The user's own ear on the
recording is a better instrument than you are here, and telling them that is more useful
than a fabricated number.

## Generating practice

Run parts in the real format and to the real clock. The point is production under
constraint, not question variety.

**Part 1** — 3–4 questions on a familiar topic, one at a time. Two to three sentences is
the working answer length; a one-clause answer gives too small a language sample.

**Part 2** — one cue card, then hold the structure: one minute to prepare, up to two
minutes to speak. Tell the user to aim to be cut off at two minutes — being stopped proves
they can speak at length, and planning ~2:15 of material makes that happen.

**Part 3** — 4–5 abstract follow-ups on the Part 2 theme. Push toward society rather than
self, and toward speculation, evaluation and justification. Working length is 30–60 seconds.
Giving Part 1-sized answers here is the standard failure and it drags the fluency average.

Draw topics from the recurring themes — work, media, education, environment, technology,
health, city life. Circulating "forecast" lists are a coverage map, never a script bank:
use them to check no theme is unrehearsed, and never to prepare wording.

**Do not supply model answers to memorise.** IDP states the mechanism: memorised delivery
loses natural emphasis and emotion, which damages Pronunciation — a quarter of the mark.
Examiners are trained to spot it and respond by raising question difficulty. The detection
cue is a profile, not a phrase: Part 2 markedly stronger than Parts 1 and 3, which is
exactly what a bank of prepared cue-card answers produces. Preparing *ideas and
collocations* is invisible; preparing *sentences* is the thing being caught.

## Auditing a transcript

Ask the user to note where they hesitated and whether each pause was reaching for content
or reaching for words. That distinction is the actual band-8 gate — band 8 permits
hesitation that is content-related and penalises hesitation used to find words or grammar —
and only the speaker knows which it was. You cannot recover it from text, but they can
supply it.

Then audit what the transcript holds:

**Grammar** — count error-carrying utterances and group them. Articles will dominate; see
`l1-priors.md`. Part 3 is where they concentrate, because abstraction means generic noun
phrases (*the government*, *a lack of*, *the elderly*) — so the section that earns the
fluency marks is also the one that leaks grammar marks.

**Lexis** — is the range wide, and are the collocations natural? Band 8 asks for *"less
common and idiomatic vocabulary"* used skilfully, and "less common" means less common in
textbooks, not difficult. Natural word partnerships beat rare single words. Flag forced
idioms — inserted off-topic, they read as prepared and cost more than they gain.

**Answer development** — Part 3 answers should qualify rather than assert. Hedges (*to some
extent*, *generally speaking*, *arguably*, *in all likelihood*) do double duty: they count
as grammatical range and they buy content-shaped thinking time, which is the only hesitation
band 8 permits.

**Length and shape** per part, against the working targets above.

Report as located findings with quotes, same discipline as the writing audit. No band.

## Practice methods worth recommending

**Shadowing** has the strongest evidence base and it happens to match this user's needs
exactly: a systematic review finds it improves prosody, fluency, comprehensibility and
accentedness, while its effect on segmental control is inconclusive — and segmentals are
what to deprioritise anyway.

**Self-recording with one rotating target.** Reviewing everything at once is why recorded
practice stalls. One session, one target: articles on Monday, chunking on Wednesday,
hesitation type on Friday.

**Not accent reduction.** The band 8 descriptor says accent has *minimal effect on
intelligibility* — an L1 accent is explicitly compatible with the target band. What costs
marks is listener effort. Point the time at prosody instead: chunking, sentence stress,
weak forms, then word stress on the user's own topic vocabulary.
