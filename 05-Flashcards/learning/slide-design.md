Coding Questions
What are Mayer's 3 foundational assumptions about multimedia learning?
Back:
1. **Dual channel** — humans process visual and auditory information through two separate systems
2. **Limited capacity** — each channel handles only a small amount at once
3. **Active processing** — learning requires selecting, organizing, and integrating information — not passive reception

These are not independent: dual channel explains *where* info goes, limited capacity explains *why* overload fails, active processing explains *what* the learner must do.

What is Mayer's redundancy principle and what is the most common violation?
Back:
**Principle:** Do not present the same words in text AND spoken narration simultaneously.

**Why:** Both use the verbal channel. You get two verbal inputs but no second code — load increases, learning doesn't.

**Most common violation:** putting the exact sentences you're speaking as bullet points on the slide while narrating them.

Fix: graphic + spoken narration. Let the visual channel carry images, not text duplicates.

END

What is Mayer's modality principle?
Back:
Pair visuals with **spoken narration** rather than on-screen text.

**Why:** spoken words use the auditory channel; visuals use the visual channel → both channels work in parallel → load is distributed.

On-screen text uses the visual channel for both image and text → competition for the same channel → one gets neglected.

One of the largest effect sizes in the 2025 meta-analysis.

END

What is Mayer's coherence principle and what does the evidence say about its effect size?
Back:
**Principle:** Remove irrelevant words, images, animations, and sounds — even interesting ones ("seductive details").

**Evidence:** The 2025 Cromley & Chen meta-analysis (592 effects) found coherence — specifically removing seductive detail — had one of the **largest effect sizes** in the entire research program.

Every decorative element that doesn't explain content costs cognitive capacity and teaches nothing.

What two structural problems does Assertion-Evidence slide structure solve?
Back:
**Problem 1 — Topic-label titles:** "Results", "Wind Energy", "Background"
→ Name a subject. Make no claim. Give the audience nothing to look for.

**Problem 2 — Bullet-list bodies:** Lists without relationships or reasoning.
→ Force the audience to infer what matters and how items connect.

**Fix:**
- Title = complete sentence stating the main claim (the assertion)
- Body = one piece of visual evidence that proves the assertion

What did Alley's 2011 study find comparing AES vs. conventional slides?
Back:
N=110 engineering students, same content:

- AES group: **59% comprehension**
- Conventional group: **42% comprehension** → 17 percentage point gain, p < 0.01
- Misconceptions ~**10× higher** in conventional group
- AES group reported **lower cognitive load**
- Gains held at **delayed post-test** (retention, not just immediate recall)

Creating AES slides also deepened content understanding in the presenter — forcing the assertion clarifies your own thinking.

What makes an assertion valid vs. a topic label?
Back:
An assertion must be **falsifiable** — it must be possible to prove it wrong.

Test: can you argue against the title?

- ~~"Solar Energy"~~ → cannot be argued against → topic label
- "Solar panel costs have fallen 90% since 2010" → can be contested → assertion

If it can't be proven wrong, it names a subject. It doesn't make a claim.

Length target: 8–12 words, grammatically complete sentence.

What is the slideument problem in Presentation Zen?
Back:
A **slideument** is a hybrid trying to be both a projected slide AND a standalone reading document.

It fails at both:
- As a **slide:** too much text competes with the speaker → violates Mayer's redundancy and modality principles
- As a **document:** no context, transitions, or argument structure → unreadable without the speaker

Fix: three separate artifacts:
1. **Slides** — sparse visuals for projection only
2. **Speaker notes** — your full script
3. **Handout** — a real document with prose and context

END

What are Garr Reynolds' three core principles in Presentation Zen?
Back:
1. **Restraint** — in content selection; say less, include less, cut ruthlessly
2. **Simplicity** — in design; one idea per slide; white space is clarity, not emptiness
3. **Naturalness** — in delivery; speak conversationally; make genuine eye contact; never read slides

The aesthetic foundation is Japanese Zen: *wabi-sabi* (beauty in simplicity), *ma* (meaningful empty space).

What are the two systems in Dual Coding Theory and what are their mental units?
Back:
| System | Processes | Mental units |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbal** | Language, text, speech, sequential logic | Logogens |
| **Nonverbal (Imagery)** | Pictures, spatial data, sensory info | Imagens |

The systems are **independent but interconnected** — a word can activate an image (referential processing); an image can activate a label.

Why does dual coding produce stronger memory than single-channel encoding?
Back:
Encoding through **both** systems creates **two independent retrieval routes**.

Either route alone can reconstruct the memory — if one fails (verbal access blocked), the imagery route still works.

Single-channel: one retrieval route → one point of failure.
Dual-channel: two routes → redundant retrieval → far more durable.

This is why concrete words (which trigger imagery) are remembered far better than abstract words.
Tags: pedagogy dual-coding paivio
END

Coding Questions
Why does putting the same sentence on screen that you're speaking NOT create dual coding?
Back:
Dual coding requires **two different representational systems** — verbal AND imagery.

Spoken narration + on-screen text = verbal + verbal.

Both inputs use the same verbal channel. You get **no second retrieval route** — just overloaded verbal processing.

True dual coding: spoken narration (verbal) + semantically matched diagram (imagery).
