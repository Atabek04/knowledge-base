START
English Card
How is the present continuous formed?
Back: **am / is / are + -ing**
- **I** am *(I'm)*
- **he / she / it** is *(he's)*
- **we / you / they** are *(we're)*
- The auxiliary carries the person and the tense; **-ing never changes**

START
English Card
When do you use the present continuous? *(three uses)*
Back: **1 — Now.** Started and unfinished, whether or not it is happening as you speak.
- **2 — A period around now**: *today · this week · this year*
- **3 — A change in progress**: getting, becoming, changing, improving, starting, rising, falling, growing
- All three share one idea: **something is in the middle of happening**. The present simple states what is permanent or habitual instead

START
English Card
What does *I am doing* mean, and must the action be happening as you speak?
Back: **I started it and I have not finished — I am in the middle of it.**
- It does **not** have to be happening at the moment of speaking
- "I'm reading a good book at the moment" is true while you are on the phone, not reading
- The test is whether the activity is **unfinished**, not whether it is under way right now

Please don't make so much noise. I {1:'m trying} (try) to work.
Explanation: Happening now, unfinished → present continuous.
- *I try to work* is the present simple, which states a habit, not this moment
- Russian has one present tense — *я стараюсь* covers both, which is why the simple form feels correct here

START
English Grammar
Let's go out now. It {1:isn't raining} (rain, negative) any more.
Explanation: A situation going on **now** → present continuous, negative included.
- *It doesn't rain* denies that it rains **ever**, which is a different claim
- The negative is where this slips most: the habitual reading sounds harmless but changes the meaning

English Grammar
A: You {1:'re working} (work) hard today. B: Yes, I have a lot to do.
Explanation: *today · this week · this year* are **periods around now**, so they take the continuous.
- *You work hard today* is wrong: the simple form would make it a permanent trait
- The period need not be short — "the company I work for **isn't doing** so well this year"

English Grammar
The population of the world {1:is increasing} (increase) very fast.
Explanation: A **change that has started to happen** → present continuous.
- *increases* would state a timeless fact, not a change in progress
- Verbs that behave this way: **getting, becoming, changing, improving, starting, beginning, increasing, rising, falling, growing**

START
English Grammar
'Where's Mark?' 'He {1:'s having} (have) a shower.'
Explanation: *have* here is an **action**, not a state, so it takes the continuous.
- *He has a shower* would mean the bathroom contains one, or that he showers habitually
- Action *have* — a shower, lunch, a break, a good time — behaves like any other verb
- State *have* — meaning **own** — never takes the continuous: *I have a car*, never *I'm having a car*

START
English Grammar
Is your English {1:getting} better?
Explanation: A change in progress → continuous, and questions keep that form.
- *Does your English get better?* asks about a habit, which makes no sense of a change
- Question order is **auxiliary + subject + -ing**: *is your English getting*, *are you enjoying it*
- The auxiliary moves; the **-ing form never does**

English Grammar
At first I didn't like my job, but I {1:'m starting} (start) to enjoy it now.
Explanation: *start* is a change verb, and the change is under way → continuous.
- *I start to enjoy it* has no sense of a shift in progress
- The giveaway is the contrast with **at first** — a change is being described, so the continuous is forced
