START
English Card
To formally bring a meeting to an end → ______ the meeting
Back: **close the meeting** — *The chairperson closed the meeting at 4.30.*
- *close*, not *shut*: we close meetings, discussions, conferences
- *shut* takes physical openings — *shut your mouth*, *shut the door* — and is informal
- Boundary: a dentist says *close your mouth* (polite); *Shut your mouth!* is rude

START
English Card
The engine won't turn over — you can't ______ the car
Back: **start the car** — *It was a cold morning and I could not start my car.*
- *start*, NOT *begin*, for engines and vehicles
- *begin* belongs to formal and abstract contexts: *Before the universe began…*
- The two are interchangeable for *start/begin a lesson*; they are not for machinery

START
English Card
Which of *big* / *large* goes with *decision* and *problem*, and which with clothing sizes?
Back: **a big decision**, **big problems** — but **the large size**.
- *big* carries the abstract, evaluative sense: importance, seriousness
- *large* is measurement and physical size: *large size*, *large quantity*
- *a large decision* is the error; the meaning survives, the collocation does not

English Card
They decided to stop being a couple → they ______ their relationship
Back: **ended their relationship** — *end* here means *decide to stop*.
- *The film ended with the hero dying* — same verb, an event reaching its close
- *finish* means *complete*: *I haven't finished my homework yet*
- Boundary: you *finish* a task you were working through; you *end* something that was ongoing

START
English Card
Furniture that is old and valuable → ______ furniture
Back: **antique furniture**, **antique jewellery**.
- *antique* adds value to *old*; *an antique building* is wrong — that's **a very old building**
- Contrast the whole set: *an old friend* (long-known), *ancient history / ancient times* (remote past)
- The thesaurus trap: all four gloss as "old", none of them swap

START
English Card
A polite way to refer to an old person → an ______ person
Back: **an elderly person** — *I helped an elderly person cross the road.*
- *elderly* is more polite than *old* when describing people
- Register card: *old man / old woman* is neutral-to-blunt; *elderly* is the safe choice in writing
- Never *ancient* or *antique* of a person — those are for eras and objects

English Card
Someone raising a child without a partner → a ______ parent
Back: **a single parent** — *Donna is a single parent; it's difficult for her to work full time.*
- *single* here means "with no partner", not "one of a kind"
- Not *alone parent*, not *lonely parent* — *lonely* is an emotion, not a family structure
- Companion collocation for Task 2: *single-parent families*

START
English Card
Which word marks the *feeling* of being on your own, and which marks the *fact*?
Back: **lonely** is the feeling; **alone** is the fact.
- *I felt desperately lonely when I moved from London* — emotion, intensifier *desperately*
- *I live alone, but I don't like travelling alone* — plain circumstance, no sadness implied
- *a lonely place/spot* extends the feeling to a location: remote, few people

A person with no brothers or sisters → an ______ child

START
English Card
Which verb for a phone battery, and which for a lorry?
Back: **charge my phone** · **load the lorry**.
- *charge* takes batteries and electrical items
- *load* takes cargoes, lorries, vans, ships — and weapons: *load a gun*
- Both gloss as "fill up with something"; neither substitutes for the other

START
English Card
Three people were hurt in the crash, and a sofa was harmed in transit — which verb for each?
Back: **injured people** · **a damaged sofa**.
- *injure* collocates with people; *damage* collocates with things
- *Three injured people were taken to hospital* · *The shop tried to sell me a damaged sofa*
- The error runs both ways: *damaged people* and *injured furniture* are equally wrong

START
English Card
Which verb for crops, and which for cattle?
Back: **grow crops** · **raise cattle**.
- *grow* collocates with crops and plants; *raise* with animals and children
- *raise children* is the same verb — living things you bring up, not things you cultivate
- Russian *выращивать* covers both, which is exactly why *grow cattle* slips out

START
English Card
A party takes control of the national assembly → it ______ control
Back: **gained control** — also **gain power**, **gain access**.
- *gain* + abstract nouns of political authority
- Not *win control*: *win* needs a competition or a fight behind it
- Same verb, benefit sense: **gain a reputation**, **gain publicity**, **gain recognition**, **gain an advantage** (formal)

START
English Card
Which noun group does *win* take, and what are its two flavours?
Back: **win** takes competition and fighting nouns.
- Competition: **win an award** · **a prize** · **a medal** · **a match**
- Fighting: **win a battle** · **a war** · **an election**
- Boundary against *gain*: *gain* takes abstract benefit (*recognition*, *control*), *win* needs a contest

English Card
Which verb for a salary you work for, and which for a profit a company makes?
Back: **earn a salary / earn money** · **make a profit / make money**.
- You *earn* money by working for it; you *make* money by investing, trading, or running a business
- *earn a profit* is the error — a profit is made, not worked for
- Task 1 phrasing: *the company made a profit last year*

English Card
It is hard to reach the targets you set for yourself → hard to ______ your goals
Back: **achieve your goals** — also **achieve your aims**, **achieve success**.
- *achieve* is used mainly with abstract nouns
- Not *gain your goals*, not *win success*: *It's difficult to achieve success in international sport*
- The thesaurus trap this unit exists for — *gain*, *win* and *achieve* all gloss as "get", and none of them swap

START
English Card
You *win* the match — but what do you do to the person across the net?
Back: **beat / defeat an opponent** (or a team).
- You **win a match** but **beat an opponent** — the object decides the verb
- *defeat* is more formal than *beat*: *He defeated his opponent in the tennis final*
- *win an opponent* and *beat a match* are both wrong

START
English Card
Clothes you pack, clothes on your body, a bag in your hand — *take*, *wear* or *carry*?
Back: **take warm clothes** · **wear a hat / a dress** · **carry my bag / my phone**.
- *You'll need to take warm clothes to Alaska* (NOT carry)
- *She was wearing a hat* (NOT carrying) · *She's wearing a dress* (NOT using)
- *I always carry my mobile phone whenever I go out* (NOT wear)
- *wear* = on the body · *carry* = in the hand, with you · *take* = bring along on a journey

Back: **using a laptop**.
- *use* is for tools and equipment you operate — not clothing
- *She's wearing a dress* (NOT using) — the pair the book contrasts it with
- Keep the set apart: *use* a device · *wear* a garment · *carry* a load

START
English Card
Three days in the mountains, two hours in front of the TV, an hour hunting for keys — which verb, every time?
Back: **spend** — *spend* covers both time and money.
- *We spent three days in the mountains* (NOT passed)
- *Last night I spent two hours watching TV* (NOT stayed) · *I spent an hour looking for the keys* (NOT passed)
- *You'll have to spend more money on petrol* (NOT use)
- Russian *провести время* is the source of *pass the time*, and *потратить* of *use money*

English Card
Which water verbs describe lots of people moving smoothly, and which a few moving slowly?
Back: **people pour / stream** (many, smoothly) · **people trickle** (few, slowly).
- *People poured out of the hall at the end of the lecture* · *People trickled into the concert hall*
- Same source image scaled by volume — a downpour versus a dripping tap
- The gradation is the point: choosing *trickle* for a crowd is the error

- *Flare* literally means show a bright light in the dark, which is why it means *sudden*

English Card
An old conflict suddenly becomes serious again → violence / troubles ______
Back: **violence flares up** · **troubles flare up**.
- The *up* matters: *flare up* = a dormant problem reigniting, not a new one starting
- Sibling without *up*: **tempers flared** — a single angry moment
