START
English Card
English has no single future tense. What meaning does each of the four main future forms encode? *(four forms)*
Back: **The form encodes why you believe it, not when it happens.**
- **I'm doing** — already arranged, it's in the diary
- **I'm going to do** — already decided, maybe not arranged
- **I'll do** — decided at this moment, as you speak
- **will** (prediction) — what you know or believe about the future

START
English Grammar
'What {1:are you doing} (do) on Saturday evening?' 'I'm going to the cinema.'
Explanation: An arrangement already made → present continuous.
- *What do you do* asks about a job or a habit, not Saturday
- *What will you do* would ask for a decision made now, but the plan already exists

English Grammar
Alex {1:is getting} (get) married next month.
Explanation: A fixed arrangement, so present continuous — not *will get*.
- <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">will is not used for what has already been arranged</mark>
- Russian uses a future form here (*женится*), which is exactly why *will get* feels right and is wrong

START
English Card
When does a *future* meaning take the present simple instead of the present continuous?
Back: **Timetables and programmes — things fixed by an institution, not by you.**
- *My train **leaves** at 11.30* · *What time **does the film start** tonight?*
- It extends to people whose plans are timetable-fixed: *I **start** my new job on Monday*
- For ordinary personal arrangements the continuous is normal: *What time **are you meeting** Kate tomorrow?* (not *do you meet*)

START
English Grammar
I'm tired. I {1:'m going} (go) to bed now. Goodnight.
Explanation: An action just before you start it → present continuous, especially with verbs of movement.
- *I go to bed now* is wrong; the present simple states a habit
- Same pattern: 'Tina, are you ready?' 'Yes, I'**m coming**.' (not *I come*)

START
English Grammar
'I hear Sarah won the lottery. What is she going to do with the money?' 'She {1:'s going to buy} (buy) a new car.'
Explanation: *(be) going to* = the decision is already made, the intention exists.
- *She'll buy* would announce a decision being taken right now, but Sarah decided earlier
- *going to* does not require an arrangement — only a decision

START
English Card
*I'm leaving next week* vs *I'm going to leave next week* — what is the difference?
Back: **Arranged vs merely decided.**
- *I'm leaving next week. I've booked my flight.* — fixed, arranged with the world
- *I'm going to clean my shoes* — decided, but arranged with nobody
- *I don't know what I'**m doing** tomorrow* = I don't know my schedule; *I don't know what I'**m going to do** about it* = I haven't decided
- Often the difference is small and either form works

English Grammar
Look at those black clouds! It {1:'s going to rain} (rain).
Explanation: Present evidence makes it clear → *going to*, not *will*.
- *it will rain* is a belief about the future; *it's going to rain* is what the sky is showing you now
- Same logic: *I feel terrible. I think I'**m going to be** sick.*

Explanation: *was/were going to* = intended it, and it didn't happen.
- *We travelled by train* would say it did happen
- Also for events: *I thought it **was going to rain**, but it didn't*

English Grammar
Oh, I left the door open. I {1:'ll go} (go) and shut it.
Explanation: A decision taken as you speak → *I'll*.
- *I go and shut it* — the present simple is never used to announce a decision
- *I'm going to shut it* would mean you had already decided before speaking

START
English Card
Which four speech acts does *I'll* normally carry? *(four)*
Back: **Offering, agreeing, promising — and, negated, refusing.**
- Offer: *That bag looks heavy. I'**ll** help you with it.* (not *I help*)
- Agree: *Sure, I'**ll** give it to him when I see him.*
- Promise: *I'**ll** pay you back on Friday.*
- Refusal is *won't*: *I've tried to advise her, but she **won't** listen* · *The car **won't** start*

START
English Card
*Shall I shut the door?* vs *Will you shut the door?* — who wants it done?
Back: **shall I = do you want me to; will you = I want you to.**
- *shall* survives mainly in the questions *Shall I …?* / *Shall we …?*
- It asks permission or a suggestion: *What **shall I** do?* · *Where **shall we** have lunch?*
- *Will you please turn the music down?* is a request, i.e. please do it

English Grammar
Kate has her driving test next week. She's a good driver — she {1:'ll pass} (pass) easily.
Explanation: A prediction about the future → *will/won't*.
- *she's passing* / *she's going to pass* would claim a decision or present evidence; this is Joe's belief
- Predictions cluster with *probably, I'm sure, I think, I don't think, I wonder*

I hope Kate {1:passes} (pass) the driving test.
Explanation: After *I hope* we generally use the present, not *will*.
- *I hope Kate will pass* is not the natural form
- Same: *I hope it **doesn't rain** tomorrow*

START
English Card
*I think James is going to the party* vs *I think James will go to the party* — what changed?
Back: **Going to = I think he has already decided. Will = I think he will decide.**
- The tense reports where the decision sits in time, not the party
- *will* can even describe now, not the future: *Don't phone Amy — she'**ll be** busy* (busy at this moment)

START
English Card
Who can take *shall* as a plain future, and what is its negative?
Back: **Only *I* and *we*; the negative is *shall not* / *shan't*.**
- *I **shall** be late this evening* (or *I will be*) · *We **shall** probably go to France*
- *I **shan't** be here tomorrow* (or *I won't be*)
- Not with he/she/it/you/they: *She **will** be very angry* (not *She shall be*)
- Spoken English normally prefers *I'll* and *we'll*

English Grammar
'Let's have a party.' 'That's a great idea. We {1:'ll invite} (invite) lots of people.'
Explanation: The idea is new, so the decision is being made now → *will*.
- Later, reporting the same plan to Max: *We'**re going to** invite lots of people* — by then the decision is old
- <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">The choice records when the decision was made, not when the action happens</mark>

English Grammar
'Gary has been trying to contact you.' 'Yes, I know. I {1:'m going to call} (call) him.'
Explanation: *Yes, I know* shows the news is old, so the decision predates the conversation → *going to*.
- Change the reply to *Has he? OK, I'**ll** call him* and the decision becomes new
- The give-away is whether the speaker is hearing the news for the first time

START
English Card
For *predicting* the future, when are *will* and *going to* NOT interchangeable?
Back: **When present evidence is what makes you say it — then only *going to*.**
- Both are fine with no evidence: *I think the weather **will be** / **is going to be** nice later*
- Evidence forces *going to*: *Look at those black clouds. It'**s going to rain*** (not *it will rain*)
- *We'**re going to** be late* — the clock says so now; *Jane **will** be late. She's always late* — I know her character

English Grammar
Don't phone between 7 and 8. We {1:'ll be eating} (eat).
Explanation: Future continuous = you will be in the middle of it at that time.
- *we'll eat* names a complete action: *Let's wait for Liz to arrive and then we'**ll eat***
- It parallels the other continuous tenses: was working / is working / will be working

START
English Card
*will be -ing* has a second use that has nothing to do with being mid-action. What is it?
Back: **Announcing complete future actions, especially as arranged programme.**
- *The government **will be making** a statement later today*
- *Later in the programme, I'**ll be talking** to the Minister of Education*
- Used this way it is close in meaning to *will (do)* and *going to (do)*

START
English Grammar
Sally always leaves for work at 8.30. She won't be at home at 9 o'clock — she {1:'ll have gone} (go) to work.
Explanation: Future perfect = complete before a stated future time.
- *she'll go to work* puts the leaving after 9 o'clock, reversing the meaning
- Same shape: *The film **will already have started** by the time we get there*

START
English Grammar
Ted and Amy have been married for 24 years. Next year they {1:will have been} (be) married for 25 years.
Explanation: The perfect moves with the reference point — present perfect now, future perfect for next year.
- *When their son was born, they **had been** married for three years* (past perfect)
- One structure, three vantage points: had / have / will have

START
English Grammar
I'll call you again later when I {1:arrive} (arrive).
Explanation: After *when*, the future takes a present form — *when I do*, never *when I will do*.
- The main clause still carries *will*: *I'**ll** call you … when I **arrive***
- <mark style="background: #FF5582A6;">Russian does use the future in this clause (*когда приеду*), so *when I will arrive* feels correct and is a clear error</mark>

START
English Card
Which conjunctions ban *will* in their own clause? *(six)*
Back: **when, while, before, after, as soon as, until/till.**
- *What are you going to do **while I'm** away?* (not *while I will be*)
- *Wait here **until I come** back*
- *Don't forget to lock the door **when you go** out* (not *will go*)
- The main clause is unaffected and keeps its *will*

START
English Grammar
Can I have the newspaper when you {1:'ve finished} (finish, perfect) with it?
Explanation: Present perfect after *when/after/until/as soon as* marks one thing as complete before the other.
- *When I phone Kate, I'll ask her about the party* — the two happen together, so no perfect (not *when I've phoned*)
- Where they don't overlap, either form often works: *I'll come as soon as I **finish*** or *… as soon as I'**ve finished***

English Grammar
Hurry up! If we {1:don't hurry} (hurry, negative), we'll be late.
Explanation: After *if*, the future also takes the present.
- *if we won't hurry* is wrong for the same reason as *when I will arrive*
- *I'll be angry **if it happens** again* (not *if it will happen*)

START
English Card
*if* or *when* — what decides?
Back: **when = sure to happen; if = possible, may not happen.**
- *I might go out later. **If** I go out, I'll get some bread.*
- *I'm going out later. **When** I go out, I'll get some bread.*
- *If it is raining this evening, I won't go out* (not *when it is raining* — rain isn't certain)
- *Don't worry **if** I'm late tonight* — being late is only a possibility
