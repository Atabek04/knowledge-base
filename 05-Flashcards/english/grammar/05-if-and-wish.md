START
English Card
The three if-patterns differ by *time reference*, not by tense name. What does each one signal? *(three patterns)*
Back: **Real future / unreal present / unreal past.**
- *If we **take** the bus, it **will** be cheaper* — still possible, it may happen
- *If we **took** the bus, it **would** be cheaper* — decided against, imagined now
- *If we **had taken** the bus, it **would have** been cheaper* — over, it didn't happen
- Russian marks all unreal cases with one particle (бы + past), so the past/past-perfect split has no Russian counterpart — it must be chosen from the *time meant*, not felt

English Grammar
Shall we take the bus or the train? If we {1:take} (take) the bus, it will be cheaper.
Explanation: The bus is still a live option, so this is the real conditional: if + present, will in the other half.
- *If we took the bus* would signal she has already ruled the bus out
- The if-part stays present even though the sentence is about the future — never *if we will take*

START
English Card
In *If there was an election tomorrow, who would you vote for?* the verb is past but the meaning is not. What is the past form actually doing?
Back: **Marking unreality, not past time.**
- *If + past* is used when we don't expect the thing to happen: *What would you do if you **won** a lot of money?*
- The time referred to is present or future — *tomorrow* in the example
- Contrast *I'd be surprised if they **didn't** come* — I do expect them to come

START
English Card
In an unreal conditional, what can replace *would* in the main clause, and what does each change?
Back: **could = would be able to; might = it is possible that I would.**
- *If it stopped raining, we **could** go out* — we would be able to go out
- *If I won a lot of money, I **might** buy a house* — possibly, not certainly
- All three take the bare infinitive after them: *could go*, *might buy*

Explanation: An imagined present situation contrary to fact → if + past, would in the main clause.
- *if I know his number* would present it as genuinely possible that she knows it
- The past form describes *now*: she doesn't know it now

START
English Card
What is the difference between *I'm glad I live here* and *I wish I lived here*?
Back: **glad + present = it is true; wish + past = it is not true.**
- *I'm glad I live here* — I do live here, and that's good
- *I wish I lived here* — I don't live here, and I regret it
- *wish* + past always means the opposite of the fact stated

- *I wish there aren't* is not English; *wish* forces the past form
- Same pattern: *I wish I **didn't have to** work tomorrow, but unfortunately I do*

START
English Card
After *if* and *wish*, when may you use *were* with a singular subject?
Back: **Always — *were* and *was* are both correct there.**
- *If I **were** you, I wouldn't buy that coat* or *If I **was** you, …*
- *I wish Anna **were** here* or *I wish Anna **was** here*
- *were* is the more formal choice, which suits IELTS Writing

*could* after *if* and *wish* is ambiguous. What are its two meanings?

English Card
*I'd* appears twice in *If I'd seen you, I'd have said hello* — with different values. What are they?
Back: **The first is *had*, the second is *would*.**
- *If I'd seen* = If I **had** seen — the if-part takes *had*
- *I'd have said* = I **would** have said — the main clause takes *would*
- The contraction hides the split, which is why the full forms are worth rehearsing

English Card
What does *I wish I hadn't eaten so much* tell you about what actually happened?
Back: **It happened, and I'm sorry it did.**
- *I wish something **had** happened* = I'm sorry it did not happen
- *I wish something **hadn't** happened* = I'm sorry it did
- *I'm glad I saw him* (I saw him) vs *I wish I'd seen him* (I didn't)

START
English Card
In a past conditional, what do *could have* and *might have* add that *would have* does not?
Back: **could have = would have been able to; might have = maybe.**
- *If the weather hadn't been so bad, we **would have** gone out* — certainly
- *…we **could have** gone out* — we would have been able to
- *…we **might have** gone out* — maybe we would have

English Grammar
I'm sorry you're not well. I {1:hope} (hope / wish) you feel better soon.
Explanation: *wish* cannot take a clause about an event happening — that is *hope*.
- Not *I wish you feel better*; you wish somebody **a thing** (*luck*, *all the best*, *a pleasant stay*)
- *I **wish** you a pleasant stay* but *I **hope** you enjoy your stay*

START
English Card
When do you use *I wish … would …* rather than *I wish* + past? *(one rule, two sides)*
Back: **would = you want something to happen or change; past = how you'd like things to be.**
- *I wish Sarah **would** come* — I want her to come
- *I wish Sarah **was** here now* — a state, so not *I wish Sarah would be*
- *I wish somebody **would** buy me a car* but *I wish I **had** a car* (not *I wish I would have*)

Explanation: A wanted change in someone else's behaviour → *I wish … would*, the standard complaint pattern.
- *I wish somebody answered it* would describe a state of affairs, not a demand for action
- The negative complains about repeated behaviour: *I wish you **wouldn't** keep interrupting me*

English Card
*I wish I could stay* and *I wish I could have gone* both regret an inability. What separates them?
Back: **could = I can't now; could have = I couldn't then.**
- *I'm sorry you have to go. I wish you **could** stay longer* — you can't, present
- *I hear the party was great. I wish I **could have** gone* — I couldn't go, past
- Same split as *would* vs *would have*: the *have* moves the regret into the past
