Explanation: *enjoy* takes -ing, never *to …*.
- *I enjoy to read* is the error Murphy flags; the same goes for *mind* and *suggest*
- Russian *я люблю читать* uses the infinitive, which is exactly why *to read* feels right here

START
English Card
Which meaning-family of verbs takes -ing rather than *to …*? *(three families)*
Back: **Reacting to an action, avoiding/refusing it, or continuing and stopping it.**
- **Reaction to the activity:** *enjoy, mind, fancy, imagine, consider, recommend, suggest*
- **Avoiding or denying it:** *avoid, deny, admit, risk, postpone, put off*
- **Continuing or ending it:** *keep (on), go on, carry on, finish, stop, give up*
- The common thread: -ing names an activity you are reacting to, not a target you are aiming at

- *I enjoy having not to get up* and *I don't enjoy having* both change or break the meaning
- Same shape after prepositions: *He accused me of **not telling** the truth*

English Grammar
You can't stop people {1:doing} (do) what they want.
Explanation: Some -ing verbs allow **verb + somebody + -ing** — an object before the -ing.
- *stop / imagine / remember / keep* all work this way: *I can't imagine George riding a motorbike*
- With *stop* you may also add *from*: *stop me from doing* — but *stop me to do* is never possible

- *I regret saying that* or *I regret having said that* — identical meaning

English Card
*tell, advise, remind, warn, invite, encourage, persuade, force, teach, allow, enable* share one structure. What is it, and which common verb refuses it?
Back: **verb + object + to … — but *suggest* refuses it.**
- *I wouldn't advise **you to stay** there* · *Can you remind **me to call** Sam?*
- *Jane suggested **that I ask** you for advice* — never *suggested me to ask*
- These verbs also work in the passive: *I was warned not to touch the switch*

Explanation: *make* drops *to* in the active but **takes it back in the passive**.

English Card
*I regret saying that* vs *I regret to say that* — what changes?
Back: **-ing looks back at a past act; *to …* is a formal apology for the bad news arriving now.**
- *I now regret **saying** what I said* — I said it, and I am sorry
- *I regret **to say** that we are unable to accept your offer* — I am sorry I have to tell you this
- The *to …* form is near-fixed: *regret to say / to tell you / to inform you*, letters and announcements

English Card
*I like cleaning the kitchen* vs *I like to clean the kitchen* — what is the difference?
Back: **-ing = I enjoy it. *to …* = I choose to do it, whether or not I enjoy it.**
- *It's not my favourite job, but I **like to clean** the kitchen as often as possible*
- For repeated actions in general, either form works — the split only bites when enjoyment is in question
- *enjoy* and *mind* never take *to …*: *I enjoy cleaning*, *I don't mind cleaning*

*prefer* in general: which forms can follow it, and how does each continue?

- *I prefer **driving to** travelling by train* — -ing form, joined with **to**

*I'm used to driving* vs *I used to drive* — two structures, two meanings. Which is which?
Back: **be/get used to (doing) = it isn't strange for me. used to (do) = a past habit, now over.**
- *I'm used to driving on the left because I've lived in Britain a long time* — present state of familiarity
- *I used to drive to work every day, but now I go by bike* — past only, never present
- Structural tell: *be/get* + *used to* + -ing, versus bare *used to* + infinitive

{1:Having finished} (finish) her work, she went home.
Explanation: **Having + past participle** marks the first action as complete before the second.
- *Finishing her work, she went home* would wrongly make the two simultaneous
- *After finishing her work, she went home* says the same thing more plainly
- The comma after the opening clause is compulsory

English Grammar
{1:Feeling} (feel) tired, I went to bed early.
Explanation: A front-placed -ing clause gives the **reason** — here, *because I felt tired*.
- *Because I felt tired, I went to bed early* is the two-clause version this replaces
- *Being unemployed, he doesn't have much money*
- This is written English; it is rare in speech, which is exactly why it lifts a Task 2 essay

English Grammar
{1:Not having} (have, negative) a car, she finds it difficult to get around.
Explanation: A negative -ing clause puts ***not* first**, before the participle.
- *Having not a car* and *Don't having a car* are both wrong
- *Not knowing what to do, I called my friend to ask her advice*
- With a completed action: *Having seen the film twice, I didn't want to see it again*
