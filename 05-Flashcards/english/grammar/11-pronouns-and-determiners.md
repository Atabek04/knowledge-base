START
English Grammar
I don't want you to pay for me. I'll pay for {1:myself}. (reflexive or object pronoun)
Explanation: Subject and object are the same person → reflexive pronoun.
- *I'll pay for me* is impossible — an object pronoun requires a different person: *Lisa introduced me to the guests*
- the test is mechanical: look at the subject, and if the object is that same person, use *-self*

START
English Card
Which four common verbs never take *myself/yourself* in English, though many languages make them reflexive?
Back: **feel, relax, concentrate, meet.**
- *I feel nervous, I can't relax* — not *I feel myself nervous*
- *You need to concentrate* — not *concentrate yourself*
- *What time shall we meet tomorrow?* — not *meet ourselves*
- Russian marks all four with *-ся* (*чувствую себя*, *расслабиться*, *сосредоточиться*, *встретиться*), which is exactly why the reflexive gets added

English Grammar
He got up, {1:washed}, shaved and dressed. (wash, no reflexive)
Explanation: *wash / shave / dress* are normally used with no reflexive pronoun at all.
- *washed himself* is not wrong but sounds odd — English treats these as intransitive
- the alternative for *dress* is *get dressed*: *He got dressed*

START
English Grammar
Kate looked at Joe, and Joe looked at Kate. They looked at {1:each other}. (two people, one looking at the other)
Explanation: *each other* = A→B and B→A; *themselves* = the pair looked at the pair.
- *They looked at themselves* means both of them looked in the mirror at their own reflections
- *one another* is an exact synonym of *each other*: *How long have you and Ben known one another?*

English Card
*I repaired it myself.* What job is *myself* doing here — it is not reflexive.
Back: **Emphasis: I did it, not another person.**
- *Let's paint the house ourselves. It will be much cheaper.*
- *The film itself wasn't very good, but I loved the music* — the film as opposed to its parts
- this emphatic *-self* can sit next to the subject or at the end: *Lisa herself doesn't think so* or *Lisa doesn't think so herself*

START
English Grammar
I'm going to a wedding on Saturday. A friend {1:of mine} is getting married. (possessive, not *of me*)
Explanation: After *a/some/any + noun + of*, English uses the possessive pronoun, not the object pronoun.
- *a friend of me* and *some friends of us* are both impossible — it must be *of mine*, *of ours*
- the same shape with a name takes *'s*: *a friend of my sister's*, *a good idea of Tom's*

START
English Grammar
I don't want to share. I want {1:my own} room. (own, possessive form)
Explanation: *own* always follows a possessive — *my own / your own / her own*.
- *an own room* is impossible; *own* can never follow an article
- the alternative shape moves the possessive after the noun: *a room of my own*, *problems of his own*

START
English Card
*Paul cuts his own hair.* What does *own* add that a plain possessive would not?
Back: **He does it himself, instead of someone else doing it for him.**
- *Paul cuts his hair* leaves open who holds the scissors; *his own hair* says he does
- *I'd like to grow my own vegetables* = grow them myself instead of buying them
- this is the second sense of *own*; the first is "not shared or borrowed" — *their own house*

START
English Grammar
Jack was sitting {1:on his own} in a corner of the cafe. (alone, *own* version)
Explanation: *on my own* and *by myself* both mean "alone" and are freely interchangeable.
- the rival phrasing here is *by himself* — same meaning, but *by* takes the reflexive and *on* takes the possessive
- mixing them gives the two standard errors: *on himself* and *by his own*

START
English Grammar
I'm sorry I'm late. {1:There} was a lot of traffic. (there or it)
Explanation: *There …* introduces something for the first time and says that it exists.
- *It was a lot of traffic* is the error Murphy flags — *it* must point at a thing already identified
- Russian has no dummy subject (*было много машин* needs no subject at all), so English's two-way choice has nothing to map onto

START
English Card
Once something exists, what decides between *there* and *it*?
Back: **There = it exists. It = a specific thing, place, fact or situation already identified.**
- *There's a new restaurant in Hill Street. It's very good.* — introduce, then refer back
- *I wasn't expecting her to call. It was a complete surprise* — *it* = the fact that she called
- *I like this town. There's a lot to do here. It's an interesting place.*

English Grammar
They live on a busy road. {1:There} must be a lot of noise from the traffic. (there or it, with a noun following)
Explanation: A noun phrase follows, so the existential *there* is required.
- *It must be very noisy* is the rival — correct, but only because *noisy* is an adjective describing the situation
- the mechanical test: noun after the verb → *there*; adjective → *it*

English Grammar
{1:It}'s dangerous to walk in the road. (subject for a *to*-infinitive clause)
Explanation: English puts *it* at the front and pushes the infinitive clause to the end.
- *To walk in the road is dangerous* is grammatical but unnatural — Murphy marks it *(not …)*
- same shape: *It didn't take us long to get here*, *It's not worth waiting any longer*
- Russian says *опасно ходить* with no subject at all, so the missing *it* is the predictable error

English Grammar
{1:It} was windy all afternoon. (weather, adjective follows)
Explanation: Weather, distance and time all take *it*: *It's a long time since we last saw you*.
- but a weather **noun** flips it back to *there*: *There was a cold wind*
- the adjective/noun test again — *windy* is an adjective, *a cold wind* is a noun phrase

START
English Grammar
He's lazy. He never does {1:any} work. (some or any, after *never*)
Explanation: *never* makes the sentence negative, so *any* — the default split is *some* in positives, *any* in negatives.
- *some work* would need a positive frame: *He's busy. He has some work to do*
- *never* is the trap: there is no *not*, but the meaning is negative

START
English Card
Which three positive-looking structures still take *any* because the meaning is negative?
Back: **without, refuse, and hardly.**
- *She went out without any money* (= she didn't take any money)
- *He refused to eat anything* (= he didn't eat anything)
- *It's a very easy exam. Hardly anybody fails.* (= almost nobody fails)
- the rule is not "is there a *not*" but "is the meaning negative"

English Grammar
Would you like {1:something} to eat? (question, offering)
Explanation: In questions, *some-* is used when we ask for or offer things, or believe the thing exists.
- *anything to eat* would be the neutral question — do you want food, maybe yes maybe no
- same choice: *Can I have some sugar, please?* (I assume there is sugar) vs *Do you have any luggage?* (maybe you do, maybe not)

English Grammar
You can take {1:any} bus. They all go to the centre. (it doesn't matter which)
Explanation: The third sense of *any* — "it doesn't matter which", used in a fully positive sentence.
- *some bus* would mean one particular unnamed bus, which is not the point
- same for the compounds: *Anybody could have come in*, *Anywhere. I just want to go out.*

English Grammar
Someone has forgotten {1:their} umbrella. (pronoun after *someone*)
Explanation: *somebody/someone/anybody/anyone* take a singular verb but *they/them/their* afterwards.
- *Someone is here to see you* — singular verb, no exception
- *his or her umbrella* is the formal alternative and reads heavily; *their* is standard
- *If anybody wants to leave early, they can*

English Grammar
We had to walk home. There was {1:no} bus. (no or none, before a noun)
Explanation: *no + noun*; *none* is the form used **without** a noun.
- *There was none bus* is impossible — *none* stands alone: *'How much money do you have?' 'None.'*
- *no* = *not a* or *not any*: *Sarah will have no trouble* = *won't have any trouble*

START
English Card
*no*, *none* and *any* all express zero. What is the grammatical division of labour?
Back: **no + noun · none alone or none of · any after a negative verb.**
- *I have no luggage.*
- *'How much luggage do you have?' 'None.'*
- *I don't have any.*
- so the answer to a question is *None*, never *No luggage* and never a bare *Any*

START
English Grammar
They have {1:nowhere} to live. (nowhere, positive verb)
Explanation: *nothing/nobody/nowhere* already carry the negative, so the verb stays positive.
- *They don't have nowhere to live* is the double negative Murphy flags — correct in Russian (*им негде жить*, *я ничего не сказал*), wrong in English
- the rewrite swaps both halves at once: *They don't have anywhere to live*, *I didn't say anything*, *Jane didn't tell anybody*

English Card
*None of the students … happy.* Singular or plural verb?
Back: **Either — after *none of* + a plural noun both are correct.**
- *None of the students were happy.* or *None of the students was happy.*
- the same freedom applies after *neither of them*: *is* or *are*
- do not carry it over to *every/everybody/everything*, which are strictly singular

English Grammar
There aren't {1:many} tourists here. (countable noun)
Explanation: *many* and *few* go with plural countable nouns; *much* and *little* with uncountables.
- *much tourists* is impossible — the noun decides, before anything else does
- *a lot of / lots of / plenty of* sidestep the split and work with both

START
English Grammar
We didn't spend {1:much} money on the trip. (uncountable, negative sentence)
Explanation: *much* is unusual in positive sentences, especially spoken — it lives in negatives and questions.
- the positive equivalent switches word: *We spent a lot of money*, not *We spent much money*
- but *too much / so much / as much* are fine in positives: *We spent too much money*
- *many* has no such restriction: *Many people drive too fast* is natural

START
English Card
*few* vs *a few*, *little* vs *a little* — what does the article change?
Back: **Without *a* it is negative (not many, not much). With *a* it is positive (some, enough).**
- *He spoke little English, so it was difficult to communicate with him.*
- *He spoke a little English, so we were able to communicate.*
- *She has few problems* (= she's lucky) vs *She has a few problems* (= things aren't going well)
- one letter reverses the meaning of the sentence, which is why this is the quantifier that costs marks

START
English Grammar
Vicky doesn't like living in London. She has {1:few} friends there. (negative meaning: fewer than she'd like)
Explanation: *few* = not many — the sentence is a complaint.
- *a few friends* would flip it into a positive: *I enjoy my life here. I have a few friends and we meet quite often.*
- when the negative reading is intended, *very few* is the more usual written form: *Vicky has very few friends*

English Grammar
Hurry! We only have {1:a little} time. (only + small positive amount)
Explanation: After *only*, the article is obligatory — *only a little*, *only a few*.
- *only little time* and *only few houses* are both flagged *(not …)* by Murphy
- *only* already supplies the "not much" feeling, so the positive *a little* is what pairs with it

START
English Card
When must a quantifier take *of* before the noun, and when must it not?
Back: **Of before a determiner or pronoun; no *of* before a bare noun.**
- bare noun, general: *All cars have wheels* · *Some people learn more easily than others* — never *some of people*
- determiner (*the/this/my/those*), specific: *some of the people*, *most of my time*, *none of this money*
- pronoun (*it/us/you/them*): always *of* — *All of us were late*, not *all us*
- this one rule covers *all, half, some, any, most, much, many, (a) little, (a) few, none* — learn it once

English Grammar
I was ill yesterday. I spent most {1:of} the day in bed. (specific day)
Explanation: *the day* is a determiner phrase, so *most* needs *of*.
- *most the day* is impossible; so is *most of day*
- drop the determiner and the *of* goes too: *Most problems have a solution* — problems in general

Which two quantifiers may drop the *of* even before *the/my/these*?
Back: ***all* and *half*.**
- *All my friends live near here.* or *All of my friends …*
- *Half this money is mine.* or *Half of this money …*
- but before a pronoun the *of* comes back for both: *all of us*, *half of it* — never *half it*

I haven't finished the book. I've only read half {1:of} it. (before a pronoun)
Explanation: Before *it/us/you/them*, *of* is never optional.
- *half it* is the error — the *of*-dropping licence for *all* and *half* stops at pronouns

There are two possible restaurants, and {1:neither} restaurant is expensive. (two things, negative)
Explanation: *both/neither/either* are the two-item set; *neither* is the negative member.
- for more than two the words change: *none of them had a room*
- *the both restaurants* is impossible — *both* never follows an article

START
English Card
*either* has two meanings that look opposite. What are they?
Back: **In a positive sentence, "one or the other, it doesn't matter which". In a negative, "not one and not the other".**
- *We can go to either restaurant. I don't mind.*
- *I haven't been to either restaurant before.* (= I've been to neither)
- the verb's polarity does all the work; *either* itself is neutral

English Grammar
Neither {1:of} the restaurants we went to was expensive. (before *the*)
Explanation: Same *of*-rule as the *all/most* family — a determiner follows, so *of* is required.
- *neither of restaurants* is impossible; *neither restaurant* (no *the*) would be the bare-noun form
- *both* may drop it — *Both these restaurants are good* — but *neither* and *either* may not

English Grammar
{1:Neither} Chris nor Paul came to the party. (paired conjunction with *nor*)
Explanation: The three paired structures are *both … and*, *neither … nor*, *either … or*.
- *either Chris or Paul* would say one of them came; *neither … nor* says no one did
- the pairing is fixed: *nor* only ever follows *neither*, *or* only follows *either*
- *There was an accident, but we neither saw nor heard anything.*

English Card
Why is *All were happy* wrong where *Everybody was happy* is right?
Back: ***all* is not normally used alone — it needs a noun, *of*, or a following clause.**
- *all + noun*: *All my friends were happy*
- *all of + pronoun*: *All of us were happy*
- *we/you/they … all*: *We were all happy*
- *all …* = the only thing(s): *All I've eaten today is a banana*
- so *he knows all* is wrong and *he knows everything* is right

English Card
*each* and *every* often swap freely. What is the difference in meaning when they don't?
Back: **each = things separately, one by one. every = the group, close to *all*.**
- *Study each sentence carefully* (one by one) vs *Every window in the house was open* (= all the windows)
- *each* suits a small number: *There were four books. Each book was a different colour.*
- *every* suits a large one: *She has read every book in the library.*

English Card
*everyone* and *every one* — what does the space change?
Back: **everyone (one word) = everybody, people only. every one (two words) = each individual thing or person.**
- *Everyone enjoyed the party.*
- *Sarah is invited to lots of parties and she goes to every one.* (= to every party)
- so the one-word form can never follow a preposition pointing at things
