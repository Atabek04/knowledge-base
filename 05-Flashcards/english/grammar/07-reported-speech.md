START
English Card
What is the basic shape of reported speech, and what happens to the rest of the sentence?
Back: **The reporting verb is past (*said* / *told me*), and the rest of the sentence usually goes past too.**
- Direct: *Paul said, 'I am feeling ill.'* → Reported: *Paul said that he was feeling ill*
- Both the pronoun and the tense move: **I → he**, **am → was**
- The rival is leaving the inner verb in the present, which reads as though you are quoting rather than reporting

START
English Grammar
Paul said that he {1:was} (be) feeling ill.
Explanation: *said* is past, so the reported verb backshifts: **am → was**.
- *Paul said that he is feeling ill* is only right if he is still ill now (Unit 48A)
- The pronoun shifts with the tense — *I'm feeling ill* becomes *he was feeling ill*, never *I was*

START
English Card
Which present forms change to what in reported speech? *(six pairs)*
Back: **am/is → was · are → were · do/does → did · have/has → had · will → would · can → could**
- Ordinary verbs move to their past form too: *want → wanted*, *know → knew*, *go → went*
- One rule under all of it: **each verb takes one step back in time**
- Modals with no past form (*should*, *might*, *would*) simply stay put

START
English Grammar
Anna said that she {1:had lost} (lose) her phone.
Explanation: The present perfect *I've lost* backshifts to the past perfect **had lost**.
- *she has lost her phone* keeps the direct-speech tense and clashes with the past *said*
- Same step everywhere: *I can't come* → *she couldn't come*; *I'll phone you* → *she would phone me*

START
English Card
Is *that* required after *said* / *told me*?
Back: **No — it is optional and usually dropped in speech.**
- *Paul said that he was feeling ill* = *Paul said he was feeling ill*
- Keep *that* in writing when the sentence is long, so the reader sees where the report begins
- Dropping *that* changes nothing about the tense — the backshift still applies

START
English Card
Direct speech has a past simple (*I woke up feeling ill*). What are your options when reporting it?
Back: **Two are correct: leave the past simple, or move it to the past perfect.**
- *He said he **woke up** feeling ill, so he **didn't go** to work*
- *He said he **had woken up** feeling ill, so he **hadn't gone** to work*
- Unlike the present, which must move, the **past simple has nowhere it is forced to go**

START
English Card
When may you keep the present tense in reported speech, and when must you not?
Back: **Keep it while the situation is still true; use the past once it has changed or finished.**
- Still true: *Paul said that his new job **is** boring* — it is still boring now
- Finished: *Paul left the room. He said he **had to** go* (not *has to go*)
- Where the situation still holds, **both forms are correct** — the past is never wrong, so it is the safe default

START
English Grammar
Paul left the room suddenly. He said that he {1:had to} (have to) go.
Explanation: The reason for leaving is over, so the reported verb must be past.
- *he has to go* implies the obligation is still live, which contradicts the fact that he has already gone
- Contrast *Helen told me she **wants** to go to Canada next year* — she still wants to, so the present survives

English Grammar
Joe, this is a surprise. Rachel said you {1:were} (be) in hospital.
Explanation: What was said is different from what is really true, so the past is obligatory.
- *Rachel said you **are** in hospital* would assert it as still true — and Joe is standing in front of you
- The past tense here does real work: it marks the claim as **reported, not endorsed**

START
English Card
*say* or *tell* — what decides which one?
Back: **Name the listener → *tell*. No listener named → *say*.**
- *Rachel **told me** that you were in hospital* (not *Rachel said me*)
- *Rachel **said** that you were in hospital* (not *Rachel told that*)
- Russian has one verb, *сказать*, used both with and without a dative listener, which is exactly why this pair gets mixed

START
English Grammar
What did you {1:tell} (say / tell) the police?
Explanation: *the police* is the listener, and a named listener takes **tell**.
- *What did you say the police?* is wrong; to keep *say* you need a preposition — *What did you **say to** the police?*
- So the listener can follow either verb, but only *tell* takes them bare: **tell somebody** vs **say something to somebody**

English Grammar
Anna {1:said} (say / tell) goodbye to me and left.
Explanation: *goodbye* is the thing said and *to me* carries the listener, so the verb is **say**.
- *Anna told me goodbye* / *Anna said me goodbye* are both wrong
- The test is the bare object: **tell + person**, **say + words**

START
English Card
How do you report a command or a request?
Back: **tell somebody to do something · ask somebody to do something.**
- Order → *'Drink plenty of water,' the doctor said to me* = *The doctor **told me to** drink plenty of water*
- Request → *'Can you help me?' Jackie said* = *Jackie **asked me to** help her*
- Negative: *I **told Joe not to** work too hard* — **not** goes before *to*, never after

START
English Grammar
I told Joe {1:not to} (negative) work too hard.
Explanation: A reported negative command is **tell somebody not to do** something.
- *I told Joe to not work too hard* splits the infinitive and is not the standard form
- *say* can carry this pattern only without a listener: *Paul **said not to** worry about him* (not *Paul said me*)
