START
English Card
*boring* or *bored* — what decides which ending you write?
Back: **-ing describes the thing that causes the feeling; -ed describes the person who feels it.**
- *Jane's job is **boring**, so Jane is **bored**.* (not *Jane is boring*)
- *Jane is boring* is grammatical but means she makes **other** people bored
- The same split runs through the whole family: interesting/interested, tiring/tired, satisfying/satisfied, depressing/depressed, surprising/surprised, shocking/shocked

- *Julia is interesting in politics* is Murphy's flagged error — it would describe Julia as the source of the interest
- Test: swap in *bored/boring*. If the subject is a person having the feeling, you need *-ed*

Explanation: *We* are the ones feeling it → *-ed*.
- *The movie was disappointing* is the other half of the pair — that describes the film

START
English Card
Two adjectives before a noun — which goes first, opinion or fact?
Back: **Opinion before fact.**
- *a **nice long** summer holiday*, *an **interesting young** man*, *a **delicious hot** vegetable soup*
- Opinion = what the speaker thinks (*nice, beautiful, interesting*); fact = age, size, colour, origin, material
- *a beautiful large round wooden table* — one opinion, then four facts in order

START
English Card
What is the order of fact adjectives before a noun? *(five slots)*
Back: **Size → age → colour → origin → material.**
- *a tall young man* (size → age), *big blue eyes* (size → colour)
- *a small black plastic bag* (size → colour → material)
- *an old Russian song* (age → origin), *an old white cotton shirt* (age → colour → material)
- Size/length goes before shape/width: *a large round table*, *a tall thin girl*

START
English Grammar
She was wearing a long {1:black} dress. (long, black — join with *and* only if both are colours)
Explanation: Two adjectives before a noun take no *and* — *a long black dress*, not *a long and black dress*.
- *and* appears only between two or more **colours**: *a black and white dress*, *a red, white and green flag*
- Russian strings adjectives with commas far more freely, so the bare English sequence feels unfinished

I didn't enjoy {1:the first two} days of the course. (first, two)
Explanation: The ordinal comes first — *the first two days*, *the next few weeks*, *the last ten minutes*.
- *the two first days* is Murphy's flagged error, and it is the order most other languages use
- Same for *the few next weeks* — always *the next few weeks*

START
English Card
After which verbs do you use an **adjective**, not an adverb?
Back: **be / get / become / seem, and the sense verbs look, feel, sound, taste, smell.**
- *You look **tired**.* / *This tea tastes a bit **strange**.* / *The dinner smells **good**.*
- These verbs describe the subject, so they take an adjective, not an adverb
- But how somebody **does** something is an adverb: *Drive **carefully**!* (not *Drive careful*)

Explanation: The word describes the verb *drove*, so it must be an adverb.
- *drove careful* is the flagged error; *a carefully driver* is the same error mirrored
- Rule of thumb: adjective → noun, adverb → verb

- *perfect English* is adjective + noun; *speaks English perfectly* is verb + object + adverb

English Card
Not every *-ly* word is an adverb. Which common ones are adjectives?
Back: **friendly, lively, elderly, lonely, lovely.**
- *It was a **lovely** day.* — adjective, and it has no *-ly* adverb form at all
- To say how something is done with these you must rephrase: *in a friendly way*, not *friendlily*

English Grammar
Two people were {1:seriously} (serious) injured in the accident.
Explanation: An adverb, not an adjective, goes before a past participle.
- *serious injured* is Murphy's flagged error; likewise *The conference was **badly** organised*
- Adverbs also modify adjectives and other adverbs: *reasonably cheap*, *terribly sorry*, *incredibly quickly*

START
English Card
*good* and *well* — which is which?
Back: **good is the adjective, well is the adverb.**
- *Your English is **good**.* but *You speak English **well**.* (not *speak good*)
- Use *well*, not *good*, with past participles: *well-known*, *well-educated*, *well-paid*, *well-behaved*
- *well* is also an adjective meaning 'in good health': *'How are you?' 'I'm very **well**, thanks.'*

START
English Card
*fast*, *hard*, *late* — what is unusual about their adverb forms?
Back: **They have no -ly form; the adverb is identical to the adjective.**
- *a **fast** runner* / *can run **fast*** — not *fastly*
- *Kate works **hard**.* (not *works hardly*)
- *Sorry I'm **late**.* / *I got up **late**.* — but *lately* exists and means *recently*: *Have you seen Kate **lately**?*

START
English Card
*He works hard* vs *He hardly works* — what changed?
Back: **hardly is not the adverb of hard. hardly = very little, almost not.**
- *He tried **hard** to find a job* = he tried a lot, with effort
- *He **hardly** tried* = he tried very little — the opposite meaning
- So *Kate works hardly* does not mean she works a lot; it is simply wrong for that sense

English Grammar
We've only met once or twice. We {1:hardly know} (know, with *hardly*) each other.
Explanation: *hardly* goes **before** the verb.
- *We know each other hardly* is the flagged error
- With *can*: *Your writing is terrible. I can **hardly** read it* = it is almost impossible to read

START
English Card
What does *hardly* combine with, and what do those combinations mean? *(three patterns)*
Back: **hardly any/anybody/anything (= almost none), hardly ever (= almost never), hardly + adjective (= certainly not).**
- *There's **hardly any** difference between them.* / *Hardly anybody passed.*
- *I'm nearly always at home. I **hardly ever** go out.*
- *It's **hardly surprising** that you're tired. You haven't slept for three days.* = certainly not surprising

START
English Card
*so* or *such* — what decides which one you write?
Back: **so + adjective/adverb. such + (adjective +) noun.**
- *The story was **so** stupid.* / *Everything happened **so** quickly.*
- *It was **such a** stupid story.* (not *a so stupid story*) / *They are **such** nice people.* (not *so nice people*)
- Word order: *such a …*, never *a such …*

START
English Grammar
I haven't seen her for {1:such a long time}. (long time)
Explanation: *time* is a noun, so it takes *such*.
- *so long time* is the flagged error — *so long* alone is fine: *I haven't seen her for **so long** I've forgotten what she looks like*
- Same pattern: *so far* / *such a long way*; *so much traffic* / *such a lot of traffic*

START
English Grammar
It was {1:such} nice weather that we spent the whole day on the beach.
Explanation: *weather* is an uncountable noun → *such* with no article.
- *so nice weather* is wrong; *so nice* would need no noun after it
- The *that* is usually dropped in speech: *It was such nice weather we spent …*

START
English Card
Where does *enough* go — before or after the word it modifies?
Back: **After adjectives and adverbs, but before nouns.**
- *I'm not **fit enough**.* (not *enough fit*) / *We've waited **long enough**.*
- *We have **enough money**.* / *There weren't **enough chairs**.*
- It can also stand alone: *We don't need more money. We have **enough**.*

START
English Grammar
The food was too hot {1:to eat}. (eat)
Explanation: After *too … to* / *enough … to*, the object is **not** repeated.
- *too hot to eat it* is the flagged error — *it* is already the subject of the sentence
- Same trap: *These boxes are too heavy **to carry***, *This chair isn't strong enough **to stand on***

START
English Card
*too hard* vs *not hard enough* — what is the difference?
Back: **too … = more than is necessary. not … enough = less than is necessary.**
- *You never stop working. You work **too hard**.*
- *You're lazy. You don't work **hard enough**.*
- With quantities: *There's **too much** furniture and **not enough** space.*

START
English Card
*quite*, *pretty*, *rather*, *fairly* — how do they rank and differ?
Back: **fairly is the weakest; quite and pretty are equal; rather leans negative.**
- *fairly good* = not very good, could be better
- *quite famous* / *pretty famous* = less than 'very', more than 'a little'; *pretty* is informal and mainly spoken
- *rather* usually marks something bad (*rather cloudy*, *rather shy*); with a good thing it means 'unusually': *These oranges are **rather good**.*

START
English Grammar
We live in {1:quite an} old house. (quite, a)
Explanation: *quite* goes **before** *a/an*.
- *a quite old house* is the flagged error
- *pretty* takes the normal position: *Sarah has **a pretty good** job* but *Sarah has **quite a good** job*

START
English Card
*The story is quite interesting* vs *The story is quite true* — why do these two *quite*s mean different things?
Back: **With a gradable adjective quite = fairly; with an absolute adjective quite = completely.**
- Completely: *quite sure, certain, right, wrong, true, safe, clear, obvious, different, impossible*
- *She was **quite different** from what I expected* = completely different
- *not quite* = not completely: *I don't **quite** understand what you mean.*

START
English Card
When do you write *-er* and when *more …*?
Back: **-er for one syllable and for two-syllable words ending in -y; more … for everything longer.**
- *cheap → cheaper*, *large → larger*, *thin → thinner*
- *lucky → luckier*, *easy → easier*, *early → earlier*
- *more serious*, *more expensive*, *more comfortable*
- Adverbs in *-ly* always take *more*: *more slowly*, *more easily*, *more quietly* (not *slowlier*)

START
English Card
Which comparatives are irregular? *(three)*
Back: **good/well → better · bad/badly → worse · far → further (or farther).**
- *He did very badly in the exam – **worse** than expected.*
- *further* (but not *farther*) also means 'more, additional': *any **further** news*
- Superlatives follow: *best, worst, furthest/farthest*

I don't play tennis much these days. I used to play {1:more often}. (often)
Explanation: Adverbs of two or more syllables take *more*, even without *-ly*.
- *oftener* is not used
- One-syllable adverbs still take *-er*: *Can you walk a bit **faster**?*

START
English Card
Which words grade a comparative up or down? *(six)*
Back: **much, a lot, far — much bigger. a bit, a little, slightly — slightly bigger.**
- *I feel **much** better now.* (or *a lot better*)
- *This bag is **slightly** heavier than the other one.*
- *The problem is **far** more serious than we thought.* — *far* here means 'a lot'
- These are the words that turn a bare comparison into a graded one, which is most of Task 1

English Grammar
Sales in March were {1:slightly} higher than in February — a rise of just two per cent. (a very small amount)
Explanation: *slightly* / *a bit* / *a little* mark a small difference before a comparative.
- *very higher* is impossible — *very* never modifies a comparative; the graders are *much / a lot / far / a bit / a little / slightly*
- For a big gap use the other end: *far higher*, *much higher*

English Card
What do *any* and *no* do in front of a comparative?
Back: **They mean 'not even a little'.**
- *We expected their apartment to be big, but it's **no bigger** than ours.* = the same size
- *I'm not waiting **any longer**.* / *Do you feel **any better**?*
- *no more expensive* = the same price, not 'cheaper' — a real reading trap in a comparison

Explanation: A repeated comparative says the change is continuous.
- *more and more* for long words: *I became **more and more** convinced that he was lying*

- *My sister is **older** than me.* (not *elder than me*) — *elder* never follows *be*

Explanation: *not as … as* says the first is less than the second.
- *not so … as* is also possible in negatives: *it's not so cold as yesterday*

Laura's salary is {1:the same as} mine. (identical to)

*You're taller than …* — *me* or *I*?
Back: **than me / as him is normal. than I am / as he can is the full alternative.**
- *You're taller than **me**.* or *You're taller than **I am**.* — but not usually *than I*
- *I can't run as fast as **him**.* or *… as **he can**.*

English Card
When do you use *in* after a superlative, and when *of*?
Back: **in for places, organisations and groups; of for periods of time.**
- *the longest river **in** the world* (not *of the world*), *the youngest student **in** the class*
- *the hottest day **of** the year*, *the happiest day **of** your life*
- A superlative usually takes *the*, and often a present perfect after it: *the most important decision you've **ever made***

This hotel is {1:the cheapest} (cheap) in town — it's cheaper than all the others.

In English, what may never come between a verb and its object?
Back: **Nothing — adverbs, adverbials and place phrases all wait until after the object.**
- *I lost **also** my passport* → *I **also lost** my passport*
- *you'll see **on your left** a supermarket* → *you'll see **a supermarket on your left***
- Russian moves these freely, so this is a genuine interference point, not a stylistic preference

Where do *always / also / probably / never* go relative to the verb?
Back: **Before a one-word verb, after am/is/are/was/were, and after the first of two or more verbs.**
- *Emily **always drives** to work.* / *I cleaned the house and **also cooked** the dinner.*
- *We **were also** hungry.* (not *also were*) / *You're **always** late.*
- *I can **never** remember her name.* / *The meeting will **probably** be cancelled.*

English Card
*still* vs *not … any more* — what is the contrast?
Back: **still = the situation continues. not … any more / any longer = it has changed.**
- *Sally **still** works here, but Lucy **doesn't** work here **any more**.*
- *any more* / *any longer* go at the end; *no longer* goes in the middle: *Lucy **no longer** works here.*
- *no more* is not used this way: *We are **no longer** friends* (not *no more friends*)

English Card
*yet* vs *still* in a negative sentence — what is the difference in feeling?
Back: **yet is neutral expectation; still … not adds surprise or impatience.**
- *He **hasn't replied yet**.* — I expect he will reply soon
- *He **still hasn't replied**.* — he should have replied before now
- Position differs too: *yet* at the end, *still* before the negative

START
English Card
*even if* vs *if* — what changes?
Back: **if makes the action conditional; even if says the condition doesn't matter.**
- *We won't go **if** the weather is bad.* — the weather decides
- *We're going **even if** the weather is bad.* — the weather is irrelevant
- Russian *даже если* covers both halves, so the *even* is easy to drop and the sentence then says the opposite
