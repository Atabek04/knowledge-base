START
English Card
at, on, in for time — what does each one take? *(three levels)*
Back: **The shorter the period, the shorter the word: at → on → in.**
- **at** = a point in the day — *at 5 o'clock, at midnight, at lunchtime, at sunset*
- **on** = days and dates — *on Friday, on 16 May 2012, on my birthday, on New Year's Day*
- **in** = anything longer — *in June, in 2012, in the 1990s, in winter, in the past*

START
English Grammar
I don't like working {1:at} night, but I was woken up by a noise {2:in} the night.
Explanation: *at night* is nights in general; *in the night* is one particular night.
- swapping them is not ungrammatical, it changes the meaning — the trap is that both sound fine
- same family: *at the moment, at present, at the same time, at the weekend, at Christmas* (but *on Christmas Day*)

Explanation: A bare part of the day takes *in*; the moment a day is named in front of it, the whole phrase takes *on*.
- *in the morning / in the afternoon / in the evening* — but *on Friday morning, on Sunday afternoon*
- the day-name wins, because *on* is the days-and-dates preposition

START
English Card
*The train leaves in ten minutes.* What are the two jobs *in + period* is doing here?
Back: **Either "ten minutes from now" or "it took ten minutes".**
- future point: *He'll be back **in** a week* (= a week from now)
- duration of an achievement: *I learnt to drive **in** four weeks* (= it took me four weeks)
- *after ten minutes* is the rival for the first sense and is wrong for a future event counted from now

START
English Grammar
The 11.45 train left {1:on} time, and we got to the station just {2:in} time for it.
Explanation: *on time* = punctual, at the planned moment; *in time* = soon enough, before a deadline.
- *on time* has a schedule behind it — the opposite is *late*
- *in time* has a purpose behind it — the opposite is *too late*: *I got home too late to watch the game*
- *just in time* = almost too late

English Card
*into* — when do you need it, and when does *in* do the same job?
Back: **into = entering. With go/get/put, plain *in* is an accepted alternative.**
- *I went **into** the room and sat down*; *a bird flew **into** the kitchen*
- *she got **in** the car* or *got **into** the car* — both fine

- but buses, trains and planes use *get on* and *get off*, never *into*

English Grammar
I don't know when we'll arrive — it depends {1:on} the traffic.
Explanation: *depend on*, *rely on* — the support is underneath you, not coming out of you.
- *depend from* is a direct rendering of Russian *зависеть от* and is always wrong
- *depends* + *when/where/how* may drop the *on*: *it depends how much it is*
- rest of the *on* group: *live **on** money/food*, *congratulate / compliment somebody **on*** something

START
English Grammar
Do you believe {1:in} God? — but the story can't be true, I don't believe {2:it}.
Explanation: *believe in* = believe it exists or is right; *believe something* = accept it as true.
- *I believe **in** saying what I think* (= I think it's right to)
- *I don't believe in it* about a story is wrong — it would claim the story does not exist
- the rest of the *in* group: *specialise **in***, *succeed **in** doing something*

English Grammar
I prefer tea {1:to} coffee.
Explanation: *prefer one thing **to** another* — English compares with *to*, not *than*.
- *prefer tea than coffee* is wrong; *than* only appears with *would rather*
