START
English Card
What are the two parts of the present perfect simple, and which one changes?
Back: **have/has + past participle.** Only the auxiliary changes for person.
- *I/we/they/you **have** lost* · *he/she/it **has** lost*
- The participle often ends in *-ed* (*finished, decided*) but many are irregular (*lost, done, written*)
- Contractions: *I've*, *he's* — note *he's* can be *he is* or *he has*

English Grammar
Tom can't find his key. He {1:has lost} (lose) it.
Explanation: A past action whose result matters now → present perfect.
- *he lost it* would tell us only about the past, saying nothing about whether he has it now
- Russian has no perfect aspect — *он потерял* covers both, so the past simple feels sufficient

START
English Card
Why is the present perfect called a *present* tense when the action is past?
Back: **It reports the situation now, using a past event as the evidence.**
- *Tom has lost his key* = he doesn't have it now
- *He told me his name, but I've forgotten it* = I can't remember it now
- *Sally hasn't gone out* = she is here now
- The event is past; the claim is present

English Grammar
James is on holiday. He has {1:gone} (go/be) to Italy — he's there right now.
Explanation: *has gone to* = went and is still there (or on the way).
- *has been to* would mean he visited and has already come back — *Amy is back home now. She has been to Italy*
- The pair *gone/been* is the one place where the participle alone carries the whole meaning

START
English Card
*just*, *already*, *yet* — what does each one add to a present perfect?
Back: **just = a short time ago · already = sooner than expected · yet = until now.**
- *I've **just** had lunch*
- *'Don't forget to pay the bill.' 'I've **already** paid it.'*
- *Has it stopped raining **yet**?* — *yet* only in questions and negatives, and it signals you expect the thing to happen

English Grammar
I've written the email, but I haven't sent it {1:yet}.
Explanation: *yet* = until now, in a negative, showing the sending is still expected.
- *already* would claim it was sent sooner than expected — the opposite of the sentence
- *still* would need a different word order: *I still haven't sent it*

START
English Card
What kind of time period does the present perfect describe in *Have you ever been to China?*
Back: **A period that runs from the past up to now — here, the whole of the speaker's life.**
- *Have you **ever** eaten caviar?* · *We've **never** had a car*
- *It's the most boring movie I've ever seen*
- *been to* here means *visited*: *I've never been to Canada*

START
English Card
What are the two parts of the present perfect continuous, and what does it emphasise?
Back: **have/has been + -ing — it emphasises the activity, not a result.**
- *I've been working* · *he's been raining*… *it's been raining*
- Used for an activity that has just stopped: *Why are you out of breath? Have you been running?*
- Or one still going on: *How long have you been learning English?*

- *it's raining* is the present continuous and would claim it is raining at this moment, which the sentence denies
- *it has rained* is possible but reports a completed event rather than the recent activity that explains the wet ground

English Card
Compare *We're waiting* and *We've been waiting for an hour.* What does each one tell you?
Back: **Present continuous = happening now. Present perfect continuous = happening now *and* how long it has run.**
- *Hurry up! We're waiting* — the state of affairs
- *We've been waiting for an hour* — the same state plus its duration
- *We need an umbrella. It's raining* vs *The ground is wet. It's been raining*

*How long* vs *how many* — which present perfect does each take?

- *How long have you been reading that book?* vs *How many pages have you read?*
- *She's been writing emails all morning* vs *She has sent lots of emails this morning*

I've {1:known} (know) about the problem for a long time.
Explanation: *know* is a state verb and has no continuous form, so *how long* takes the simple here.
- *I've been knowing* is impossible, even though *how long* normally pulls the continuous
- Same for *have*: *How long have you had that camera?* (not *have you been having*)

*want* and *mean* are state verbs. What is unusual about them in the present perfect?
Back: **They do take the continuous: *I've been meaning to phone Anna, but I keep forgetting.***
- Most state verbs (*know, like, have*) refuse *been + -ing*
- These two are the footnote exception, and they turn up in natural speech constantly

START
English Card
Which form does *I haven't … since/for …* take — simple or continuous?
Back: **Present perfect simple.**
- *I haven't seen Tom since Monday* = Monday was the last time I saw him
- *Sarah hasn't phoned for ages* = the last time she phoned was ages ago
- The negative measures the gap since the last occurrence, not an ongoing activity

What is the form of the past perfect, and what is it for?
Back: **had + past participle — for what happened *before* a past point already established.**
- *I/he/we **had** gone / seen / finished*, contracted *I'd*, *he'd*
- *When Sarah arrived at the party, Paul had already gone home* — arriving is the story's starting point, going home is earlier still
