START
English Card
What are the two things a countable noun can do that an uncountable noun cannot? *(two)*
Back: **Take a plural, and take a number.**
- *banana → bananas*, *one banana, two bananas*; but never *rices*, never *one rice*
- An uncountable noun has **one form only**: *rice*, *music*, *sand*, *money*, *electricity*
- Russian marks this too (*рис* has no plural), so the category itself is familiar — what is unfamiliar is that English then forces an **article decision** on the countable one

START
English Grammar
Joe goes everywhere by bike. He doesn't have {1:a car} (car).
Explanation: *Car* is singular countable, and a singular countable noun can never stand alone.
- *He doesn't have car* is the error — bare singular countable is impossible in English
- Compare *He doesn't have money*: uncountable, so bare is fine
- Russian and Uzbek allow the bare noun in both cases, which is exactly why this slip is automatic

START
English Card
Which nouns can stand alone with no article, and which cannot? *(three cases)*
Back: **Singular countable — never alone. Plural countable — alone is fine. Uncountable — alone is fine.**
- ✗ *There's been accident* → ✓ *There's been **an** accident*
- ✓ *I like bananas* (= bananas in general)
- ✓ *I eat rice every day* · *There's blood on your shirt*
- So the only form that **forces** a word in front of it is the singular countable

- *a bowl of rice · a packet of rice · a grain of rice*

English Card
*noise*, *paper*, *hair*, *room*, *experience*, *time* — what changes when they become countable?
Back: **The meaning narrows from the general substance to one specific instance.**
- *a noise* = one specific noise · *too much noise* = noise in general
- *a paper* = a newspaper · *some paper* = the material
- *a hair* = one single hair · *long hair* = all the hair on your head
- *a room* = a room in a house · *there isn't room* = space

Drinks like coffee and beer are uncountable. So why is *two coffees, please* correct?
Back: **The countable form means *cups/glasses of* it, not the substance.**
- *I don't like coffee very much* — the substance, uncountable

English Card
*Travel*, *work*, *scenery*, *weather*, *baggage*, *advice*, *traffic* — what is the countable partner of each?
Back: **Every uncountable has a countable twin you use when you need *a*.**
- *travel* → *a trip / a journey* (never *a travel*)
- *work* → *a job* · *scenery* → *a view* · *weather* → *a day*
- *baggage/luggage* → *bags* · *advice* → *a suggestion* · *traffic* → *cars*
- ✓ *What beautiful scenery!* / *What a beautiful view!* — one takes no article, the other must

English Card
*Some* has two meanings with plural nouns. What are they?
Back: **1 — a number of / a few of. 2 — some but not all.**
- *I've seen some good movies recently* (= a few)
- *Some children learn very quickly* (= but not all children)
- With a **pair** noun it means one pair: *I need some new sunglasses*
- Often optional: *I need (some) new clothes*

START
English Card
What is the single decision behind *a sandwich* becoming *the sandwich* in the next sentence?
Back: **First mention → *a*. Once the listener knows which one → *the*.**
- *I had **a** sandwich and **an** apple for lunch. **The** sandwich wasn't very good, but **the** apple was nice.*
- *A man and a woman were sitting opposite me. **The** man was American.*
- This is the master rule of the whole article system, and it has **no Russian counterpart** — Russian marks the same idea by word order, not by a word

Explanation: The hotel has already been introduced, so it is now the known one.
- *a hotel* the second time would mean **a different hotel**, which contradicts the sentence
- *a restaurant* stays indefinite because no restaurant has been mentioned yet

Why is *Can you turn off the light?* correct when no light has been mentioned?

*The government have decided…* — why is a plural verb possible after a singular noun?
Back: **Group nouns can be read as the people in them (= they), not the body (= it).**
- *audience · committee · company · family · firm · government · staff · team*

You are about to write a singular countable noun. What are the three questions, in order?
Back: **1 — Does the reader already know which one? 2 — Is there only one of it? 3 — If neither, use *a/an*.**
- Known from earlier mention, from context, or from a narrowing modifier → *the*
- Unique, superlative, or ordinal → *the*

- A bare singular countable is **never** an option, which is the error Russian and Uzbek habits produce

You are about to write a plural or uncountable noun. What is the one question?
Back: **Do I mean this specific set, or the class in general?**
- Specific set → *the*: *the cars in this car park · the coffee we had after dinner*
- General class → **nothing at all**: *cars have wheels · I don't like coffee*
- The mistake in both directions is real, but for Russian speakers the frequent one is inserting *the* into a general statement
