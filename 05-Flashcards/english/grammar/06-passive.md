START
English Card
What does the passive change about a sentence, compared to the active?
Back: **The active says what the subject *does*; the passive says what *happens to* the subject.**
- Active: *Somebody built this house in 1981* — the doer is the subject
- Passive: *This house was built in 1981* — the thing acted on is the subject
- The object of the active becomes the subject of the passive; nothing else about the meaning changes

START
English Card
Why choose the passive instead of the active? *(two reasons)*
Back: **The doer is unknown or unimportant — or you want the topic in subject position.**
- *A lot of money was stolen in the robbery* — somebody stole it, but we don't know who
- *Is this room cleaned every day?* — it doesn't matter who cleans it
- Use **by** only when the doer matters: *This house was built **by my grandfather***

START
English Card
Does writing in the passive make your English more academic?
Back: **No. The passive is a tool for keeping the topic in subject position, not a register marker.**
- *It was decided by the committee* is not a higher register than *The committee decided*
- The genuine reasons are the ones Murphy gives: unknown or unimportant doer, or continuity of topic
- Choosing the passive with no such reason just adds words and hides the agent

START
English Card
What is the passive made of, in every tense?
Back: **be (in whatever form the tense needs) + past participle.**
- *(be) done · (be) cleaned · (be) damaged · (be) built · (be) seen*
- Only **be** carries the tense; the participle never changes
- Participles usually end in *-ed*, but the frequent verbs are irregular: *built, done, stolen*

START
English Grammar
Many accidents {1:are caused} (cause, passive) by careless driving.
Explanation: Present simple passive = am/is/are + past participle.
- *cause* would make careless driving the subject doing the causing, which reverses the sentence
- *are causing* is the continuous — a process under way now, not a general truth

English Grammar
How much money {1:was stolen} (steal, passive) in the robbery?
Explanation: Past simple passive = was/were + past participle; the thief is unknown, so no *by*.
- *did steal* is the active and needs a doer we do not have
- Question form keeps the auxiliary first: *was ... stolen*, not *was stole*

English Grammar
The situation is serious. Something must {1:be done} (do, passive) before it's too late.
Explanation: After a modal the passive is **be + past participle** — the modal already carries the tense.
- *must do* would demand a subject who does it, and *something* is not that person
- Same shape everywhere: *can't be explained*, *could be heard*, *is going to be built*, *want to be left alone*

English Grammar
I haven't received the letter yet. It might {1:have been sent} (send, passive) to the wrong address.
Explanation: Perfect infinitive passive = **have been + past participle** — a completed action seen from now.
- *might have sent* is active and would accuse the letter of sending something
- *might be sent* points forward to a future sending, not to what already happened

English Grammar
The room looks nice. It {1:has been cleaned} (clean, passive).
Explanation: Present perfect passive = have/has **been** + past participle.
- *has cleaned* is active: the room did the cleaning
- Dropping *been* — *has cleaned* / *is cleaned* — is the usual slip; the perfect needs both *has* and *been*

English Grammar
The vegetables didn't taste good. They {1:had been cooked} (cook, passive) too long.
Explanation: Past perfect passive = **had been** + past participle — done before another past point.
- *were cooked* is the past simple: cooked at some past time, with no earlier-than relation
- *had cooked* is active and makes the vegetables the cook

English Grammar
A new bridge {1:is being built} (build, passive) across the river. It will be finished next year.
Explanation: Present continuous passive = am/is/are + **being** + past participle — in progress now.
- *is built* is the present simple: a general or repeated fact, not this month's work
- *is building* is active and makes the bridge the builder

START
English Card
The passive of the continuous needs an extra word. Which, and where?
Back: **being, between *be* and the past participle.**
- Present: *The room **is being** cleaned at the moment*
- Past: *The room **was being** cleaned when I arrived*
- Without it you get *is cleaned* — the simple, which states a routine instead of an action in progress

START
English Card
Verbs like *give, ask, offer, pay, show, tell* take two objects. What happens in the passive?
Back: **Two passives are possible, and English usually starts with the person.**
- *My grandfather gave me this watch* → *I was given this watch* **or** *This watch was given to me*
- Preferred: *I've been offered the job* · *You will be given plenty of time* · *I was shown a copy*
- Starting with the thing needs *to* before the person: *given **to** me*

English Grammar
I've {1:been offered} (offer, passive) the job, but I don't think I want it.
Explanation: With a two-object verb the passive normally begins with the person, so *I* is the subject.
- Active would be *somebody has offered me the job* — but the offerer is not the point
- *I've been offered to the job* is wrong: the person-subject passive takes the thing directly

English Grammar
Where {1:were} (be) you born?
Explanation: Birth is a past event, so English uses the past: *was/were born*.
- *Where are you born?* is wrong — the present only works for a general statement: *How many babies **are born** every day?*
- Russian *где вы родились* is also past, so the trap is fixing the tense once and keeping it

START
English Card
When can *get* replace *be* in a passive, and when can it not?
Back: **Only when something *happens*; never for a state — and it is informal.**
- Happening: *nobody **got hurt*** · *I don't **get invited** to many parties*
- Not a happening: *Jessica **is liked** by everybody* (not *gets liked*) · *Very little **was known** about him* (not *got known*)
- *be* is safe in every situation, so in IELTS writing use *be*

START
English Card
*get married, get dressed, get lost, get changed* — what are these, grammatically?
Back: **Fixed expressions with *get*, not passives at all.**
- *get married, get divorced* · *get dressed* (= put on your clothes) · *get changed* (= change your clothes)
- *get lost* = not know where you are — nobody lost you
- Nothing is being done *to* the subject by anyone, so no *by* phrase is possible

START
English Card
*People say he is 108 years old.* Give both reporting passives.
Back: **It is said that he is 108. / He is said to be 108.**
- Structure 1 — impersonal **it**: *It is said **that*** + a full clause
- Structure 2 — personal: the subject moves to the front and the clause becomes a **to-infinitive**
- Same verb family both ways: *alleged, believed, considered, expected, known, reported, thought, understood*

START
English Card
How do you convert *It is said that she runs 10 miles a day* into the personal form?
Back: **Take the subject of the *that*-clause to the front, then turn its verb into a to-infinitive.**
- *It is said that **she runs**…* → ***She** is said **to run** 10 miles a day*
- The reporting verb keeps its own tense: *is said* stays *is said*
- *there* moves too: *It is said that **there is** a tunnel* → ***There** is said **to be** a tunnel*

START
English Grammar
It is alleged that he stole a car. → He is alleged {1:to have stolen} (steal) a car.
Explanation: When the reported event is **earlier** than the reporting, the infinitive goes perfect: *to have + past participle*.
- *to steal* would put the theft at the same time as the allegation, or in the future
- Same shift with a passive event: *It is reported that two people were injured* → *…are reported **to have been injured***

START
English Grammar
The strike started three weeks ago. It is expected that it {1:will end} (end) soon. → The strike is expected to end soon.
Explanation: A future event keeps the plain to-infinitive after the reporting verb — no *will* survives the conversion.
- *is expected to will end* is impossible; the infinitive already carries the forward meaning
- Compare the perfect infinitive, which is reserved for events **before** the report

START
English Card
Why are reporting passives worth having in IELTS writing?
Back: **They let you attribute a claim without naming a source.**
- *It is widely believed that…* / *Air travel is expected to double…* — the claim is credited to people generally
- They are the standard voice of news reports: *Two people are reported to have been injured*
- They report a view, they do not endorse it — which is exactly what a Task 2 body paragraph often needs before you disagree

START
English Card
*supposed to* carries two different meanings. What are they?
Back: **(1) people say — same as *said to*. (2) intended, arranged or expected — often not what is happening.**
- Rumour: *It's **supposed to be** good* (= people say it's good) · *He's supposed to have robbed a bank*
- Intention: *The plan **is supposed to** be a secret, but everybody knows* · *You're supposed to be on holiday*
- Context decides; the form is identical

START
English Card
What does *You're not supposed to …* mean?
Back: **It is not allowed or not advised — a rule, not a rumour.**
- *You're **not supposed to** park your car here. It's private parking only.*
- *He's much better, but he's **not supposed to** exercise too hard.*
- The negative kills the "people say" reading entirely

START
English Card
What is the difference between *Lisa repaired the roof* and *Lisa had the roof repaired*?
Back: **The first says she did it herself; the second says she arranged for somebody else to do it.**
- *have something done* = you organise it, you do not perform it
- *Did you make those curtains yourself?* vs *Did you **have** those curtains **made**?*
- Lisa called a builder — so *had the roof repaired* is the true sentence

English Grammar
How often do you have {1:your car serviced} (your car / service)?
Explanation: The order is fixed — **have + object + past participle**. The thing comes before the participle.
- *have serviced your car* is the error Murphy flags; it reads as a present perfect and loses the "arranged" meaning
- Same order everywhere: *having **a garage built***, *should have **that coat cleaned***, *Did you have **it cut**?*

START
English Card
What is the informal alternative to *have something done*?
Back: ***get* something done — same meaning, same word order.**
- *When are you going to **get** the roof **repaired**?* (= have the roof repaired)
- *I think you should **get** your hair **cut** really short.*
- As with the *get* passive, *have* is the safe choice in written English

START
English Card
*Paul and Karen had their bags stolen.* Did they arrange it?
Back: **No — *have something done* has a second meaning: something bad simply happened to you or your belongings.**
- It means only *their bags were stolen*
- *Gary **had his nose broken** in a fight* (= his nose was broken) · *Have you ever **had your bike stolen**?*
- The form is identical to the "arranged it" meaning; only sense decides — nobody arranges a broken nose
