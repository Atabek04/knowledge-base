START
English Card
Some concession and time words take a clause, others take a noun. Which is which? *(two families)*
Back: **Clause (subject + verb): although, though, even though, while, as long as, provided, unless. Noun or -ing: in spite of, despite, during, because of.**
- *Although the traffic was bad, we arrived on time* — clause
- *In spite of the traffic, we arrived on time* — noun
- The two errors this prevents are *despite he was tired* and *during I was studying*
- This one split covers Units 113 and 119 and is the most expensive mistake in Task 2

START
English Grammar
{1:Although} the traffic was bad, we arrived on time. (although / in spite of)
Explanation: *The traffic was bad* is a subject + verb, so the concession word must be a conjunction.
- *in spite of the traffic was bad* is wrong — *in spite of* needs a noun: *In spite of the traffic, we arrived on time*
- Same pair again: *although I was very tired* / *despite being very tired*

START
English Grammar
I couldn't sleep {1:despite} being very tired. (despite / although)
Explanation: *being* is an -ing form, not a clause, so the preposition *despite* is required.
- *despite I was tired* is wrong — with a clause you need *although I was very tired*
- To keep *despite* and still use a clause, add the noun *the fact*: *despite the fact (that) I was tired*

START
English Card
Why is *despite of the rain* wrong?
Back: ***Despite* already contains the *of* — it is *in spite of* but *despite*, never *despite of*.**
- ✓ *in spite of the rain* · ✓ *despite the rain* · ✗ *despite of the rain*
- Same with a pronoun: *despite this she continued working* (not *despite of this*)
- Learners produce this by blending the two forms, so it is worth checking every *despite* you write

English Grammar
We went out {1:although} it was raining heavily. (although / because)
Explanation: The rain is an obstacle the result overcame, so the link is concession, not cause.
- *because it was raining heavily* would explain why we stayed in — the opposite outcome
- Noun version of the same contrast: *We went out in spite of the rain* / *We didn't go out because of the rain*

START
English Card
How do you keep *despite* / *in spite of* in front of a full clause?
Back: **Insert the noun *the fact*: *despite the fact (that) …* / *in spite of the fact (that) …***
- *I didn't apply for the job despite the fact (that) I had the necessary qualifications*
- The preposition still takes a noun — *the fact* — and the clause hangs off *that*
- This is the phrase that lets a Task 2 concession sentence stay formal without switching to *although*

START
English Card
What are the two special things *though* does that *although* does not?
Back: **It can end a sentence, and it takes *even* in front of it.**
- End position, spoken/informal: *The house isn't so nice. I like the garden though.* (= but I like the garden)
- *Even though I was really tired, I couldn't sleep* — stronger than *although*
- *even* alone is not a conjunction: ✗ *even I was really tired*
- Otherwise *though* = *although* and is simply less formal

{1:Even though} I was really tired, I couldn't sleep. (even, emphatic form)
Explanation: *Even* cannot introduce a clause on its own; the conjunction is *even though*.
- ✗ *even I was really tired* — *even* is a focusing adverb, not a linker
- *Even though* is the emphatic member of the *although / though / even though* family

START
English Card
*in case something happens* — what does it actually mean?
Back: **Because it is possible it will happen — so you act *now*, in advance.**
- *Your car should have a spare wheel in case you have a puncture*
- *I'd better write down my password in case I forget it*
- *just in case* marks a smaller possibility: *I don't think it will rain, but I'll take an umbrella just in case*

<!--ID: 1788669089108-->

START
English Grammar
We'll buy some more food {1:in case} Tom comes. (in case / if — we buy the food now, whether he turns up or not)
Explanation: *In case* gives the reason for acting in advance; the action happens either way.
- *if Tom comes* makes the buying conditional — no Tom, no food
- Same split: *I'll give you my number in case you need to contact me* / *You can call me if you need to contact me*

English Grammar
I'll write down my password in case I {1:forget} it. (forget)
Explanation: *In case* is never followed by *will*, even about the future — use a present tense.
- ✗ *in case I will forget it*
- Same restriction as after *if*, *unless*, *while* and *when*
- For a past reason, shift the tense instead: *I gave him my number in case he needed to contact me*

START
English Card
*In case of fire, leave the building.* How does *in case of* differ from *in case*?
Back: **_in case of_ = *if there is …* — a condition, not a precaution.**
- *In case of emergency, call this number* (= if there is an emergency)
- Almost always on notices and instructions, followed by a bare noun
- Plain *in case* + clause is the opposite idea: doing something in advance because something might happen

START
English Card
*unless* — give the one-word paraphrase that never fails.
Back: **_unless_ = *except if*.**
- *You can't go in unless you are a member* (= except if you are a member = only if you are a member)
- It usually rewrites as *if … not*: *Unless we leave now, we'll be late* = *If we don't leave now, we'll be late*
- Because *unless* is already negative, never add a second negative to the clause

START
English Grammar
I'm not going out {1:unless} it stops raining. (unless / if)
Explanation: *Unless* = *except if* — the only circumstance in which I go out is the rain stopping.
- *if it stops raining* would say the rain stopping makes me go out, without excluding everything else
- Note the tense: ✗ *unless it will stop raining* — present tense after *unless* for the future

START
English Card
*as long as*, *so long as*, *provided (that)*, *providing (that)* — what do all four mean?
Back: **_if, on condition that_ — they attach a single condition to a claim.**
- *You can borrow my car as long as you promise not to drive too fast*
- *Travelling by car is convenient provided (that) you have somewhere to park*
- All four take a subject + verb, and all four can open the sentence: *Providing the room is clean, I don't mind which hotel we stay at*
- In Task 2 these are how you qualify a claim precisely instead of overstating it

English Grammar
Travelling by car is convenient {1:provided} that you have somewhere to park. (provided / in spite of)
Explanation: A condition on the claim, followed by a subject + verb — so a conjunction, not a preposition.
- ✗ *in spite of you have somewhere to park* — *in spite of* takes a noun and states concession, not condition
- *providing (that)*, *as long as* and *so long as* would all fit here with the same meaning

English Grammar
Providing the weather {1:is} good, we're going to have a picnic tomorrow. (be)
Explanation: After *unless / as long as / so long as / provided / providing*, the future takes a present tense.
- ✗ *providing the weather will be good*
- The same rule governs *if*, *when*, *while* and *in case*

START
English Card
*unless* and *as long as* both set conditions. What is the difference?
Back: **_unless_ names the one exception that blocks the claim; *as long as* names the one requirement that permits it.**
- *You can't go in unless you are a member* — membership is the exception to the ban
- *You can borrow my car as long as you promise not to drive too fast* — the promise is the price of permission
- Negative framing vs positive framing of the same single condition

English Grammar
{1:As} I walked along the street, I looked in the shop windows. (as / when)
Explanation: The two actions run together in time, and *as* is the word for simultaneity.
- *When I walked along the street* pushes towards one action following the other
- *as* also works over a longer stretch: *As the day went on, the weather got worse*

START
English Grammar
{1:When} I got home, I had something to eat. (as / when)
Explanation: One thing happens after the other, so it must be *when*.
- ✗ *as I got home, I had something to eat* — *as* claims the two happened at the same time
- Contrast: *As I drove home, I listened to music* — genuinely simultaneous

START
English Card
*as* has a second meaning besides "at the same time as". What is it, and what else can say it?
Back: **_as_ = *because*; *since* does the same job.**
- *As I was hungry, I decided to find somewhere to eat*
- *Since it's late and we're tired, let's get a taxi home*
- *David wasn't in the office as he was on holiday* (= because) vs *David lost his passport when he was on holiday* (= during that time)
- Both *as* and *since* read as more formal than *because*, which suits Task 2

START
English Card
*like a palace* or *as a palace* — which, and why?
Back: **_like_, because *like* is the preposition meaning "similar to".**
- *What a beautiful house! It's like a palace* (not *as a palace*)
- It takes a noun (*like a palace*), a pronoun (*weather like this*) or -ing (*It's like walking on ice*)
- ✗ *as this*, ✗ *as walking on ice*

START
English Card
*As a taxi driver, I spend my life in a car* vs *I'm like a taxi driver*. What changed?
Back: **_as_ + noun states what you really are; *like* + noun states a resemblance only.**
- *As a taxi driver* — it is my actual job
- *I'm like a taxi driver* — I am not one, I just get treated as one
- *Many years ago I worked as a photographer* means he really was one
- This is the pair that makes *as* a preposition rather than only a conjunction

English Grammar
I didn't move anything. I left everything {1:as} it was. (as / like — the neutral written form)
Explanation: *As* + subject + verb means "in the same way / condition as", and is the form to write.
- *like it was* means the same but is informal, so it costs marks in Task 2
- Fixed expressions take *as* only: *as usual*, *as always*, *the same as mine* (not *the same like mine*)

START
English Card
Which expressions take *as* and refuse *like*? *(three families)*
Back: **_as usual / as always_ · *the same as* · comment clauses like *as you know / as I said / as she expected*.**
- *You're late as usual* · *Your phone is the same as mine* (not *the same like*)
- *As you know, it's Emma's birthday next week* · *Andy failed his driving test, as he expected*
- The single exception is *say*: *Like I said yesterday …* is possible, though *As I said* is the safer written form

START
English Card
What do *like*, *as if* and *as though* all do after *look*, *sound* and *feel*?
Back: **They introduce a subject + verb describing how something seems.**
- *That house looks like / as if it's going to fall down*
- *Amy sounded like / as if she had a cold*
- *I don't feel as though I've had a holiday*
- With a bare adjective no linker appears at all: *You look tired*, but *You look as if you haven't slept*
- *as if* and *as though* are the written forms; *like* here is informal

English Grammar
He talks {1:as if} he knew everything. (as if / like — the past-tense unreal form)
Explanation: *As if* + past marks the idea as untrue; *like* is not normally used this way.
- The past is not past time — he does not know everything, exactly as with *if* and *wish*
- ✗ *like he knew everything*

START
English Card
*He talks as if he knew everything* — why the past tense about the present?
Back: **The past marks the idea as not real, the same use as in *if* clauses and after *wish*.**
- *As if I didn't have enough to do already* (I do have enough to do)
- *Why do you talk about him as if he was an old man?* (he isn't one)
- With this unreal past you may use *were* for *was*: *as if he were an old man*, *they treat me as if I were their own son*
- Russian marks this with the particle *бы*, which has no English word — the tense shift carries it instead

- Same pair: *during our holiday* / *while we were on holiday*

START
English Card
*during* and *in* both fit *the night*, *the summer*, *the morning*. Where does *during* have no rival?
Back: **With an event rather than a time word — *during the movie*, *during the exam*, *during our holiday*.**
- With time words either is fine: *It rained in the night* or *… during the night*
- *during* means "at some point between the start and the end of" something, which is why it needs a thing with a start and an end

I hope the weather will be good while I {1:'m} there. (be)
Explanation: After *while*, a future is expressed with a present tense.
- ✗ *while I will be there*, ✗ *while you'll be waiting*
- The same restriction as after *if*, *unless*, *provided*, *in case* and *when*

English Grammar
Joe will be back {1:by} Monday. (by / until — he returns no later than Monday)
Explanation: *By* = not later than — a deadline, a single point.
- *Joe will be away until Monday* describes a situation continuing up to Monday, then ending
- Same pair: *I'll have finished my work by 11.30* / *I'll be working until 11.30*

START
English Card
*by* or *until*? Give the one-line test.
Back: **_until_ + a situation that continues; *by* + a deadline the thing happens no later than.**
- *I stayed in bed until half past ten* — the staying ran up to that time
- *They should arrive by Monday* — on or before Monday
- *She should be here by now* (= now or before now, so she is already late)
- *till* is simply the informal spelling of *until*

English Grammar
It's too late to go to the bank now. By the time we {1:get} there, it will be closed. (get)
Explanation: *By the time* is a conjunction of time, so the future takes a present tense.
- ✗ *by the time we will get there*
- The main clause carries the future: *it will be closed*, *the film will already have started*

START
English Card
*By the time she arrived, most of the other guests had left.* Why the past perfect?
Back: **_By the time_ + past sets the reference point, and whatever was already finished before it takes the past perfect.**
- *We went to the cinema late. By the time we got there, the film had already started*
- A state instead of an event stays simple: *I was very tired by the time I finished*
- *by then* and *by that time* replace the whole clause: *she got to the party at midnight, but by then most of the guests had left*
