START
English Card
*can* has how many forms, and what follows from that?
Back: **Only two — *can* (present) and *could* (past).**
- No infinitive, no participle, so *can* cannot follow another verb or *have*
- Wherever a third form is needed, *(be) able to* supplies it: *I haven't **been able to** sleep*, *Tom might **be able to** come*, *Applicants must **be able to** speak two languages*
- In a plain present tense *can* is the usual choice — *We can see the lake* over *We are able to see the lake*

START
English Grammar
Applicants for the job must {1:be able to} (can) speak two foreign languages.
Explanation: *must* already occupies the modal slot, and two modals cannot stack — so *can* is replaced by *be able to*.
- *must can speak* is impossible in English; the same swap happens after *will*, *might*, *have to* and *to*
- Russian *мочь* is an ordinary verb and stacks freely, which is why *must can* feels sayable

START
English Card
*could* as a past ability sits comfortably with which verbs?
Back: **The perception and mental-state verbs: *see, hear, smell, taste, feel, remember, understand*.**
- *We had a lovely room. We **could see** the lake.*
- *As soon as I walked in, I **could smell** gas.*
- With these, *could* is not a hedge — it is simply the past of *can*

English Grammar
The fire spread quickly, but everybody {1:was able to} (can, past) escape.
Explanation: One specific occasion that actually succeeded → *was/were able to* or *managed to*, not *could*.
- *could escape* would claim a general ability to escape fires, not that they got out this time
- *could* is for general ability: *My grandfather **could** speak five languages*

START
English Card
When does the *could* vs *was able to* distinction stop mattering?
Back: **In the negative — *couldn't* works in every situation.**
- *I looked for Max everywhere, but I **couldn't** find him.* (one occasion, still *couldn't*)
- *My grandfather **couldn't** swim.* (general ability, also *couldn't*)
- The ban is only on positive *could* for a single completed success

English Grammar
Jack and Andy played a match yesterday. Andy played well, but Jack {1:managed to} (succeed in) beat him.
Explanation: A single match he actually won → *managed to* / *was able to*.
- *He **could** beat anybody* describes Jack in his prime — the ability, never a particular match
- Test: does the sentence report an outcome or a capacity? Outcome → *managed to*

START
English Card
*could* is not always the past of *can*. What are its three present/future jobs?
Back: **Suggestions, unrealistic ideas, and present possibility.** *(three uses)*
- Suggestion — *'What shall we do tonight?' 'We **could** go to the cinema.'* (*can* also works; *could* is less sure)
- Unrealistic — *I'm so tired, I **could** sleep for a week.* (not *I can sleep*)
- Possibility — *The story **could** be true, but I don't think it is.* (not *can be true*)
Tags: grammar could
<!--ID: 1788669016134-->

START
English Grammar
The story {1:could} (can/could) be true, but I don't think it is.
Explanation: A possibility about *this* particular story → *could*.
- *can* states what is generally possible, not what may be true in one case: *The weather **can** change quickly in the mountains* (in general) vs *The weather is nice now, but it **could** change later*
- Rule of thumb: *can* = it happens; *could* = it might, this time

English Grammar
Why did you stay at a hotel? You {1:could have stayed} (stay) with me.
Explanation: *could have done* = it was possible, but it did not happen.
- *You could stay with me* would be an offer about now or the future
- Same shift throughout: *I'm so tired, I could sleep for a week* → *I was so tired, I **could have slept** for a week*

START
English Card
*I couldn't do something* has two meanings. What are they?
Back: **(1) It would not be possible now; (2) I was not able to, in the past.**
- *I **couldn't** run ten kilometres now. I'm not fit enough.* (hypothetical present)
- *I **couldn't** run yesterday because I'd hurt my leg.* (past inability)
- The present-hypothetical sense also gives *Things **couldn't** be better* and, in the past, *It **couldn't have been** better*

START
English Grammar
You've been travelling all day. You {1:must} (must/can't) be tired.
Explanation: *must* = I believe this is certain, given the evidence.
- *can't* is the opposite pole — *You've just had lunch. You **can't** be hungry already*
- Deduction *must*, not obligation *must*: nobody is ordering you to be tired

START
English Card
What is the structure after deductive *must* / *can't*?
Back: **must/can't + be, be -ing, or a bare infinitive.**
- *You must **be** tired.* / *You must **be joking**.*
- *They can't **know** many people.*
- No *to*, and no *-s* on the verb — the modal takes the whole load

START
English Grammar
I lost one of my gloves. I {1:must have dropped} (drop) it somewhere.
Explanation: A confident deduction about the past → *must have + past participle*.
- *I must drop it* would be a present obligation, and nonsense here
- The negative twin: *Sarah hasn't contacted me. She **can't have got** my message.*

START
English Card
Which negative deduction forms are interchangeable in the past?
Back: ***can't have (done)* and *couldn't have (done)* — both mean "it is not possible that".**
- *Sarah **can't have got** my message.* = *Sarah **couldn't have got** my message.*
- *Max **can't have been looking** where he was going.*
- The trap is treating *couldn't have* as merely "wasn't able to" — here it is a deduction, not an ability

He may {1:be} (be) in his office — I saw his car outside.

- One word, *maybe*, is an adverb meaning *perhaps*: *'Is it true?' '**Maybe**. I'm not sure.'*
- Test: if you can replace it with *perhaps* on its own, it is *maybe*

START
English Card
*may*, *might* and *could* for present possibility — what separates them?
Back: **Almost nothing in the positive; the negatives split hard.**
- *It **may** be true* = *It **might** be true* = *It **could** be true*
- *may not* / *might not* = perhaps it isn't — *She **might not have received** it* (perhaps she did, perhaps not)
- *couldn't have* = it is not possible — *Sarah **couldn't have received** my message. Otherwise she would have replied.*

START
English Grammar
Why wasn't Amy at the meeting? She {1:might not have known} (know, negative) about it.
Explanation: *might not have done* = perhaps she didn't — the possibility is left open.
- *couldn't have known* would rule it out entirely: it was impossible for her to know
- Same contrast in the positive: *She **may have been** asleep* = perhaps she was

START
English Grammar
Take an umbrella with you. It {1:might} (will/might) rain later.
Explanation: Future possibility, not certainty → *may* or *might*.
- *It will rain* asserts it as fact; *I'm going to buy a car* (for sure) vs *I **might** buy a car* (possible)
- Continuous works the same way: *I'll be watching the football* (sure) vs *I **might be watching** it*

START
English Grammar
If they paid me better, I {1:might} (may/might) work harder.
Explanation: The situation is unreal — they do *not* pay well — and unreal situations take *might*, never *may*.
- Everywhere else the two swap freely: *I **may** go to Ireland* = *I **might** go to Ireland*
- This is the single place the choice is forced, so it is the one worth remembering

START
English Card
What does *might as well* mean, and when do you reach for it?
Back: **Do it because there is no better alternative — no reason not to.**
- Missed the bus, next one in an hour: *Shall we walk?* — *We **might as well**.*
- *I'm ready, so I **might as well** go now.*
- *may as well* is equally correct; it is resignation, not enthusiasm

START
English Grammar
What {1:do} (do/have) I have to do to get a new driving licence?
Explanation: *have to* is an ordinary verb, so questions and negatives need *do/does/did*.
- *What have I to do?* and *Karen hasn't to work Saturdays* are both wrong — *Karen **doesn't have to** work Saturdays*
- Because it is a real verb it also has the forms *must* lacks: *I'll have to*, *I might have to*, *I haven't had to*

START
English Card
*must* or *have to*? What decides it?
Back: **Whose necessity it is. Own opinion → either. Outside fact → *have to*.**
- Opinion or recommendation: *I **must** phone her* / *I **have to** phone her*; *You **must** meet him*
- An obligation from outside the speaker: *I **have to** work from 8.30 to 5.30* (a fact, not my view)
- But written rules and instructions take *must*: *Seat belts **must** be worn.*
Tags: grammar must have-to obligation
<!--ID: 1788669016149-->
END

START
English Grammar
I went to the meeting yesterday, but I {1:had to} (must, past) leave early.
Explanation: *must* has no past form, so past obligation is always *had to*.
- *I must leave early yesterday* is impossible — the same gap that forces *be able to* after other modals
- *have got to* is an alternative to *have to*, but only in the present: *I've got to work tomorrow*

START
English Grammar
You must keep this a secret. You {1:mustn't} (must/have to, negative) tell anyone.
Explanation: *mustn't* = don't do it — a prohibition.
- *You **don't have to** tell anyone* would mean it isn't necessary, but you may if you like
- The two negatives are not opposites of one *must*; they are different verbs entirely

START
English Card
*mustn't* vs *needn't* — state both in one line each.
Back: **mustn't = don't do it. needn't = you don't need to, but you can if you want.**
- *You **mustn't** tell Steve. I don't want him to know.*
- *You **needn't** tell Steve. I can tell him myself.*
- The trap is hearing the *n't* as a single shared negation; *needn't* removes an obligation, *mustn't* creates one

English Grammar
We have plenty of time. We {1:needn't} (need, negative modal) hurry.
Explanation: *needn't* is the modal form and takes a bare infinitive.
- The ordinary-verb version keeps *to*: *We **don't need to** hurry*
- So: *needn't do*, but *don't need to do* — never *needn't to do*

START
English Grammar
The restaurant was almost empty. We {1:needn't have reserved} (reserve) a table.
Explanation: *needn't have done* = we did it, and now we know it was unnecessary.
- *We needn't reserve a table* would be advice before the fact
- Compare: *Everything **was** OK. You **needn't have worried**.* (you did worry) vs *Everything **will be** OK. You **needn't worry**.*

START
English Card
*needn't have done* vs *didn't need to do* — what does each tell you actually happened?
Back: ***needn't have done* → he did it. *didn't need to do* → unstated.**
- *He **needn't have got up** so early.* — he got up early, pointlessly
- *He **didn't need to get up** early, so he didn't.* / *… but it was a beautiful morning, so he did.* — both are fine
- *didn't have to* works wherever *didn't need to* does

START
English Card
How strong is *should* next to *must* and *have to*?
Back: **Much weaker — *should* advises, *must* leaves no alternative.**
- *You **should** apologise.* (= it would be a good thing to do)
- *You **must** apologise. / You **have to** apologise.* (= no choice)
- *ought to* is a drop-in replacement for *should*, but keeps its *to*: *Do you think I **ought to** apply?*

English Grammar
Where's Tina? She {1:should} (should/must) be here by now.
Explanation: *should* also covers what is expected but isn't so — she isn't here, and that's not normal.
- *must be here by now* would be a confident deduction that she *has* arrived
- Same use forward-looking: *Helen has studied hard, so she **should** pass.*

English Grammar
You missed a great party last night. You {1:should have come} (come). Why didn't you?
Explanation: *should have done* = you didn't, and it would have been good to.
- *shouldn't have done* is the mirror — *I'm feeling sick. I **shouldn't have eaten** so much* (I did eat too much)
- Present twin: *You look tired. You **should go** to bed now.*

START
English Card
Which verbs and adjectives pull *should* after *that*?
Back: **The demand family and the importance family.**
- Verbs: *insist, demand, recommend, suggest, propose* — *I insisted that he **should** apologise*
- Adjectives: *it's important / vital / necessary / essential that … **should*** — *It's essential that everyone **should be** here on time*
- A separate set — *strange, odd, funny, typical, natural, surprising* — also takes it: *It's strange that he **should** be late*

English Grammar
It's essential that everyone {1:be} (be, subjunctive) here on time.
Explanation: *should* can simply be dropped, leaving the bare infinitive — the subjunctive.
- Full forms: *… that everyone **should be** here* and *… that everyone **is** here* are both also correct
- *I insisted that he **apologise*** = *I insisted that he **should apologise***

English Grammar
What do you suggest we {1:do} (do)?
Explanation: *suggest* never takes an object + *to*-infinitive.
- *What do you suggest us to do?* and *I suggested her to buy a car* are both wrong
- The three legal patterns: *suggest that she **should buy***, *suggest that she **buy***, *suggest **buying***

START
English Card
Besides advice, what else does *should* do at the start of a clause?
Back: **It replaces *if* for a possibility the speaker thinks unlikely.**
- *If the situation **should** change, we will contact you.*
- Inverted, *if* disappears: ***Should** the situation change, we will contact you.*
- Plain *If the situation changes* means the same but treats it as a live possibility

English Grammar
I have to meet Amy in ten minutes. I {1:'d better} (had better) go now or I'll be late.
Explanation: *had better* = advisable, and there is trouble if you don't.
- It takes a bare infinitive: *We'**d better take** an umbrella*, not *we'd better to take*
- Negative is *I'd better not*, with *not* after *better*: *I'**d better not**. It's very expensive.*

START
English Card
*had better* or *should*? Two differences.
Back: **A specific situation, and a consequence if ignored.**
- Specific only: *It's late. You'**d better** go.* — but *You're always at home. You **should** go out more often* (in general, never *had better*)
- Danger attached: *The film starts at 8.30. You'**d better** go now or you'll miss the beginning* vs *It's a great film. You **should** go and see it* (no problem if you don't)
- Despite *had*, it is present or future, never past

English Card
*it's time to go* and *it's time we went* — do they mean the same?
Back: **No. The *to* form is neutral; the past form criticises.**
- *It's time to go home. / It's time for us to go home.* — plain statement
- *It's time he **realised** he isn't the most important person in the world.* — he should have already
- *It's **about** time …* sharpens the complaint further

English Card
What does *wouldn't* mean when it is the past of *won't*?
Back: **Refused to.**
- *I tried to warn him, but he **wouldn't** listen.* (= he refused)
- *The car **wouldn't** start.* (= it 'refused' to)
- Reported speech uses the same shift without the refusal sense: *I'll call you on Sunday* → *Tom said he'**d call** me on Sunday*

When we were children we lived by the sea. In summer we {1:would} (would/might) all get up early and go for a swim.

START
English Card
Requests, offers, permission: which modal does each?
Back: **can/could you → ask someone to act. can/could I → ask to act yourself. can I → offer.**
- Request: *Could you tell me how to get to the bus station?*
- Permission: *Could I use your phone charger?*
- Offer: *Can I get you some coffee?*
- *may* fits the *I* forms only (*May I ask you a question?*) and is more formal

- *Do you think you can take me* is not used, even though bare *Can you take me?* is fine
- The same frame works for permission: *Do you think I **could** borrow your bike?*

{1:Would you like} (like) some coffee? — No, thanks.
Explanation: Offers and invitations use *Would you like*, never *Do you like*.
- *Do you like coffee?* asks about taste in general, not about this cup
