START
English Grammar
The woman {1:who} lives next door to me is a doctor. (relative pronoun, people, not *that*)
Explanation: *who* is the standard relative pronoun for people.
- *that* is also correct here — *the woman that lives next door* — but *which* is never used for people
- from Unit 95 on, some clauses force *who* and forbid *that*, so *who* is the safer default

English Grammar
Grace works for a company {1:that} makes furniture. (relative pronoun, things, most usual form)
Explanation: For things, *that* or *which* — and Murphy notes *that* is the more usual one.
- *who* is impossible: it is a company, not a person
- *which* is not wrong here, but it becomes obligatory only in extra-information clauses (Unit 95)

START
English Grammar
Everything {1:that} happened was my fault. (relative pronoun after *everything*)
Explanation: After *everything / all / anything*, the relative pronoun is *that*, never *what*.
- *Everything what happened* is the classic Slavic-speaker error — Russian *всё, что* uses one word *что* for both jobs
- *what* only works with no noun in front of it: *What happened was my fault* = *the thing that happened*

START
English Card
*what* or *that* in a relative clause — what decides?
Back: **Is there already a noun or *everything/all* in front of the gap?**
- Noun present → *that/which*: *The machine **that** broke down is working again* (not *the machine what*)
- Nothing in front → *what*, because *what* already means *the thing(s) that*: ***What** happened was my fault*
- So *what* is not a relative pronoun at all — it carries its own noun inside it

English Grammar
I met a Canadian woman {1:who} is an English teacher. (one sentence, not *she*)
Explanation: A relative clause replaces the pronoun — you cannot keep both.
- *I met a Canadian woman who she is an English teacher* doubles the subject
- Same trap with objects: *Where are the keys that were on the table?* (not *the keys they were*)

START
English Card
When can you leave out *who/that/which*, and when can you not? *(one rule)*
Back: **Leave it out only when it is the object of the relative clause; never when it is the subject.**
- Object → droppable: *The woman I wanted to see was away* / *Did you find the keys you lost?*
- Subject → obligatory: *The woman **who** lives next door is a doctor* — no version without *who* exists
- Test: put the clause back as a sentence. *I wanted to see the woman* has its own subject *I* → drop it. *The woman lives next door* does not → keep it

English Grammar
The dress Lisa bought doesn't fit her very well. Nothing goes in the gap: *the dress Lisa bought* {1:} — never *bought it*.
Explanation: The dropped relative pronoun already is the object, so no second object may follow.
- *the dress Lisa bought it* and *the keys you lost them* are the standard resumptive-pronoun error
- Russian and Uzbek both allow a resumed pronoun inside such a clause, which is why it sounds acceptable

English Grammar
Do you know the woman Tom is talking {1:to}? (preposition placement, informal)
Explanation: In ordinary English the preposition stays at the end of the relative clause.
- *The bed I slept in wasn't comfortable*, *Are these the books you were looking for?*
- Again no resumptive pronoun: *the man I was sitting next to* (not *next to him*)

START
English Grammar
A widow is a woman {1:whose} husband is dead. (possessive relative)
Explanation: *whose* replaces *his/her/their* — it always has a noun straight after it.
- *who* would mean the woman herself does the verb: *a man **who** knows you* vs *a man **whose** sister knows you*
- Do not write *who's*: that is *who is* or *who has* — *a friend **who's** learning Arabic* vs *a friend **whose** sister is learning Arabic*

START
English Grammar
I recently went back to the town {1:where} I grew up. (relative for a place)
Explanation: *where* covers *in/at/to which* — the place already contains the preposition.
- *the town which I grew up* is incomplete; without *where* you must say *the town **which** I grew up **in***
- Same shape for time and reason with no word at all: *the day I'm going away*, *the reason I'm calling you*

START
English Card
*whom* — when is it actually used?
Back: **Only as the object, and it is formal; spoken English avoids it.**
- Object: *George is a person **whom** I admire very much* — but *a person I admire* is the natural version
- After a preposition it is normal and correct: *friends **with whom** you can relax*
- Never as a subject: *a person whom knows you* is wrong — that is *who*

START
English Card
*My brother who lives in London* vs *My brother, who lives in London,* — what changes?
Back: **The commas change the meaning, not the style.**
- No commas → the clause identifies which brother, so it implies **I have more than one brother**
- Commas → I have one brother and you already know which; the clause is just extra information
- Same for things: *the hotel that you recommended* picks one hotel out of many; *the Park Hotel, which you recommended,* names it and then adds a fact

START
English Grammar
Anna told me about her new job, {1:which} she's enjoying a lot. (extra-information clause, not *that*)
Explanation: After a comma, *that* is impossible — extra-information clauses take only *who* and *which*.
- *John, who speaks French and Italian, works as a tour guide* (not *John, that speaks*)
- Without the comma both are fine: *a company **which/that** makes furniture*

START
English Grammar
This morning I met Chris, {1:who} I hadn't seen for ages. (extra-information clause, object, informal)
Explanation: In an extra-information clause the pronoun can never be left out, even as object.
- Identifying version drops it freely: *This morning I met somebody I hadn't seen for ages*
- Here *whom* is also available and sounds formal: *Chris, **whom** I hadn't seen for ages*

START
English Card
Which relative words work in **both** clause types?
Back: **whose and where — they behave the same with or without commas.**
- *We helped some people **whose** car had broken down* / *Lisa, **whose** car had broken down, was in a bad mood*
- *the place **where** you went on holiday* / *Sweden, **where** her daughter lives*
- Only *that* and the zero pronoun are barred from the comma type

START
English Grammar
Mr Lee, to {1:whom} I spoke at the meeting, is interested in our proposal. (preposition + relative, people)
Explanation: A fronted preposition forces *whom* for people and *which* for things.
- *to who I spoke* is wrong — after a preposition, only *whom* survives
- Move the preposition to the end and *whom* becomes wrong: *Mr Lee, **who** I spoke **to** at the meeting* (not *whom I spoke to*)

START
English Grammar
Helen has three brothers, all of {1:whom} are married. (quantifier + relative, people)
Explanation: After *all of / most of / none of / neither of*, use *whom* for people and *which* for things.
- *all of them are married* as a second sentence becomes *all of whom* inside one sentence
- Things: *They asked me a lot of questions, most of **which** I couldn't answer*; *three jackets, none of **which** fitted him*
- Same pattern with a noun: *a hotel, **the name of which** I don't remember*

START
English Grammar
Joe got the job, {1:which} surprised everybody. (comment on the whole previous clause)
Explanation: To comment on a whole fact, English uses *which*, never *what*.
- *which* here means *the fact that Joe got the job* — it refers to the entire clause, not to one noun
- *Sarah couldn't meet us, which was a shame* (not *what was a shame*); Russian *что* again pushes learners to *what*

START
English Card
What does an **-ing clause** replace, and how do you build one?
Back: **It replaces a relative clause whose verb is active — delete *who/which + be* and keep the -ing form.**
- *the woman **who is** talking to Tom* → *the woman **talking** to Tom*
- *police **who are** investigating the crime* → *police **investigating** the crime*
- It carries no tense of its own: *Who **were** those people **waiting** outside?* is past only because of *were*

Explanation: One clause, not two — the participle absorbs *who are*.
- The full form *Police who are investigating the crime* is correct but longer, and a whole essay written that way reads as a list of simple sentences
- Keep the main verb where it is: *are looking* is still the sentence's verb

English Grammar
The road {1:connecting} (connect, -ing clause) the two villages is very narrow.
Explanation: An -ing clause also covers permanent facts, not only actions in progress.
- Equivalent to *the road **which connects** the two villages* — a present simple, not a continuous
- Same use: *a room **overlooking** the garden*, *a flower **beginning** with T*

START
English Card
When do you use an **-ed clause** instead of an **-ing clause**? *(one test)*
Back: **-ed is passive, -ing is active — ask whether the noun does the verb or receives it.**
- Does it → -ing: *the woman **talking** to Tom* (she talks)
- Receives it → -ed: *the boy **injured** in the accident* (he was injured)
- The -ed clause is a past participle, so irregulars keep their own form: *the money **stolen** in the robbery*, *goods **made** in this factory*

English Grammar
George showed me some pictures {1:painted} (paint, -ed clause) by his father.
Explanation: The pictures receive the action, so the past participle replaces *which were*.
- Full form: *pictures **which were painted** by his father* — the participle clause drops *which were*
- *pictures painting by his father* would say the pictures did the painting

English Grammar
There was a big red car {1:parked} (park, -ed clause) outside the house.

- Active version takes -ing: *There were some children **swimming** in the river*; *Is there anybody **waiting**?*
