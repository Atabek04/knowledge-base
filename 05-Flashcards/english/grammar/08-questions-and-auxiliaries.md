START
English Card
What is the word order in a direct question?
Back: **The subject goes after the first verb.**
- *Tom will* → ***Will Tom** be here tomorrow?*
- *the house was* → *When **was the house** built?*
- Only the **first** verb moves: *Is Katherine working today?* (not *Is working Katherine*)

START
English Grammar
Is {1:Katherine working} (Katherine / working) today?
Explanation: Only the auxiliary *is* jumps in front of the subject; the main verb stays behind it.
- *Is working Katherine?* moves the whole verb phrase, which English never does
- The pattern is fixed: **auxiliary + subject + rest of the verb**

START
English Card
There is no auxiliary in the statement (*you live near here*). How do you make the question?
Back: **Borrow one: *do/does* for the present simple, *did* for the past simple.**
- ***Do** you live near here?* · *What time **does** the film start?*
- ***Did** you sell your car?* · *Why **did** the train stop?*
- The borrowed auxiliary carries the tense, so the main verb goes back to its bare form: *did you **sell***, not *did you sold*

START
English Card
When is *do/does/did* left out of a *who/what* question?
Back: **When *who/what/which* is the subject of the sentence.**
- Subject → ***Who** phoned Emma?* (not *Who did phone Emma*)
- Object → *Who **did** Emma phone?*
- The word order already reads as a statement, so nothing needs inverting — and with nothing to invert there is no work for the auxiliary

English Grammar
What {1:happened} (happen) to you last night?
Explanation: *What* is the subject here, so the verb is used directly with no auxiliary.
- *What did happen to you?* adds *did* where there is no subject–verb inversion to support
- Same shape: *Who **wants** something to eat?* · *How many people **came** to the party?* · *Which bus **goes** to the centre?*

START
English Card
Where does the preposition go in a *who/what/which/where* question?
Back: **At the end.**
- *Where are you **from**?* · *What was the weather **like**?*
- *Who do you want to speak **to**?* · *Which job has Tina applied **for**?*
- Fronting it needs *whom* and sounds formal: *To whom do you wish to speak?*

START
English Card
What does a negative question (*Didn't you …?*) do that a plain one does not?
Back: **It signals surprise, or invites the listener to agree.**
- Surprise → ***Didn't** you hear the doorbell? I rang it three times*
- Seeking agreement → *'**Haven't** we met before?' 'Yes, I think we have.'*
- *Why …?* takes the same shape: *Why **don't we** eat out tonight?* (not *Why we don't eat*)

START
English Card
*'Don't you want to go?'* — what do *yes* and *no* mean in reply?
Back: **They answer the verb, not the negative: *Yes* = I do want to go · *No* = I don't want to go.**
- English ignores the *n't* in the question and reports your actual situation
- Russian *да* / *нет* agree with the question as asked, so *да* to *Не хочешь пойти?* can mean "no, I don't" — the opposite of English *yes*
- Safest fix under pressure: **add the clause** — *Yes, I do* / *No, I don't*

START
English Card
An embedded question (*Do you know where …?*) — what happens to the word order?
Back: **It goes back to statement order: subject before verb, no inversion.**
- *Where **has Tom** gone?* → *Do you know where **Tom has** gone?* (not *has Tom gone*)
- *What time **is it**?* → *Do you know what time **it is**?*
- Triggered by anything the question hangs off: *I don't know … / Can you tell me … / I wonder … / Do you have any idea …*

START
English Grammar
Do you know where {1:Tom has} (Tom / has) gone?
Explanation: Inside a longer sentence the question becomes a clause, so it takes statement order.
- *Do you know where has Tom gone?* keeps the direct-question inversion, which is the single most common error in this unit
- Russian keeps its question word order in embedded clauses — *Ты знаешь, куда ушёл Том?* — so the inverted version feels correct

START
English Grammar
Do you know what time the film {1:starts} (start)?
Explanation: Statement order means the borrowed *does* disappears and the tense returns to the main verb.
- *Do you know what time does the film start?* keeps an auxiliary the clause has no use for
- Same move: *What do you mean?* → *Please explain what you **mean*** · *Why did she leave early?* → *I wonder why she **left** early*

English Grammar
I don't know {1:if} (if / whether — one word) anybody saw me.
Explanation: With no question word of its own, the embedded clause needs **if** or **whether** to join it on.
- *I don't know did anybody see me* leaves the direct question un-embedded
- *whether* is the same meaning in a more formal register: *… whether anybody saw me*

START
English Card
Reported questions and *Do you know where …?* — what do they have in common?
Back: **The same word order change: both turn the question into a statement-order clause.**
- *'Where are you going?'* → *The police officer asked us where **we were** going*
- *'What time do the shops close?'* → *Clare wanted to know what time the shops **closed***
- Reporting adds one thing on top: the verb also **backshifts to the past** (Unit 47)
- So it is **one decision met twice** — embed it, and only then ask whether to backshift

English Grammar
The police officer asked us where {1:we were} (we / be) going.
Explanation: Reported question — statement order first, then the past tense from *asked*.
- *asked us where were we going* keeps the inversion; *asked us where we are going* skips the backshift
- The two errors are independent, so a card you get right by fixing only one of them is still wrong

START
English Grammar
She asked {1:if} (if / whether — one word) I was willing to travel.
Explanation: The direct question *Are you willing to travel?* has no question word, so the report needs *if* or *whether*.
- *She asked was I willing to travel* leaves it in question form
- The *if*-or-*whether* choice is exactly the one from *I don't know **if** anybody saw me* — reported speech does not introduce a new rule here

START
English Card
What is an auxiliary verb for, beyond building a tense?
Back: **It stands in for a whole verb phrase you don't want to repeat.**
- *'Have you locked the door?' 'Yes, I **have**.'* (= I have locked the door)
- *Gary wasn't working, but Laura **was**.* · *Jessica could lend me the money, but she **won't**.*
- With no auxiliary in the original, *do/does/did* fills the slot: *'Do you like onions?' 'Yes, I **do**.'*

START
English Card
How do you contradict *'You didn't lock the door before you left'*?
Back: **With the auxiliary alone: *Yes, I did.***
- *'You're sitting in my place.' '**No, I'm not**.'*
- The auxiliary carries the denial; repeating the main verb sounds heavy and unnatural
- Note again that *yes* answers the fact, not the negative in the accusation

START
English Card
What does *'Oh, have you?'* do in a conversation?
Back: **It shows interest or surprise at what was just said.**
- *'I've just seen Steven.' '**Oh, have you?** How is he?'*
- *'Lisa isn't very well today.' '**Isn't she?** What's wrong?'*
- The tag copies the auxiliary and **keeps the polarity** of the original: *It rained* → *Did it?*, *Lisa isn't* → *Isn't she?*

English Grammar
I passed the exam and so {1:did Paul} (Paul / did).
Explanation: After *so* and *neither* the verb comes **before** the subject.
- *and so Paul did* keeps statement order and is wrong
- *'I'm tired.' '**So am I**.'* · *Sarah can't drive and **neither can** Mark*

START
English Card
How do you agree with *'I never read newspapers'*? *(three ways)*
Back: **Neither do I. · Nor do I. · I don't either.**
- *Neither* and *nor* are interchangeable, and both invert: **verb before subject**
- *not … either* keeps ordinary statement order because the negative is already in the verb
- For a positive statement the equivalent is *So do I*

English Card
What does *so* replace in *'Are those people Korean?' 'I think so.'*?
Back: **The whole clause: *I think **they are Korean***.**
- Same pattern with *I suppose so · I expect so · I hope so · I guess so · I'm afraid so*
- It is the noun-clause counterpart of the auxiliary trick — say the verb, drop what follows

START
English Grammar
'Do you think it will rain?' 'I {1:hope not} (hope, negative).'
Explanation: *hope* and *be afraid* form the negative as **hope not / I'm afraid not**, never with *don't*.
- *I don't hope so* is wrong
- The other group negates the verb instead: *I **don't think** so · I **don't expect** so*
- *guess* and *suppose* go with the *not* group: *I guess not · I suppose not*

START
English Card
How is a question tag built, and which auxiliary does it use?
Back: **Copy the auxiliary from the sentence and flip the polarity: positive sentence → negative tag, negative sentence → positive tag.**
- *Kate will be here soon, **won't she**?* · *Kate won't be late, **will she**?*
- *You haven't eaten yet, **have you**?* · *There was a lot of traffic, **wasn't there**?*
- With no auxiliary, borrow *do/does/did*: *Karen plays the piano, **doesn't she**?*
Tags: grammar question-tags
<!--ID: 1788669016264-->
END

START
English Grammar
You didn't lock the door, {1:did you}?
Explanation: A negative past simple sentence takes a positive *did* tag.
- *didn't you?* repeats the negative instead of flipping it
- The subject in the tag is always a **pronoun**, never the full noun: *Joe should pass the exam, **shouldn't he**?*

START
English Card
*'It's a nice day, isn't it?'* — what decides whether this is a real question?
Back: **The intonation. Voice down = you expect agreement; voice up = you are genuinely asking.**
- Down → *'Paul doesn't look well today, does he?' 'No, he looks very tired.'*
- Up → *'You haven't seen Kate today, have you?'* = *Have you seen Kate today?*
- A negative sentence + rising positive tag is also how English asks politely: *You couldn't do me a favour, could you?*

START
English Card
Which three sentence openings take an irregular question tag?
Back: **Let's … → *shall we* · Don't … → *will you* · I'm … → *aren't I*.**
- *Let's go for a walk, **shall we**?*
- *Don't be late, **will you**?*
- *I'm right, **aren't I**?* — *amn't I* does not exist, so English borrows *aren't*

English Grammar
Let's go for a walk, {1:shall we}?
Explanation: *Let's* is a suggestion, not a statement, so it takes **shall we** rather than a copied auxiliary.
- *don't we?* / *will you?* both treat it as an ordinary sentence
- *will you?* belongs to the imperative instead: *Don't be late, **will you**?*
