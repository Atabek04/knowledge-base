How educational technology succeeds or fails in real schools: adoption, evidence standards, business models, the Kazakhstan context, and the rules on children's data and AI.
The platform needs it because a tool that works in a trial still dies if teachers, buyers, regulators and parents do not accept it.

Parent: none. Read in order; each section assumes the ones above. Teaching progress: not started.

## 1. Why EdTech fails (Core)

- [ ] Classroom technology since the radio promised transformation and mostly changed little, because schools absorb tools into existing routines (Cuban 1986, 2001)
- [ ] Scale learning tools help self-directed, already advantaged learners most (Reich 2020)
- [ ] MOOCs reached mostly educated, affluent learners, and completion stayed low, so open access did not mean equal access (Jordan 2014, 2015; Reich and Ruiperez-Valiente 2019; Kizilcec 2017)
- [ ] inBloom closed in April 2014 after parents objected to over 400 stored student data fields, a lesson in trust before technology (EdWeek 2014; Data and Society 2017)
- [ ] A failure analysis asks who adopts, who pays, who is held accountable, and what must change in the teacher's day (synthesis of Cuban and Reich)

## 2. Teachers as users and adoption models (Core)

- [ ] The technology acceptance model says perceived usefulness and ease of use predict intention to use a tool (Davis 1989)
- [ ] A second meta-analysis of 45 studies finds the model explains only about 39 percent of variance in teachers' intention, so most is left to context (Scherer and Teo 2019)
- [ ] TPACK says effective technology teaching needs content, pedagogy and technology knowledge together, so a tool must fit the subject (Mishra and Koehler 2006)
- [ ] Diffusion of innovations describes adopter groups, and the chasm between early adopters and the pragmatic majority is where products stall (Rogers 2003; Moore 1991)
- [ ] Teachers adopt tools that save planning time or fix a visible problem this week (Cuban 2001)
- [ ] A teacher dashboard helps only when it shows information the teacher can act on within the lesson (Molenaar and Knoop-van Campen 2019; Knoop-van Campen, Wise, Molenaar 2023)

## 3. Implementation science in schools (Core)

- [ ] An intervention that works in a trial fails in schools when it is not delivered as designed, so fidelity must be measured (O'Donnell 2008; Durlak and DuPre 2008)
- [ ] Implementation outcomes such as reach, adoption, fidelity and sustainability are tracked separately from learning outcomes (Proctor et al. 2011; Glasgow 1999)
- [ ] Implementation takes years in stages, so a one-term pilot measures only the first stage (Fixsen et al. 2005)
- [ ] Cognitive Tutor Algebra I showed no effect in year one and about one fifth of a standard deviation in year two, possibly from the whole program (Pane et al. 2014)
- [ ] A pilot protocol should record dosage, teacher training and technical issues alongside outcomes (Pane et al. 2017)

## 4. Evidence standards for EdTech (Core)

- [ ] ESSA defines four evidence tiers, and tiers 1 to 3 need a favourable significant effect for Title I school improvement funds (US Department of Education guidance)
- [ ] The What Works Clearinghouse excludes designs that fail its attrition and design standards however good the results look (WWC Handbook v5.0) (unverified boundaries)
- [ ] Across about 750 randomised trials the median education effect is near 0.1 standard deviation, with benchmarks of under 0.05 small and 0.20 large (Kraft 2020; Simpson 2021 critique)
- [ ] EdTech in K-12 mathematics gives a small positive effect of 0.16, smaller in randomised and large studies (Cheung and Slavin 2013)
- [ ] A logic model links features to mechanisms to outcomes and is the first thing an evidence reviewer asks for (Digital Promise) (unverified)
- [ ] Efficacy under ideal conditions and effectiveness under real conditions are different claims, and ministries want the second (Flay et al. 2005)
- [ ] Pre-registration and independent evaluation make a small pilot credible to funders and ministries (Open Science Framework)

## 5. EdTech business models and sales cycles (Core)

- [ ] B2C scales quickly but faces high acquisition cost and low willingness to pay for abstract learning (Duolingo filings) (unverified)
- [ ] B2B2C sells through an institution to the learner, trading price per seat for access and trust (HolonIQ) (unverified)
- [ ] B2G sells to ministries and districts, with long cycles tied to budget calendars and procurement rules (Digital Promise, CoSN) (unverified)
- [ ] The school user is not the buyer: teacher demand, principal approval, budget and compliance all decide (Reich 2020)
- [ ] District purchases are made by committee over roughly 6 to 12 months, so a spring pilot rarely becomes a contract before the next fiscal year (indicative figures)
- [ ] A free teacher product can win word of mouth that later helps district sales, but selling only to the district buyer risks ignoring the user (Reach Capital)
- [ ] A free pilot earns zero revenue, so it pays off only if designed as both a research asset and a sales asset (synthesis)

## 6. Product metrics that align with learning (Working)

- [ ] Daily active users and time on task measure attention and not learning, and optimising them can reduce learning (Vesselinov and Grego 2012)
- [ ] A learning metric needs a pre-post or comparison design and a validated measure, not completion alone (Kraft 2020)
- [ ] Online dropout is predicted by weak self-regulation, so retention metrics need diagnosis (Huang, Jew, Qi 2023; Hone and El Said 2016)
- [ ] A metric a child can game will be gamed, so pair every engagement metric with a quality check (Goodhart's law)

## 7. Startup and venture fundamentals for EdTech (Working)

- [ ] Customer discovery means asking buyers and users about past behaviour, not whether they like your idea (Fitzpatrick 2013; Blank 2013)
- [ ] Investors ask for evidence, retention, payback and a path past pilots to paid contracts (investor blogs) (unverified)
- [ ] A Kazakhstan pilot aligned with a ministry outcome is fundable through grants and government programs (Astana Hub programme documents) (unverified)

## 8. Case studies (Working)

- [ ] Duolingo reached 58.7 million daily users and 12.7 million subscribers in Q2 2026, while its efficacy study was company-commissioned (Duolingo Q2 2026 letter; Vesselinov and Grego 2012; Shortt et al. 2023; Loewen et al. 2019)
- [ ] Khan Academy's mastery model has good evidence, but real-world gains depend on usage and supervised time (Oreopoulos 2026; Eames 2026)

## 9. UX research with children and teachers (Working)

- [ ] Contextual inquiry means watching users in their real classroom, because teachers cannot describe routines they perform automatically (Beyer and Holtzblatt 1998)
- [ ] Children are best treated as informants or design partners with age-fitted methods, not as small adults (Druin 2002)
- [ ] Consent, assent and school permission must be arranged before any research with minors (local ethics rules)
- [ ] Cultural and language fit for Kazakh, Russian and Arabic users is a design requirement and not a localisation afterthought (needs primary research)

## 10. Kazakhstan school system structure (Core)

- [ ] The Ministry of Enlightenment governs preschool, school and vocational education, while the Ministry of Science and Higher Education governs universities and science (Kazinform, Zakon.kz 2026)
- [ ] Schooling runs preschool, primary, basic and senior secondary levels to grade 11, with grade 12 only in NIS (Law on Education 2007) (unverified)
- [ ] The updated content curriculum shifted standards from knowledge lists to learning outcomes and criterion-based assessment (OECD 2014) (unverified start year)
- [ ] The 2026 Unified National Testing has 120 tasks, a 140-point maximum and 4 hours, with three mandatory subjects plus two profile subjects (National Testing Center)
- [ ] NIS trilingual policy teaches some subjects in English, some in Russian, with Kazakh as core language, and mainstream rollout lacks clear regulation (NIS policy page; Nazarbayev University studies)

## 11. International assessments and Kazakhstan results (Working)

- [ ] In PISA 2022 Kazakhstan scored below the OECD average in mathematics, reading and science, flat versus 2018 in mathematics and reading (OECD 2023 country note)
- [ ] TIMSS 2023 Kazakhstan averages sit below the international average at grade 4, so the belief that Kazakhstan is strong at grade 4 is not supported (Kazinform, secondary)
- [ ] Rural, urban and language-of-instruction gaps explain much of the variance in Kazakh scores (OECD 2014; World Bank 2022) (unverified)

## 12. Kazakhstan EdTech market and digital programs (Working)

- [ ] Four platforms serve state schools (Kundelik, Edus mektep, Bilim All, BilimClass), and Kundelik is used in 15 regions (Informburo)
- [ ] Platform choice is made by regional and city akimats within their authority, so a school pilot needs akimat and school agreement (Deputy Minister Urazova, bes.media)
- [ ] Astana Hub residency reportedly gives tax and foreign-staff relief until 1 January 2029 for qualifying IT activity, though sources conflict on rates (law-firm guides)

## 13. Kazakhstan personal data law (Core)

- [ ] Law No. 94-V of 21 May 2013 on Personal Data and its Protection is the core statute, consolidated to 12 July 2026 (zakon.uchet.kz mirror)
- [ ] Article 12 requires personal data to be stored in a database located in Kazakhstan (Law 94-V; DLA Piper)
- [ ] Article 16 allows cross-border transfer only to states that ensure data protection, otherwise with consent, under treaties or on other legal grounds (Law 94-V)
- [ ] Article 8 lets consent be written, given through a consent service or provable otherwise, and withdrawal must stop processing within 15 working days (Law 94-V)
- [ ] Article 19-1 bars automated processing that changes a person's rights unless the subject consents or a law permits, and objections must be reviewed within 3 working days (Law 231-VIII of 17 November 2025)
- [ ] The Law on Artificial Intelligence No. 230-VIII took effect 18 January 2026 and covers personal data, consumer information and AI media labelling (Stones Law, single source)
- [ ] Law 94-V sets no minimum age for self-consent and no child-specific rule, referring to the subject or the legal representative; family and child-rights law was not checked (unverified)

## 14. Foreign child-privacy regimes as reference (Working)

- [ ] GDPR Article 8 sets 16 as the default age for valid online consent, and member states may lower it to no less than 13 (GDPR text)
- [ ] COPPA covers children under 13 and requires verifiable parental consent (16 CFR Part 312) (age and consent rule unverified in text)
- [ ] The UK Age Appropriate Design Code has 15 standards, including best interests of the child, data minimisation and default settings (ICO)
- [ ] FERPA lets US schools act for parents, a model for the school-as-controller pilot (20 U.S.C. 1232g) (unverified)

## 15. AI safety and fairness for minors (Core)

- [ ] EU AI Act Annex III classes AI that decides admission or evaluates learning outcomes as high-risk (Regulation 2024/1689)
- [ ] EU AI Act Article 5(1)(f) prohibits inferring emotions in educational institutions except for medical or safety reasons, which rules out affect-detecting gamification (Regulation 2024/1689)
- [ ] Algorithmic bias in education arises from skewed data, proxy variables and unequal measurement across groups (Baker and Hawn 2022) (unverified)
- [ ] Common fairness criteria cannot all hold at once, so a platform must choose and document one (Kleinberg et al. 2016; Barocas, Hardt, Narayanan 2023) (unverified)
- [ ] Kazakh and Russian data are thin in large models, risking weaker tutoring for Kazakh-medium pupils (thesis hypothesis, needs a source)
- [ ] [[Automation bias makes people accept a machine's recommendation without verifying it|Automation bias: teachers and pupils over-trust AI feedback, so show uncertainty]]

## 16. Equity in education and EdTech (Working)

- [ ] EdTech tends to widen gaps because advantaged students use tools more effectively (Warschauer and Matuchniak 2010; Reich 2020)
- [ ] The three-level digital divide separates access, skills and outcomes (van Dijk 2005; Scheerder et al. 2017)
- [ ] Rural Kazakhstan schools face connectivity and teacher gaps, so an urban pilot cannot claim national generalisability (OECD PISA Kazakhstan reports) (years unverified)

## 17. Language of instruction and multilingualism (Working)

- [ ] Learners taught in a language they do not speak at home learn less content, the case for home-language instruction in early grades (UNESCO 2016) (unverified)
- [ ] Content and language integrated learning shows modest language gains with possible costs to content learning (Cenoz, Genesee, Gorter 2014) (unverified)

## Contested claims to avoid
- Disruptive innovation will replace schools: predicted in 2008, contradicted by the next decade (Reich 2020).
- MOOCs democratise education: participation skews to educated learners (Reich and Ruiperez-Valiente 2019; Kizilcec 2017).
- A single MOOC completion rate: it depends on how enrolment and intent are counted, so report the definition.
- Duolingo equals a college semester: company-funded study with no strong comparison design (Vesselinov and Grego 2012).
- Khan Academy effect sizes from working papers with Khan-affiliated authors: unreplicated until the peer-reviewed version and PNAS correction are read.
- Acceptance model scores prove a product works: they predict intention to use, not learning.
- Learning styles and digital native arguments in EdTech marketing: not supported by evidence.
- Market size and funding numbers from HolonIQ and similar firms: estimates, order of magnitude only.
- 85 percent of edtech tools are poor fits or poorly implemented: attributed to the EdTech Evidence Exchange but untraceable to it (unverified).
- Chegg stock down 99 percent: aggregator summaries, no price-history check.
- Kraft's benchmarks as a universal scale: derived from randomised trials with standardised achievement outcomes and criticised by Simpson (2021).
- UTAUT explains 70 percent of variance: an adjusted R squared from workplace samples, not teachers.
- Customer discovery shows product-market fit in schools: a few friendly teachers are early adopters and do not represent the majority.
- The ministry was renamed Ministry of Education in 2026: no source found; a September 2026 resolution still says Ministry of Enlightenment.
- Kazakhstan has a GDPR-equivalent law: Law 94-V has no minimum consent age and no child-specific clauses in the text read.
- Kazakhstan is strong in TIMSS grade 4: reported 2023 averages of 487 and 467 are below the international 503 and 494.
- Kundelik is being displaced by a monopolist: allegations from social media and a consortium letter, denied by the ministry and akimats (New Times; bes.media).
- Press figures on UNT, NIS or EdTech revenue: partly estimated, cite the National Testing Center or OECD.
- Vendor claims that content filters make child-facing LLMs safe: no strong independent evidence found.
- Anonymised pupil data is safe to export: re-identification risk in small classes is high, so treat pseudonymised data as personal data.
