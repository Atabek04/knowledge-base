How memory, attention and understanding actually work. Both studying and teaching build on these mechanisms, and every design decision on the learning platform rests on them.

Parent: none. Read in order; each section assumes the ones above. Teaching progress: not started.

---

## 1. Memory systems: encoding, storage, forgetting (Core)

- [ ] Working memory holds only a few items for seconds, so everything a learner knows had to pass through that gate (Cowan 2001; Baddeley and Hitch 1974)
- [ ] Deeper semantic processing at encoding produces more durable memory than shallow processing of surface features (Craik and Lockhart 1972)
- [ ] Retrieval success depends on how well the cue at recall matches the context stored at encoding (Tulving and Thomson 1973)
- [ ] The forgetting curve falls steeply at first and then flattens, and each successful recall flattens it further (Ebbinghaus 1885; Murre and Dros 2015)
- [ ] Most forgetting is failed retrieval caused by interference from similar memories, not decay of the trace (Wixted 2004)
- [ ] Sleep after learning consolidates new memories, so an all-night cram trades away retention (Diekelmann and Born 2010)
- [ ] Storage strength and retrieval strength are separate, so easy retrieval now predicts little about retention later (Bjork and Bjork 1992)
- [ ] Long-term memory is reconstructive, so confident recollections can be wrong without the learner noticing (Bartlett 1932; Loftus 2005)

## 2. Retrieval practice (Core)

- [[Retrieval practice strengthens memory by recalling information rather than re-reading it|Retrieval practice beats re-reading]]
- [[Forward testing effect shows that retrieving prior content frees working memory to encode new incoming material|Forward testing: retrieving old material frees room for new]]
- [[Interpolated retrieval testing after each video segment produces larger retention gains than restudying the same content|Quizzing after each video segment beats restudying]]
- [ ] Practice testing beats restudy with a medium effect in lab studies and in classrooms (Rowland 2014; Adesope 2017)
- [ ] Learners overrate rereading and underrate testing, so they choose the weaker strategy by feel (Karpicke, Butler, Roediger 2009)
- [ ] Feedback after a failed retrieval attempt makes the attempt productive instead of merely discouraging (Butler and Roediger 2008)
- [ ] Retrieval practice benefits transfer to new questions, not only to repeated ones (Pan and Rickard 2018)
- [ ] Retrieval of one item can make related unretrieved items harder to recall, a cost to plan for (Anderson, Bjork, Bjork 1994)
- [ ] Low-stakes quizzing in real classrooms raises later exam scores without raising anxiety (Agarwal et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2021)

## 3. Distributed practice and spacing algorithms (Core)

- [[Spaced repetition schedules vocabulary review at expanding intervals matched to the forgetting curve|Spaced repetition matches the forgetting curve]]
- [ ] Spaced practice beats massed practice for long-term retention across ages and materials (Cepeda 2006)
- [ ] The best gap between study sessions scales with how long the learner needs to remember (Cepeda 2006, 2008)
- [ ] Expanding intervals are no better than equal intervals, so growing gaps is not the source of the spacing benefit (Latimier, Peyre, Ramus 2021)
- [ ] SM-2 schedules each card by multiplying the last interval by a per-card ease factor from self-rated recall (Wozniak 1990)
- [ ] Half-life regression predicts recall probability per word from history and word features (Settles and Meeder 2016)
- [ ] FSRS models memory with stability, retrievability and difficulty, and schedules reviews at a target retention (Ye et al. 2022)
- [ ] Setting target retention near 90 percent balances review workload against forgetting (Open Spaced Repetition documentation)
- [ ] Spaced scheduling without a retrieval step is just a timer: each review must require recall (Kang 2016)

## 4. Cognitive load theory and multimedia design (Core)

- [[Cognitive load theory explains why working memory bottlenecks determine learning speed|Cognitive load: working memory is the bottleneck]]
- [[Worked Examples method reduces cognitive load by showing solved problems before independent practice|Worked examples cut load before solo practice]]
- [[Backward fading removes final worked example steps first to preserve context while building independent problem-solving skill|Backward fading: remove final steps first]]
- [[Expertise reversal effect makes scaffolded instruction counterproductive once learners build sufficient schema|Expertise reversal: scaffolds backfire once schema forms]]
- [[Expertise Reversal Effect means worked examples hurt performance once domain knowledge grows|Expertise reversal: worked examples hurt experts]]
- [[Mayer's Multimedia Learning Principles reduce cognitive load through twelve evidence-based design rules|Mayer's twelve multimedia design rules]]
- [[Pre-training principle reduces working memory split-attention by teaching component names before the main lesson begins|Pre-training: teach component names first]]
- [[Dual coding theory shows that pairing images with verbal explanation creates stronger memory traces|Dual coding: image plus words, stronger trace]]
- [[Visual representations have a larger retention advantage over text at delayed recall than at immediate recall|Visuals beat text more at delayed recall]]
- [[Online delivery increases extraneous cognitive load through self-view split-attention and screen fatigue|Online delivery adds extraneous load]]
- [ ] Intrinsic, extraneous and germane load have different causes, and only extraneous load is cut by better design (Sweller, van Merrienboer, Paas 1998)
- [ ] Novices learn more from studying worked examples than from solving the same problems unaided (Sweller and Cooper 1985; Barbieri et al. 2023)
- [ ] Split attention and redundancy raise extraneous load when related information is separated or duplicated (Sweller 2011; Mayer and Moreno 2003)
- [ ] Minimal-guidance discovery methods fail novices because they overload working memory (Kirschner, Sweller, Clark 2006)
- [ ] Cognitive load cannot be measured by a single self-rating, which is only a proxy (Paas 1992; Sweller et al. 2019)

## 5. Generative learning: self-explanation, elaboration, generation (Core)

- [[Elaborative interrogation deepens schema by asking why facts are true during study|Elaborative interrogation: ask why it is true]]
- [[Generative note-taking that forces paraphrase and reorganization outperforms verbatim transcription|Generative notes beat verbatim transcription]]
- [[Self-generated diagrams produce better transfer than provided diagrams when used as a generation-then-correction sequence|Self-generated diagrams: generate then correct]]
- [ ] The generation effect: producing an answer yourself beats reading it (Slamecka and Graf 1978; Bertsch 2007)
- [ ] Self-explanation prompts raise learning from worked examples and text (Chi 1989; Bisra 2018)
- [ ] Good self-explainers infer missing steps and justify them rather than paraphrasing (Chi et al. 1989)
- [ ] Elaboration helps only when the elaboration is precise and correct (Pressley and McDaniel 1987)
- [ ] Eight strategies make learning generative: summarising, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching, enacting (Fiorella and Mayer 2016)
- [ ] Learning by teaching improves understanding mainly when the learner prepares to teach and explains without notes (Fiorella and Mayer 2013)
- [ ] Generating on top of weak prior knowledge backfires, so generation needs a base to generate from (Kirschner, Sweller, Clark 2006)
- [ ] Generative strategies are the right target for AI tutors that must not simply hand over answers (Bastani et al. 2025)

## 6. Metacognition and self-regulated learning (Core)

- [[Metacognition regulates learning by monitoring whether study methods are actually working|Metacognition: monitor whether methods work]]
- [ ] Metacognition splits into knowledge about learning and monitoring and control of one's own learning (Flavell 1979)
- [ ] Self-regulated learning cycles through forethought, performance and self-reflection phases (Zimmerman 2002)
- [ ] Learners judge their own learning by how easy material feels, and fluency is a poor guide to retention (Koriat and Bjork 2005)
- [ ] Judgments of learning made after a delay predict recall far better than immediate ones (Nelson and Dunlosky 1991)
- [ ] Rereading creates an illusion of competence because recognition fluency is mistaken for recall ability (Kornell and Bjork 2007)
- [ ] Teaching self-regulation strategies raises achievement, more so than general advice (Dignath and Buettner 2008)
- [ ] Generative AI can encourage metacognitive laziness by letting learners skip monitoring (Fan et al. 2025)
- [ ] Scaffolding self-regulation online produces small to moderate gains, mostly on performance not motivation (Zheng 2016; Wong 2019)

## 7. Interleaved practice and desirable difficulties (Working)

- [[Desirable difficulties make learning harder in ways that improve long-term retention over short-term fluency|Desirable difficulties trade fluency for retention]]
- [[Interleaving mixes problem types during practice to build pattern recognition over in-session fluency|Interleaving builds pattern recognition]]
- [ ] Interleaving helps when categories look alike because comparing them teaches discrimination (Brunmair and Richter 2019)
- [ ] Interleaved maths practice lifts delayed test scores sharply while feeling less effective during practice (Rohrer and Taylor 2007)
- [ ] Learners rate blocked practice as better than interleaved practice even when results show the reverse (Kornell and Bjork 2008)
- [ ] Spacing and interleaving are confounded in most field studies, so each effect is harder to isolate than reported (Firth, Rivers, Boyle 2021)

## 8. Schema theory, prior knowledge and expertise (Working)

- [[Zone of Proximal Development defines the teachable gap between independent and guided performance|ZPD: the gap between solo and guided performance]]
- [ ] A schema is an organised unit of knowledge that lets one item in working memory stand for many (Bartlett 1932; Sweller 1994)
- [ ] Experts see chunks and principles where novices see surface features (Chase and Simon 1973; Chi, Feltovich, Glaser 1981)
- [ ] Wrong prior knowledge must be confronted, not merely added to, or misconceptions persist (Chi 2008; Vosniadou 2013)
- [ ] Activating relevant prior knowledge before new material improves comprehension of it (Hattie 2009)
- [ ] Domain knowledge, not general reasoning skill, drives expert performance (Ericsson, Krampe, Tesch-Romer 1993)

## 9. Transfer of learning (Working)

- [ ] Students rarely apply what they learned to superficially different problems (Gick and Holyoak 1980)
- [ ] Near and far transfer differ along content, context, time and modality, so a claim must name which one (Barnett and Ceci 2002)
- [ ] Comparing two worked cases helps learners abstract a principle that carries to a new case (Gentner, Loewenstein, Thompson 2003)
- [ ] Brain-training programmes show almost no far transfer in meta-analyses (Sala and Gobet 2017)

## 10. Attention and distraction (Working)

- [[High affective filter blocks language acquisition even when input is comprehensible|High affective filter: anxiety blocks intake]]
- [[Background music in instructional video occupies the phonological loop and measurably reduces verbal learning|Background music steals the phonological loop]]
- [[Six-minute video threshold marks a sharp decline in learner engagement and retrieval opportunity|Engagement drops sharply after six minutes of video]]
- [ ] Mind wandering in lectures rises over time and lowers later recall of what was said (Risko 2012)
- [ ] Media multitasking splits attention and lowers comprehension, so learning tasks should be single-threaded (Ophir, Nass, Wagner 2009; Wammes 2019)
- [ ] Segmenting a video and adding cues directs attention to what matters and cuts wasted effort (Mayer and Chandler 2001; de Koning 2009)

## 11. Reading science and literacy (Working)

- [ ] The simple view of reading says comprehension is the product of decoding and language comprehension (Gough and Tunmer 1986)
- [ ] Systematic phonics instruction beats unsystematic or no phonics for early decoding (National Reading Panel 2000)
- [ ] Reading fluency bridges decoding and comprehension and responds to repeated oral reading (National Reading Panel 2000)
- [ ] Orthographic depth changes how long decoding takes: shallow scripts are easier than deep ones (Seymour, Aro, Erskine 2003)

## 12. Sleep and learning (Aware)

- [ ] Sleep after learning consolidates memory, and sleep deprivation in adolescents lowers learning (Diekelmann and Born 2010)
- [ ] School start times and late-night app notifications bear on product design (evidence unverified)

## 13. Neuromyths (Aware)

- [[Edgar Dale's Cone of Experience contains no retention percentages — the statistics were fabricated and have no empirical basis|Dale's Cone retention percentages were fabricated]]
- [ ] Matching teaching to learning styles has no evidence of improving outcomes (Pashler 2008; Rogowsky 2015)
- [ ] Left-brain and right-brain personality types lack support from brain imaging, and multiple intelligences lacks empirical support as a basis for instruction (Nielsen 2013; Waterhouse 2006)

## Contested claims to avoid

- Learning styles: no evidence that matching instruction to style helps (Pashler 2008; Rogowsky 2015).
- Learning pyramid and Dale's Cone retention percentages: invented numbers (Letrud and Hernes 2019).
- 10,000 hours of deliberate practice as the cause of expertise: practice explains 26 percent of variance in games, 21 in music, 4 in education (Macnamara 2014).
- Multiple intelligences as an instructional basis: no empirical support (Waterhouse 2006).
- Left-brain and right-brain types: imaging finds no lateralised personality networks (Nielsen 2013).
- Hattie's ranked effect sizes: mixed designs and a calculation error; a map, not a ranking (Wecker 2016; Bergeron and Rivard 2017).
- Germane load as a separate additive load type: later CLT work dropped it (Sweller 2010, 2019).
- Ego depletion and some grit and growth-mindset effects replicate weakly; do not present as settled.
- Smartphone-presence brain drain: the second experiment failed to replicate; a 2024 meta-analysis finds a working-memory cost only (Ruiz Pardo and Minda; Parry 2024).
- Expanding review intervals as the key to spacing: no real difference from uniform (Latimier 2021, g = 0.034).
- Vendor claims that FSRS cuts reviews by 20 to 40 percent: figures differ across sources and the Anki manual gives none.
- Dunning-Kruger as a cognitive deficiency of the unskilled: much of it is regression and better-than-average effects (Gignac and Zajenkowski 2020).
- The forgetting curve as a universal formula: Ebbinghaus was the only subject; replications support the shape, not exact constants.
- Interleaving as always helpful: no or negative effects for text materials (Brunmair and Richter 2019).
- Generative strategies for novices: summarising and mapping often fail without training and prior knowledge.
