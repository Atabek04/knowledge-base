How a startup goes from an idea to a funded, growing company, read from the founder's side of the table.

The module order follows the spine shared by YC Startup School and Stanford CS183B: ideas, users, team, product, growth, money. Accelerators sit near the end, because a demo day judges everything before it.

Each `- [ ]` bullet is a future atomic note. "Learn from" bullets are study material, not notes.

## 1. The founder and the idea

- [ ] A startup is a company built to grow fast, and growth is what separates it from a small business
- [ ] Good ideas come from noticing problems you have, not from brainstorming
- [ ] Founder-market fit: why this team is the right one for this problem
- [ ] Tarpit ideas look easy and attract many founders, and they fail for known reasons
- [ ] An idea is judged by problem frequency, intensity and who pays, not by novelty
- Learn from:
    - Paul Graham, How to Get Startup Ideas (essay, paulgraham.com)
    - YC Startup School, How to Get and Evaluate Startup Ideas (YC YouTube channel)
    - CS183B Lecture 1: Ideas, Products, Teams and Execution (Altman, Moskovitz)

## 2. Customer discovery and validation

- [ ] Customer discovery tests assumptions by talking to customers before building
- [ ] Ask about past behaviour, never opinions about the future (The Mom Test)
- [ ] A commitment (time, money, reputation) is the only real validation signal
- [ ] The Business Model Canvas lays out the nine assumptions a business rests on
- [ ] Jobs to be done: customers hire a product to make progress in a situation
- Learn from:
    - Rob Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test
    - Steve Blank, How to Build a Startup (free, Udacity) and The Startup Owner's Manual
    - Osterwalder, Business Model Generation
    - CS183B: How to Run a User Interview (Emmett Shear)
    - YC Startup School, How to Talk to Users (YC YouTube channel)

## 3. Co-founders and the founding team

- [ ] Most investors prefer a team over a solo founder, and why
- [ ] Co-founder conflict is a leading cause of early startup death
- [ ] Equity split among co-founders, and vesting with a cliff protects it
- [ ] Choosing a co-founder: shared values, complementary skills, prior work together
- Learn from:
    - Noam Wasserman, The Founder's Dilemmas
    - YC Co-Founder Matching and the YC talks on splitting equity

## 4. MVP and product-market fit

- [ ] An MVP is the smallest thing that tests the riskiest assumption
- [ ] Launch early: an unlaunched product teaches nothing
- [ ] Product-market fit is when the market pulls the product out of you
- [ ] Retention curves that flatten are the clearest sign of product-market fit
- [ ] The 40 percent "very disappointed" survey as a fit test
- Learn from:
    - Eric Ries, The Lean Startup
    - YC Startup School, How to Plan an MVP (Michael Seibel)
    - YC Startup School, How to Find Product Market Fit (Peter Reinhardt, Segment)
    - Marc Andreessen, The Only Thing That Matters (pmarchive essay)

## 5. Go-to-market, sales and growth

- [ ] Do things that don't scale to get the first users
- [ ] Founder-led sales comes before hiring salespeople
- [ ] B2B sales: pipeline, champion, buyer, pilot to paid
- [ ] Positioning: the category you claim decides who compares you to what
- [ ] Pick one or two traction channels and test them, not all at once
- [ ] Pricing: charge more than feels comfortable, price on value
- Learn from:
    - Paul Graham, Do Things That Don't Scale (essay)
    - Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, Traction (the 19 channels)
    - April Dunford, Obviously Awesome
    - YC Startup School, How to Get Your First Customers and How to Price (YC YouTube channel)

## 6. Metrics and unit economics

- [ ] Pick one primary metric and measure weekly growth on it
- [ ] Unit economics: LTV must comfortably exceed CAC
- [ ] Burn rate and runway decide when you must raise
- [ ] Default alive vs default dead
- [ ] MRR, churn and net revenue retention for subscription businesses
- Learn from:
    - Paul Graham, Default Alive or Default Dead and Startup equals Growth (essays)
    - YC Startup School, How to Set KPIs and Goals (YC YouTube channel)
    - Accounting and cost-behaviour basics in [[Business Fundamentals MOC]]

## 7. Legal, incorporation and equity

- [ ] Where to incorporate decides which investors can fund you
- [ ] Founder shares vest, and the cliff protects the remaining founders
- [ ] An employee option pool is carved out of founder equity
- [ ] Intellectual property must be assigned to the company
- Learn from:
    - CS183B: Legal and Accounting Basics for Startups (Nathoo, Levy)
    - Feld and Mendelson, Venture Deals

## 8. Venture vocabulary and fundraising

- [ ] Pre-seed, seed and Series A differ by what the company must already prove
- [ ] Venture returns follow a power law, so funds only back outcomes that could return the fund
- [ ] A SAFE is a right to future equity that converts at the next priced round
- [ ] A post-money valuation cap fixes the investor's ownership as investment divided by cap
- [ ] Dilution: every new SAFE on post-money terms comes out of the founders' share
- [ ] A cap table records who owns what after each round
- [ ] A term sheet splits into economic terms and control terms
- [ ] Venture funds need an exit inside their fund life, which sets the horizon they judge you on
- [ ] Angels, micro-VCs, VCs and corporate investors want different things
- [ ] Run a fundraise as a short, parallel process, not one meeting at a time
- Learn from:
    - Feld and Mendelson, Venture Deals
    - Paul Graham, How to Raise Money (essay)
    - YC Startup School, fundraising talks (Geoff Ralston and others) and YC's SAFE user guide
    - Carta, pre-money vs post-money SAFEs, and Carta SAFE cap data

## 9. Pitching

- [ ] A pitch deck answers problem, solution, market, traction, team and ask, in that order
- [ ] Say what you do in one sentence a stranger understands
- [ ] Size the reachable market, not the global one: does 1 to 2 percent of it make a unicorn
- [ ] A moat is whatever an AI-assisted clone cannot copy: network, sales channel, distribution
- Learn from:
    - YC Startup School, How to Pitch Your Startup (Michael Seibel)
    - YC Seed deck template and the YC demo day videos
    - CS183B: Sales, marketing, talking to investors, investor roleplay

## 10. Accelerators

#### What an accelerator is

- [ ] An accelerator is a fixed-term cohort school attached to a fund, ending in a demo day
- [ ] Money-upfront accelerators fund on entry; demo-day accelerators fund only a winning share of the cohort
- [ ] Vertical and regional accelerators filter applicants by industry, market or geography
- [ ] A typical programme runs 10 weeks to 3 months with 1 to 5 events a week
- [ ] Accelerators speed up ventures through intensive paced consultation, not only through selection

#### How a demo-day accelerator picks whom to fund

- [ ] Ten weeks is too short for traction, so judges score founders, moat and market instead
- [ ] Founder-problem fit: can this team build, sell, and stay pleasant to work with until exit
- [ ] Programme exclusions (crypto, gambling, defence, no local plan) reject before merit is read
- [ ] Investors bucket a startup into a category within seconds, and some buckets are automatic rejections
- [ ] If the pitch keeps needing "we are not X, we are Y", the positioning is wrong

#### What an accelerator is worth

- [ ] The lasting asset of an accelerator is the cohort and fund network, not the cheque
- [ ] An accelerator doubles as an express course in venture language and pitching
- [ ] The limits: small cheques, thin co-founder matching, repetitive content after the first programme

## 11. Hiring, culture and management

- [ ] Hire slowly at first: early hires set the culture
- [ ] Culture is what the company rewards and tolerates, not what it writes down
- [ ] Wartime and peacetime CEOs need different behaviour
- [ ] Moving from founder to manager: delegation and one-on-ones
- Learn from:
    - Ben Horowitz, The Hard Thing About Hard Things
    - Andy Grove, High Output Management
    - CS183B: Culture and Team (Lin, Chesky), How to Operate (Rabois), How to Manage (Horowitz)

## 12. Building a startup in the AI era

Study after modules 4 to 8: every topic here revises one of them for a world where code is cheap.

#### What changed

- [ ] When code is cheap to write, building stops being the bottleneck and distribution becomes it
- [ ] AI-native companies run with far fewer people per dollar of revenue
- [ ] Investors now ask whether a vibe-coded clone could copy you in a weekend

#### Moats when software is cheap

- [ ] A thin wrapper over a foundation model has no moat, because the model is everyone's
- [ ] Proprietary data that the product generates through use compounds into a moat
- [ ] Workflow lock-in: the rules, integrations and history customers build inside your product
- [ ] Distribution, brand and trust survive commoditization when code does not
- [ ] Vertical AI defends through domain data, deep integration and compliance barriers

#### SaaS economics after AI

- [ ] Inference is a real cost of goods, so AI gross margins sit below classic SaaS margins
- [ ] Agents shrink seat counts, which breaks per-seat pricing
- [ ] Usage, outcome and hybrid pricing, and why hybrid is winning
- [ ] Outcome pricing needs an outcome both sides can measure and attribute
- [ ] Moving down the stack (own models, routing, caching) to defend margins

#### Building the product

- [ ] Vibe coding is good for prototypes and validation, and dangerous as the production codebase
- [ ] The engineering bar moves to evals, reliability, security and cost control
- [ ] Sell the job done, not the AI: customers buy outcomes
- [ ] Pick problems where models keep improving in your favour, not against you

- Learn from:
    - Bessemer Venture Partners, State of the Cloud and State of AI reports (annual, free)
    - ICONIQ, State of AI report (inference cost and margin data)
    - Kyle Poyar, Growth Unhinged newsletter (AI pricing and monetization data)
    - YC YouTube channel and the Lightcone podcast (YC partners on what current batches build)
    - J.P. Morgan, Vibe Coding: A Guide for Startups and Founders
    - Startups for the Rest of Us, episode 855: the eight SaaS moats that still work in the age of AI
    - Deloitte TMT Predictions 2026: SaaS and AI agents

#### Data points (2025 to 2026, check before citing)

- Bessemer: scaled AI-native companies average about $1.13M revenue per employee, against about $283K for the median public software company; early-stage AI companies are about the same as SaaS peers
- Lovable: reported $400M ARR with 146 employees in early 2026 (Forbes, citing TechCrunch)
- Pricing: seat-based pricing fell from 21 to 15 percent of companies in a year while hybrid rose from 27 to 41 percent (Growth Unhinged, 2025)
- Intercom Fin charges $0.99 per resolved conversation and nothing for failures: the standard outcome-pricing example
- Margins: AI gross margins of 50 to 60 percent vs 80 to 90 percent for classic SaaS (Bessemer, via secondary sources); ICONIQ reports inference at about 23 percent of revenue for scaling AI B2B companies
- YC W26 batch AI share: reported between 60 and 80+ percent by secondary sources; verify on YC's own directory

## Accelerator programmes

- Antler residency (Australia structure): weeks 1 to 3 co-founder matching, 4 to 6 validation, 7 to 10 pitch and investment committee
    - Terms differ by region and change often; read the current local terms page
    - Funding share: third-party estimates say 20 to 40 percent of residency teams get the cheque, and well under 5 percent of applicants
- MYStartup Accelerator (Cradle with NEXEA, Malaysia): demo day, NEXEA angel investment, CIP Sprint grant route afterwards
    - Top 20 selected; content is mentors, corporate partners, masterclasses, grant and follow-on access; top 5 get cash prizes
    - Length is listed as four months in press coverage and six months on NEXEA's page; no public week-by-week syllabus
    - Cohort 5 (2025): 8 finalists from 747 applicants, 4 funded with RM800,000 total by NEXEA
- Generali GROW 2026 (Generali Malaysia, first edition, shaped with Artem PACE): about 10 weeks, September to November 2026
    - For Malaysian insurtech, healthtech and medtech startups, seed to Series A, with an MVP or live product
    - Workshops, 1:1 mentoring, industry visits, pitch coaching
    - Topics: product refinement, the insurance value chain, go-to-market, compliance, governance, financial planning, fundraising
    - Ends with a demo day pitched to Generali and a judging panel

## Data and research

- Exit horizon: CB Insights found the 100 largest US tech exits of 2009 to 2016 took a median of 6 to 7 years from first funding; M&A exits run shorter (about 5 years), IPOs longer (about 8)
    - These figures are from 2009 to 2016 data; check WilmerHale or PitchBook for current medians
- Hallen, Cohen and Bingham (2020), Organization Science 31(2), Do Accelerators Work? If So, How? [doi:10.1287/orsc.2019.1304](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2019.1304)
    - Some top accelerators measurably speed up ventures, not all; the mechanism is broad, intensive, paced consultation
